In a nutshell

It is a lecture, not a class: about twenty minutes to listen and think, with no exercises, no countdowns and no questions to take home. It is given by Isaac, a guest science communicator. It is the third in a series of seven, “Does time run the same for everyone?”, which tells the story of relativity through its history, and it stands on its own: from the earlier lectures it needs only two things, which Isaac recalls when he gets to them. From the first, three ideas that seem obvious and cannot all be true at once: the laws are the same for anyone moving without accelerating, speeds add up, and light always travels just as fast. From the second, that in 1905 Einstein let go of the ether: nobody is really at rest.

The question, which Isaac raises after the opening hook (“Today we see what happens to time, then”) and repeats with its answer in the last chapter (“Back to today’s question”): if nobody is really at rest, what happens to time? Before it, a puzzle that prepares it and stays on screen, in silence: the muons passing through your hand shouldn’t get here, and they do. If they live so briefly, how do they reach your hand? It is answered halfway through, with numbers.

The message, what listeners take away for their lives: each of us carries our own clock, and it isn’t a matter of opinion: it is measured. Nobody has the right point of view, and even so we can agree on what is the same for everyone, if we know where each person is looking from.

Objective. By the end, students will know why, if light travels just as fast for everyone, “at the same time” is not the same for everyone; why a moving clock runs slower and a passing train is shorter, with a triangle and a formula that can be read out in words; how muons from cosmic rays and atomic clocks on planes and in a laboratory confirm it; and why the twin who travels comes back younger without any contradiction.

The physics, measured; the reading, Isaac’s, and it shows. The judgements are voiced as his own: “it has something that would be unthinkable today” (a paper without references), “And that is being honest too: saying how far the evidence goes” (Mileva Marić), “Here is what hardly anyone explains well” (simultaneity), “And now, the strangest part” (the symmetry), “And now, the best part” (the muon that sees the mountain shrunk) and, halfway through, to camera, “So far, it sounds like science fiction”. The step to everyday life is also his, and weighed in its words: someone who sees things differently is “often” not wrong, “and that doesn’t mean anything goes”. Where the evidence runs out, he says so: “What they talked about at home, we don’t know”.

One formula, read out. It is the only one. First it comes out without a formula, from the 3-4-5 triangle, with Pythagoras’ theorem drawn (“nine plus sixteen is twenty-five”). Then the gamma formula (γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²)) is built on screen piece by piece, and the voice reads it out in words: “one, divided by the square root of one minus your speed squared, counted in speeds of light”. It is filled in with 0.6: 0.36, 0.64, 0.8 and five quarters, “the triangle, again”. The rest are one-line calculations: “5/4 = 1,25”, “× 15”, “× 8,8”, “1/3”. The Lorentz transformations are not written out: they are used to compute the positions and instants of the split screens of the train (200 m at 0.6 c), but they are not shown.

The route, in seven chapters:

  • Particles that should not arrive. “Open your hand”: right now some muons are passing through it, roughly one a second. They are born about fifteen kilometres up, when cosmic rays hit the air, and they come down at almost the speed of light. But a muon lives, on average, about two millionths of a second, and then breaks up: even at the speed of light, it would travel about 660 m. With the calculation without relativity, out of every thousand million not a single one would arrive, and a great many do. First pause. The map of the series and lecture 2 in one sentence: Einstein let go of the ether; today, what happens to time. “It isn’t a matter of opinion: it is measured.”
  • 1905. Einstein, at twenty-six, at the Patent Office in Bern, examining other people’s inventions. That year he sends Annalen der Physik, among others, four papers (light, atoms, relativity and energy); the relativity paper arrives on 30 June. Thirty-one pages without a single reference to other work, and only one acknowledgement, to his friend and office colleague, the engineer Michele Besso. Mileva Marić, with the 1901 letter and with what the documents say. The two postulates, with the three ideas of lecture 1: the first holds for light too, the third stays and the second falls. Running after light doesn’t slow it down. Second pause: what has to give way?
  • At the same time? Time gives way. The sentence about the train arriving at seven: every time of day is two things happening at once. Far away, clocks are set with light (Poincaré had already seen it), and Einstein writes it as a definition: light takes as long to go as to come back. The train from his 1917 book, with a girl sitting in the middle of the train, a boy on the platform and two lightning bolts striking its ends. Third pause. What hardly anyone explains well: the girl isn’t fooled by a delay of the light; she does the calculation properly, and finds that the front bolt struck first. They are both right: “each reference body has its own time”. Repeated slowly, with its name: the relativity of simultaneity. Fourth pause: whose is your “now”?
  • The light clock. Two mirrors and a flash bouncing between them, as drawn in 1909 by two American chemists, Gilbert Lewis and Richard Tolman. The girl takes one onto the train. Fifth pause. From the platform, the train’s light makes a zigzag, a longer path at the same speed: the train’s clock runs slower. At 0.6 c, the 3-4-5 triangle, and five ticks of the platform against four of the train. Time dilation and gamma, read out and with numbers. At low speed, gamma is almost one (the Space Station: about four and a half thousandths of a second in six months); close to light, it grows without limit; at light speed, the calculation breaks down. “And now, the strangest part”: each sees the other’s clock running slower. Sixth pause, and the answer: the boy needs two platform clocks, and for the girl they aren’t in step. And the passing train is shorter, from 200 to 160 m: it is FitzGerald and Lorentz’s contraction from lecture 2, now without the ether.
  • How do we know? Halfway through, Isaac to camera: “So far, it sounds like science fiction. But it isn’t a matter of opinion: it can be measured”. Around 1940, in the mountains of Colorado, fast muons lasted longer than slow ones. In 1962, David Frisch and James Smith count for an hour on the summit of Mount Washington, at about 1,900 m: 568 muons, all with almost the same speed. The same detector, another hour at sea level, in Cambridge: if their clock were like ours, about 27 would arrive. Seventh pause. 412 arrived: their clock was running almost nine times slower. “And now, the best part”: seen from the muon, the mountain is about 220 m high. Two stories and the same number in the detector: “It doesn’t say that everything is relative”. And that is why they reach your hand.
  • The twins. Paul Langevin, in 1911: a traveller shut inside a projectile comes back two years older to an Earth two hundred years older. Today, the twin paradox, told with two twin sisters, and with its objection: if it is symmetric, the traveller can say that it was the Earth that moved. Eighth pause. The one who travels comes back younger, because only she changes train: ten years against eight, at 0.6 c. It isn’t the braking: with a trip twice as long, twice the difference. Real clocks: Hafele and Keating (1971), with atomic clocks strapped into aircraft seats; going east they fall behind by about 60 ns and going west they gain about 270, and it makes sense looking at the Earth from above the North Pole, with height in the calculation. And NIST (2010), with a single aluminium atom moving back and forth at the speed of a bike. Height, gravity, is left for later.
  • To each their own clock. The answer: each of us carries our own; the three effects and whoever travels and comes back, and it is measured “on mountains, on planes and on a laboratory table”. The message, with “todo vale” (anything goes) crossed out and the number of muons as what is the same for everyone. A question for the listener and the series’ question, alone. The farewell announces the next one: E = mc².

What it doesn’t do. It doesn’t use “relativistic mass”: energy and mass are lecture 4, and the series doesn’t use that mass that grows, which physicists today avoid. The “first clue” that nothing with mass can reach the speed of light is only that the gamma formula breaks down at c. It doesn’t draw spacetime diagrams: the traveller’s view, with the jump in her “now” when she turns round, belongs to lecture 4. It doesn’t explain why height makes clocks run fast: it names it (in Hafele and Keating, on the Space Station and in NIST’s 33 cm) and leaves it for lectures 5 and 6. It doesn’t write out the Lorentz transformations. Of E = mc² it only says that the fourth paper of 1905 was “on energy”, and announces it in the farewell. It doesn’t name Rossi and Hall (“around nineteen forty, in the mountains of Colorado”). It doesn’t say who called Langevin’s story the “twin paradox”, because nobody knows. It doesn’t say that Mileva Marić was a co-author, nor that she contributed nothing. It doesn’t say that the muons were the first evidence for time dilation. And it doesn’t go into the fact that Einstein’s synchrony is a definition and the constancy of light over the round trip a measurement: it says so in passing (“Einstein writes it as a definition”) and the fine points go in “Going further”, because to the ear they would clash with the “it is measured” of the message.

Where it falls in the curriculum

The Lectures sit outside the curriculum: they neither follow the syllabus nor replace it. But this one falls within it (Spain’s Royal Decree 243/2022, annex II; the decree’s wording, in our translation of the official Spanish text):

  • Seven chapters, each with something that moves. The board is being programmed now and will be checked against the draft, so what follows is what the script asks for, not every detail: the camera rising from the girl’s hand to fifteen kilometres up and the shower from the cosmic ray; the muons, each with its own life clock, breaking up at different heights; a 1905 calendar with its four cards; a page counter running from 891 to 921 without finding a single reference; the three boxes from lecture 1; the station clock and a flash carrying its “7:00” from one station to another; the train with its two bolts, on a split screen in which each panel runs on its own time; the zigzag of the light clock; Pythagoras’ tiles; the formula assembled piece by piece and a live graph of gamma; the two platform clocks; the train that shrinks; the “little sticks” for the lives of the 568 muons, in the manner of Frisch and Smith’s film; the flattened mountain seen from the muon; each twin’s years, dot by dot; the Earth seen from above the pole, with its three arrows; and a school ruler that light crosses in one nanosecond.
  • From the present, and back to it. The lecture begins in the listener’s hand, right now, and the puzzle is closed halfway, when the evidence is in: “And that is why they reach your hand”. The muon count comes back at the end, in the message, as what is the same for everyone.
  • What hardly anyone explains well. Simultaneity is not presented as an effect of how long the light takes to arrive. The girl knows she is in the middle and that light travels just as fast for her in both directions: she does the calculation, and gets something different from the boy. The screen shows it with two panels that don’t share a clock, computed with the Lorentz transformation, and the voice repeats it slowly, because “everything that follows comes from here”.
  • The same case, all the way through. Everything is told with 0.6 c: the 3-4-5 triangle, five ticks against four, γ = 5/4, the 200 m train that measures 160, and the twins, ten years against eight. Each step can be checked against the previous one, with no new numbers.
  • Symmetric, and without contradiction. “Both see the other’s clock running slower” is not left as a mystery: to measure one tick of the train’s clock, the boy needs two platform clocks, set with light, and for the girl the one further on is already ahead. “It’s the lightning again”. Lewis and Tolman had already seen it that way in 1909.
  • Two stories, one number. The muons are told twice: from the Earth, their clock runs slow; from the muon, the mountain is lower. The detector at the bottom counts the same. It is the bridge to the message: relativity isn’t that anything goes, but knowing how to pass from one point of view to another.
  • Ten pauses, of 3.9 seconds each, without warning and without a countdown. What each one is for, and how to make use of it if the lecture is watched in class:
    1. If they live so briefly, how do they reach your hand? The opening puzzle: a simple calculation, 660 m on average against 15 km, says none should arrive, and they do. It is answered in chapter 5. In class: everyone writes down a hypothesis in one line (they are all welcome, including “they go faster than light” or “they’re born lower down”) and checks it at the end.
    2. If light travels just as fast for everyone, even if you run after it, what has to give way? The step of 1905, right after the postulates. If the speed doesn’t change, something we use to measure it has to. In class: write “speed = distance ÷ time”, as in lecture 1, and ask which part can give way. The voice answers: time.
    3. So, did the two bolts strike at the same time, or not? With the two lights reaching the boy together and the girl separately. It draws out the answer the lecture takes apart straight afterwards: that to the girl it “seems” they didn’t, but “really” they did. In class: by show of hands, “yes”, “no” or “it depends”, and another vote after hearing the answer.
    4. When you say “now”, whose “now” is it? The relativity of simultaneity, brought home to the listener. It has a physical answer (the “now” of your reference body) and another that each person takes away. At everyday speeds, two people’s “nows” can’t be told apart; with the train on screen, 200 m at 0.6 c, they differ by 400 nanoseconds. In class: it is the basis of the one-session debate below.
    5. And the boy, from the platform, what do you think he sees? A geometric prediction, with the light clock on the train. In class: have students draw the path of the light seen from the platform before seeing it. It is the ten-minute activity.
    6. How can they both be right? The hardest one: each sees the other’s clock running slower. The next block answers it, with two platform clocks. In class: with 2nd year, ask them to answer it with the lightning before hearing the answer. The hint: measuring how long one tick of the train’s clock lasts takes two platform clocks, one where it starts and another where it ends.
    7. How many do you think arrived at the bottom? A bet before the result: 568 at the top, about 27 if their clock were like ours. In class: by show of hands, “about 27”, “about a hundred” or “almost all of them”. It is the fifteen-minute activity.
    8. When they meet again, which one is younger? Right after the symmetry objection. In class: let someone argue “neither, because it’s symmetric”. The answer, that only one of them changes train, is better understood against that objection.
    9. And you, when someone sees things differently, do you ask yourself where they are looking from? The message question, for each person’s life, right after “that doesn’t mean anything goes”. There is no need to share answers.
    10. Does time run the same for everyone? The series’ question, alone and in large letters. This lecture answers it for what moves: no, each of us carries our own, and it is measured. Height, which also changes the rate, is announced for lectures 5 and 6.
  • Each quotation, with its work on screen: the two sentences of § 1 of the 1905 paper, the one about the train arriving at seven (p. 893) and the one about light taking as long to go as to come back, “by definition” (p. 894); the receipt stamp, “(Eingegangen 30. Juni 1905.)” (Received 30 June 1905), and the thanks to Besso (p. 921); “each reference body has its own time”, from the 1917 book (§ 9); and the 1901 letter to Mileva Marić. The translations are ours. Isaac reads them; the authors have no voice. Einstein’s texts are protected in Spain until the end of 2035: his pages and sentences appear as quotations (Article 32 of Spain’s Intellectual Property Act). Langevin’s work, protected in Spain until the end of 2026, is told in our own words.
  • Words, with their names: muon and cosmic rays, postulates, reference body, relativity of simultaneity, light clock, time dilation, gamma, length contraction, twin paradox and nanosecond. Each is said when it comes up and explained in the same sentence.
  • The round figures, and where they come from. “Roughly one a second”: with a palm of 60 to 100 cm² and the Particle Data Group’s rule of thumb (about one per cm² per minute), 1 to 1.7 per second; with the integrated flux, 0.7 to 1.1. “About fifteen kilometres up”: “typically 15 km”, in the 2020 and 2022 editions of the Particle Data Group (the 2024 and 2025 editions no longer say so). “About two millionths of a second”: 2.1969811 µs (2025). “About six hundred and sixty metres”: c × 2.197 µs = 658.6 m. “Out of every thousand million, not one”: 15 km at the speed of light takes 50.0 µs, 22.8 mean lifetimes, and 1.3 in ten thousand million survive. “Twenty-six”: he was born on 14 March 1879. “On 30 June”: the stamp on p. 921; it was published on 26 September. “Almost eight kilometres a second”: 7.66 km/s at 420 km (ESA gives 7.6-7.7 km/s, at 370-460 km). “About four and a half thousandths of a second”: 4.47 ms in six months, the net lag (see “Going further”). “More than double”: γ(0.9) = 2.29. “400 ns”, on the train screen: 0.6 × 200 m ÷ c = 400.3 ns. “Nineteen sixty-two”: the year of the film according to the APS (the credits of the copy say 1963, and the paper is from 1963). “At about nineteen hundred metres”: the summit is at 6,288 ft, 1,917 m, and the drop to the lower detector is 1,907 m. “Almost the same speed”: between 0.9950 and 0.9954 c. “Fifteen times more”: 412 ÷ 27 = 15.3. “Almost nine times slower”: 8.8 ± 0.8 measured, against 8.4 ± 2 calculated allowing for their slowing down in the air (γ at 0.995 c would be 10.0): 6.40 µs of descent by our clock and 0.73 µs by the muon’s. “About two hundred and twenty”: 1,907 ÷ 8.8 = 217 m (in the film, about 700 ft, 213 m). “A third”: 0.727 ÷ 2.197 = 0.33. “Almost three out of four”: 412/568 = 72.5 %, and the exponential gives 71.8 %. “Two years, two hundred”: Langevin puts the projectile at one twenty-thousandth below the speed of light, and γ = 100. “About sixty nanoseconds”: −59 ± 10 ns; “about two hundred and seventy”: +273 ± 7 ns. “A thirty-centimetre ruler”: c × 1 ns = 29.98 cm. “Under ten metres a second”: the NIST press release speaks of “several meters per second” and compares it to a car going “about 20 miles per hour”, 8.9 m/s; on screen, “< 36 km/h”. “One second every fifty-seven million years”: at 10 m/s, v²/2c² = 5.56 × 10⁻¹⁶, about 48 trillionths of a second a day. “Thirty-three centimetres”: the NIST press release turns it into about 90 nanoseconds over a 79-year lifetime.

Using it in class, if you wish

The lecture sets no homework. If teachers want to use it, here are four ways, from shortest to longest. They are suggestions: adapt them to the group; each says how to shorten it.

  • Reading aloud, three minutes. The two fragments of § 1 of 1905, the one about the train arriving at seven and the one with the definition, which are on the student sheet in English and in the original German. Two questions for afterwards. Why does Einstein say that every judgement involving time is a judgement about simultaneous events? (Because saying “it’s seven o’clock” is saying that the hand points to seven and that something else happens, at the same time.) And what does he lay down “by definition”, and what is measured? (He lays down that light takes as long from A to B as from B to A, and clocks are set by that rule; what experience says, a few lines further on, is that the round-trip speed is a universal constant.) If there is no time, the train sentence and the first question are enough.
  • With 1st year, ten minutes: the triangle, before seeing it. Stop the video at the fifth pause (“And the boy, from the platform, what do you think he sees?”). Data: two mirrors, one above the other, and a flash going up and down; the train travels at 0.6 c, three out of every five. Ask students to draw the path of the light, seen from the platform, while it rises once. If the light covers 5 along the diagonal, the mirrors move forward 3; how far has it risen? With Pythagoras, √(5² − 3²) = 4. Since the height between the mirrors is the same for both (the train doesn’t move upwards), the girl sees the light cover 4, straight up; the boy sees it cover 5, along the diagonal, at the same speed: each tick lasts 5/4. Then the formula: 1/√(1 − 0.6²) = 1/√0.64 = 1/0.8 = 1.25. Carry on watching: the voice gives the same numbers. A subtlety worth a question: the train’s clock gives 4 ticks for every 5 of the platform’s, 20 % fewer, and each tick lasts 25 % longer. Both statements are true, and that is why the lecture doesn’t say “25 % slower”. For those who want more: at 0.8 c the same triangle appears, turned round (the mirrors move forward 4 and the light rises 3), and γ = 5/3, about 1.67; at 0.9 c, 2.29, as on the graph on screen. It needs only paper; Pythagoras is from ESO, and the formula can be followed in 4th year of ESO. If there is no time, drawing the zigzag is enough.
  • With 2nd year, fifteen minutes: the muons of Mount Washington. Stop the video at the seventh pause (“How many do you think arrived at the bottom?”). Data, from Frisch and Smith’s film and paper: at the top, 568 muons in an hour, all between 0.9950 and 0.9954 c; the drop, 1,907 m; the mean lifetime, 2.2 µs. (1) How long do they take to come down, by our clock? 1,907 ÷ (0.995 × 3 × 10⁸) = 6.4 µs. (2) Without relativity, that is 6.4 ÷ 2.2 ≈ 2.9 mean lifetimes, and about 568 × e^−2.9 ≈ 31 would be left at the bottom. The film says 27, because it doesn’t use the pure exponential: it reads it off its own measured curve, with a string stretched across at the 6,300 ft mark. Both calculations say the same thing, about thirty out of 568, and it is worth saying where each one comes from. (3) 412 arrived. How much time passed for them? If e^(−t/2.2) = 412/568, then t = 2.2 × ln(568/412) ≈ 0.71 µs, and 6.4 ÷ 0.71 ≈ 9. The paper gives 8.8 ± 0.8, read off its curve, against the 8.4 ± 2 that theory requires for those muons in their apparatus: less than the γ = 10 of 0.995 c, because they slow down in the air (at the bottom they removed 30 cm of iron from the stack to compensate). (4) Seen from the muon, the mountain is 1,907 ÷ 8.8 ≈ 217 m high; it crosses it in about 0.73 µs, a third of its mean lifetime, and e^−1/3 ≈ 0.72: almost three out of four (412/568 = 0.725). This is criterion 5.1: experimental data with their error, and a comparison with what was expected that doesn’t ask for an exact match, only for falling within the margin. And a question of method: why can they compare different muons? (Because the rain doesn’t change from one hour to the next: before, they had counted five other hours, between 527 and 588, averaging 564.) If there is time for only one thing, the bet at the seventh pause and the jump from 27 to 412.
  • With 2nd year, one session: “to each their own now”. A debate with one text and two pauses. The text, § 9 of the 1917 book (“each reference body has its own time”), is on the student sheet. Watch the fourth pause again. In groups, one argues that the platform is the right one, another that the train is, and a third that both are right, and they may use only what the lecture gives them: light travels just as fast for both, and each is in the middle of their own marks. Then the underlying question: if “at the same time” depends on who is looking, does anything go? What is the same for everyone: the laws, the speed of light, the number of muons the detector counts, and whatever happens at the same place and at the same time, such as the instant the girl passes in front of the boy, which both panels on screen share. With a group that wants to go further, § 1 of 1905: Einstein lays down synchrony “by definition”, and philosophers of physics still argue about whether that rule is a convention or the only reasonable one (the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, in “To learn more”). Finish with the ninth pause, the message one. Nobody “wins”: physics says what belongs to each and what belongs to all. If the session runs short, the fourth pause and a vote are enough.

Misreadings the lecture avoids or takes apart

What is often said What the measurements and the sources say
“Simultaneity is a delay of the light: the girl sees one bolt first because its light arrives first” The girl allows for the time the light takes. She knows she is in the middle of her train and that, for her, light travels just as fast in both directions, so if one light arrives first, it set off first. She finds that the front bolt struck first, and it isn’t a mistake in her calculation. In 1917 Einstein puts it like this: what is simultaneous relative to the embankment is not simultaneous relative to the train, “and vice versa”. The voice: “It isn’t that the girl gets confused because one light takes longer to reach her”
“Everything is relative” The theory starts from what is the same for everyone: the laws and the speed of light. What belongs to each is their own time and their own measurements of length, and you pass from one set to another by a fixed rule. The detector counts the same number of muons in both stories, and what happens at the same place at the same time is so for everyone. The voice: “It doesn’t say that everything is relative”. On the name, which Einstein didn’t choose, see the sheet for lecture 1
“Acceleration is what makes the traveller age less” What breaks the tie is that only she changes reference frame: she rides one train on the way out and another on the way back, and the one who stays rides the same one throughout. The turn can last a moment, and the difference grows with the length of the trip for the same turn: 10 years against 8, or 20 against 16. There are variants in which nobody feels any acceleration, swinging round a star in free fall, which “cast doubt on how relevant the acceleration is” (Weiss, Physics FAQ). The idea goes back a long way: in 1911 Langevin wrote that it is enough to “undergo accelerations” to age less, and NIST’s 2010 press release says that “the crucial factor is the acceleration”. These are simplifications. The voice: “it isn’t the braking that takes years off her”
“You need general relativity to explain the twins” Special relativity is enough, doing the calculation from the one who stays: “To explain it, today’s lecture is enough”. In 1918 Einstein told it from the traveller’s side with a gravitational field, and that is one way of looking at it, not a requirement. The traveller’s view in special relativity needs the spacetime diagram: her “now” on Earth jumps 3.6 years when she turns round, from 3.2 to 6.8 (lecture 4)
“The clock that travels by plane falls behind, and that’s that” It depends on the direction. Going east, the clocks came back about 59 ns behind; going west, about 273 ahead. Seen from above the pole, the Earth turns eastwards: at 35° latitude the ground moves at about 381 m/s, and the plane flying west is still moving east, at about 130 m/s, more slowly than the ground. That is why the slow one is then the ground clock. And height makes both run fast. Predicted, −40 ± 23 and +275 ± 21 ns; measured, −59 ± 10 and +273 ± 7
“A muon lives 2.2 µs and breaks up after 660 m”: 660 m as a fixed deadline 2.2 µs is the mean lifetime: an average. Each muon breaks up when its turn comes, at random. At the speed of light, half would still be there after 460 m, 37 % after 660 m, 10 % after 1.5 km and 1 % after 3 km. The screen shows it with muons breaking up at different heights and a mark for “660 m de media” (660 m on average)
“It’s an illusion”, or “it’s a matter of perspective” It is not an optical effect or an appearance: detectors count it and clocks record it. Hafele and Keating’s clocks come back with a different reading, the muons arrive, and the twin comes back younger. Contraction is what someone measures by marking the nose and tail of what passes at the same time, with their own rulers and clocks; whoever is inside notices nothing
“Muons were the first evidence for time dilation” Before them, in 1938, Ives and Stilwell saw it with fast hydrogen atoms, and Rossi and Hall, with muons, in 1940-1941. That is why the voice says “one of the clearest tests”
“Frisch and Smith put one detector at the top and another at the bottom, at the same time” It was a single detector: an hour on the summit and then another hour in Cambridge, about 150 miles away, at sea level. They didn’t count the same muons, and they didn’t need to: the rain is the same from one hour to the next. The voice: “They weren’t the same muons, but that doesn’t matter”
“The 1905 paper doesn’t name Lorentz”, or “Einstein copied without citing” It names Lorentz’s theory twice (§ 9), and Maxwell, Hertz and Newton, but it refers to no article: it has not a single reference. It names neither Michelson nor Poincaré. That this would be unthinkable today is Isaac’s own opinion. What influenced him is argued over with other documents (sheet for lecture 2). The voice: “Not to Michelson, not to Poincaré, not to Lorentz’s papers”
“Mileva Marić co-authored relativity, and there is a manuscript signed ‘Einstein-Marity’” The 1901 letter, “our work on relative motion”, is genuine, but in 1901 Einstein still believed in the ether, and motion relative to the ether was a problem half the world was working on. The signed manuscript is not on record: Joffe wrote in 1955 that the author was “Einstein-Marity”, after the Swiss custom of adding the wife’s surname, and the word “signed” was added by a Russian popular-science writer, Daniil Danin, in 1962. No document survives that shows what she contributed, nor any that proves she contributed nothing (“Going further”). The voice: “What they talked about at home, we don’t know”
“Langevin told the twin paradox, with a rocket” He spoke of a traveller, with no twins and no paradox, in a projectile that the Earth would launch; he himself rules out his friend Perrin’s rocket (p. 53). The twins appear, as far as we know, in Weyl (1918); that year Einstein speaks of the “clock paradox”, and before 1955 that is what it was called. Who called it “the twins” has not been established. The voice: “Today it is known as the twin paradox”
“Lewis and Tolman invented the light clock” They drew two mirrors and light going back and forth between them (fig. 1, p. 714), and didn’t call it a clock. It is the oldest such figure we have found, not certainly the first. The voice: “That is how they drew it, to explain it”
“At 0.6 c, the clock runs 25 % slower” It gives 4 ticks for every 5 of the platform’s: 20 % fewer. What lasts 25 % longer is each tick. The two statements are easily confused, which is why the lecture uses “five quarters” and “five ticks against four”
“Mass grows with speed, and that is why nothing reaches light speed” The lecture doesn’t use “relativistic mass”, which physicists today avoid. It only leaves a clue: the gamma formula breaks down at the speed of light. Why nothing with mass reaches c is told with energy, in lecture 4
“Hafele and Keating proved nothing” The criticism exists (A. G. Kelly, 1996, a monograph of the Institution of Engineers of Ireland), but it is a minority opinion, outside the physics journals. What was measured falls within the margin of what was predicted, and much more precise experiments, such as NIST’s in 2010, confirm it

Going further: what the lecture doesn’t say aloud

In depth, for teachers. Each point has its source in “Sources”.

  • “By definition”, and what is measured. It is the finest point of p. 894. Einstein writes in italics that the common time of A and B is defined “by laying down, by definition, that the ‘time’ light needs to go from A to B is equal to the ‘time’ it needs to go from B to A”. And he turns it into a condition: the two clocks are synchronised, by definition, if t_B − t_A = t'_A − t_B. A few lines later he states, “in accordance with experience” (“der Erfahrung gemäß”), that 2·AB/(t'_A − t_A) is a universal constant, the speed of light in a vacuum. Synchrony is defined; the constancy of light over the round trip is measured. That is why the voice says “he writes it as a definition” and goes no further: to the ear it would clash with the “it is measured” of the message. In 1917 he repeats it: that light takes as long from A to the midpoint as from B to the midpoint “is in reality neither a supposition nor a hypothesis about the physical nature of light, but a convention” (§ 8). Whether that rule is a convention or the only reasonable one is still argued over by philosophers of physics; the sheet for lecture 1 sums it up (Reichenbach and Grünbaum; Malament, 1977).
  • The footnote on p. 893. Next to the train sentence, Einstein sets a problem aside: “The imprecision contained in the concept of the simultaneity of two events at (approximately) the same place, which must likewise be bridged by an abstraction, will not be discussed here”. And he goes on: putting the position of the small hand in place of “time” is enough for the place where the clock is, but it “is no longer enough” (“genügt aber nicht mehr”) for comparing events at different places. It is the voice’s “Here, next to you, it’s easy”.
  • Poincaré had seen it. In 1898, in “La mesure du temps”: “Nous n’avons pas l’intuition directe de la simultanéité” (“We have no direct intuition of simultaneity”). In 1900 he imagined clocks set with light signals (sheet for lecture 2). The voice sums it up: “That is what Poincaré had seen”. In 1917, Einstein writes that physics had assumed, “stillschweigend”, tacitly, that time is the same for everyone: it is the “something everyone took as fixed”.
  • The Patent Office. The post was “technischer Experte III. Klasse”, technical expert third class, at the Federal Office for Intellectual Property, in Bern. The Federal Council appointed him on probation on 16 June 1902, at 3,500 francs a year, and he started work on 23 June. He was promoted to second class in 1906 (the office itself gives two dates, 10 March and 1 April). He jokingly called it his “cobbler’s trade”.
  • Besso. Michele Besso was an engineer (mechanical engineering, at the Zurich Polytechnic, where he graduated in 1895) and six years older than Einstein. He lived in Milan from 1899 to October 1901, and from January 1904 they worked together at the Bern office, walking home together afterwards. Einstein considered him the best sounding board in Europe for his ideas (“I could not have found a better sounding-board in the whole of Europe”, according to Seelig, 1956). The thanks on p. 921, in full: “In conclusion, I want to say that, while I was working on the problem dealt with here, my friend and colleague M. Besso stood faithfully by my side, and that I owe him more than one valuable suggestion”. It is sometimes said that Einstein got him the job: we have not been able to verify it, and the lecture doesn’t say so.
  • The 1905 papers, with their dates. They were received on 18 March (light), 11 May (Brownian motion, atoms), 30 June (relativity) and 27 September (energy, with E = mc² written as L/V²). The doctoral thesis, on the size of molecules, is dated 30 April, reached the journal on 19 August and was published in 1906: that is why the voice says “among others”. The relativity paper is signed “Bern, Juni 1905”.
  • Mileva Marić, with the documents. She studied physics with Einstein at the Zurich Polytechnic, and he wrote to her about physics. The letter, of 27 March 1901, says: “How happy and proud I will be when the two of us together have brought our work on relative motion to a victorious conclusion!” (the voice uses a phrasing that is easier on the ear). The letters were published in the Collected Papers in 1987, and specialists concluded that they are “just too vague” to show a contribution of hers to any publication (Martínez, 2005). At the 1990 AAAS meeting, Evan Harris Walker and Senta Troemel-Ploetz argued for the collaboration; John Stachel, editor of the Collected Papers, replied that there was not “a shred of evidence known to me” that she worked on special relativity, and added: “I cannot assert categorically that she did not”. Pauline Gagnon, who argues for Mileva, writes that nobody has been able to credit her with any specific part of the work. The most complete review, by Esterson and Cassidy, with Ruth Lewin Sime (MIT Press, 2019), concludes that the idea of the secret collaborator lacks solid footing, and grants that she helped him with some drafts in 1901. In the divorce agreement, settled in 1918, the money from a future Nobel Prize would go to her. The lecture stays with what the documents show, and says so.
  • The postulates, in the words of 1905. In § 2 (p. 895), the second reads: “Every ray of light moves in the ‘stationary’ coordinate system with the definite velocity V, whether the ray is emitted by a stationary or a moving body”. It is independence from the source: “whoever emits it”, in the voice. That it travels just as fast for everyone follows from combining it with the first, and the voice says it in that order: “And since, by the first, anyone can say they are at rest, light travels just as fast for everyone”. When the girl, in chapter 3, knows that light travels just as fast for her in both directions, what she is using is the two postulates, with the clocks set in Einstein’s way; the voice, for brevity, says “the second postulate”.
  • The train in the 1917 book. In § 8, the bolts strike two points A and B on the embankment (“Bahndamm”), and Einstein adds that they struck at the same time. In § 9 an observer gets on the train, at the midpoint: “But in reality (judged from the embankment) he is hastening towards the ray of light coming from B, while he is running on ahead of the one coming from A. So he will see the light from B earlier than the light from A”. And he concludes with the sentence on screen. The lecture swaps the embankment for a platform and adds the girl and the boy, but the reasoning is his. The German we quote comes from a school reprint, and we checked the central sentence in the 5th edition (1920, p. 18) and in Lawson’s English translation (1920).
  • Lewis and Tolman, 1909. Gilbert Lewis and Richard Tolman, chemists at MIT, presented their paper on 18 May 1909. The two mirrors are in fig. 1, p. 714, and Pythagoras on p. 715. They already saw it as symmetric: “Thus to each observer it seems that the other’s clock is running too slowly” (p. 714). With light travelling along the motion they deduced, in the same way, that a moving rod is shortened. And they add that these changes are, “in a sense”, “a scientific fiction”, because the distortion of a moving body “is not a physical change in the body itself”: whoever moves with it notices nothing.
  • Einstein didn’t call it gamma. In 1905 he called that factor β (p. 902). The letter γ came later.
  • A clock that goes round, already in 1905. In § 4 (p. 904): if there are two synchronised clocks at A and one of them is moved along a closed curve at constant speed until it returns to A, on arrival it will be behind the one that stayed put by ½ t (v/V)² seconds. It is the simple calculation of the twins, for low speeds: the one from whoever doesn’t move.
  • Langevin, in detail. He presented it at the 4th International Congress of Philosophy, in Bologna, in 1911, and it was published in Scientia. His traveller shuts himself inside “un projectile que la Terre lancerait” (a projectile the Earth would launch), at almost the speed of light; after a year of his life, an encounter with a star, for example, sends him back towards the Earth, and on arrival “he will come out of his ark”, the word for Noah’s ark (p. 50). To launch it he thinks of a sling (p. 52), and he rules out Perrin’s rocket principle (p. 53). He names Jules Verne on p. 47, for something else. His reading is that it is enough to “move about” and “undergo accelerations” to age less: today it isn’t explained that way.
  • Weyl and Einstein, in 1918. The twins appear in Hermann Weyl, Raum · Zeit · Materie (1st ed., 1918, § 21, p. 148), “two twin brothers who separate”; it is a finding of our own verification, checked in the text of two editions. That year, Einstein writes a dialogue on the objections to relativity and concludes that no internal contradictions of the theory can be deduced from the “clock paradox”. Before 1955, books said “clock paradox” (Benguigui); the great controversy began in Nature in 1956.
  • The traveller’s view. It is the question a good student will ask: if she can say she is at rest, why doesn’t she get the opposite result? For her, on the way out, the Earth’s clock runs slow, and when she turns round, the time that corresponds to “now” on Earth jumps: at 0.6 c and with a 10-year trip, it goes from 3.2 to 6.8 years, 3.6 at a stroke. On the way back, the Earth runs slow again, and in the end she gets the same result as her sister. It is the relativity of simultaneity again, on changing train. Telling it properly needs the spacetime diagram, which is why it belongs to lecture 4. The lecture does the calculation from the one who doesn’t change, which is correct and simple.
  • Frisch and Smith’s film. Time Dilation: An Experiment with Mu-Mesons, about 36 minutes long, is free on the Internet Archive, but it has no open licence. It is the main source of the numbers: “So, Jim, would you start an hour’s run?”; before that they had made five other one-hour runs (554, 582, 527, 570 and 588), averaging 564; they count the 27 by stretching a string across their graph at the 6,300 ft mark; at the bottom they didn’t set up under the mountain, because they would have had to dig a hole and “the Appalachian Mountain Club wouldn’t like it”, so they went to Cambridge, 150 miles away, where the muon rain is the same; they removed 1 ft of iron from the stack, because the air of the descent slows them down by the same amount; and they close with the mountain of “about 700 feet” seen from the muon and a pun: a fast muon will “make a molehill out of a mountain”. Watch two details: the film has no single date (the APS gives 1962 and the copy carries 1963 credits), and its 568 and 412 are those of that run; the paper averages all of them.
  • Rossi and Hall. Their 1941 paper, in Physical Review, speaks of “mesotrons”. They measured a mean lifetime of (2.4 ± 0.3) µs and saw that the slower group decayed about three times sooner. It is the voice’s “around nineteen forty, in the mountains of Colorado”.
  • Ives and Stilwell, 1938. They saw time dilation in the light of fast hydrogen atoms (Journal of the Optical Society of America 28, p. 215). That is why the muons are “one of the clearest tests”, not the first.
  • Hafele and Keating, the full calculation. Eastward: −184 ± 18 ns from speed, +144 ± 14 from height, −40 ± 23 predicted and −59 ± 10 measured (the difference is 0.76 times the combined margin). Westward: +96 ± 10, +179 ± 18, +275 ± 21 predicted and +273 ± 7 measured. The split between speed and height is taken from HyperPhysics, not checked against the original table; the totals, from the Science paper. The clocks travelled in two assemblies (clock, batteries, clock), strapped into seats in the passenger cabin, “tilting back a bit”, and according to the US Navy tickets were bought for them. The screen draws a single clock in its seat, as a summary.
  • The Space Station, net. At 7.66 km/s and 420 km up, speed slows the onboard clock by 28.2 µs a day and height speeds it up by 3.72: a net 24.5 µs a day, 4.47 ms in six months. From speed alone it would be 5.1-5.2 ms. The voice’s figure is the net one, the correct one for “younger than if they had stayed at home”, even though it comes up in the chapter about speed.
  • NIST, 2010. Chou, Hume, Rosenband and Wineland, in Science. Each clock is based on the ticking of a single aluminium ion, an electrically charged atom, says the press release; the voice says “atom”. One clock’s ion moved back and forth at “several meters per second”, and that was enough to see that its clock ran slower. Raising one by 33 cm also changed its rate. The same press release, speaking of the twins, says that the crucial factor is acceleration: not something to repeat (see the misreadings).
  • The clue that lecture 4 has to honour. “It is the first clue that nothing that has mass can ever go as fast as light” leaves a promise: lecture 4 explains it with energy, without the mass that grows.

Each work, with an English edition of reference (checked in two sources on 25/11/2026) and, where there is one, a free and legal copy. Lewis and Tolman and Frisch and Smith wrote in English, and Langevin’s and Weyl’s originals are free: they are in “Open resources”.

About this lecture

Isaac is an illustrated character of the channel, with a synthetic voice, and presents himself as what he is, someone who tells people about physics, with no invented university or CV: the authority lies in the sources. The illustrations (the girl and the boy, the train, the twins, the spaceship, the atomic clock in its seat) were made with AI for this lecture; the portraits, the photo of Mount Washington, the Earth and the original pages are real, free images, with their origin on screen and in “Sources”. Einstein’s pages and sentences, protected in Spain, appear as quotations. The script is written by an AI (Claude, by Anthropic) and reviewed by a person. Every fact and every quotation has been checked in the original source or in two sources, and every calculation has been run.
