The wind that does not blow

In a nutshell

It is a lecture, not a class: about twenty minutes to listen and think, with no exercises, no countdowns and no questions to take home. It is given by Isaac, a guest science communicator. It is the second in a series of seven, “Does time run the same for everyone?”, which tells the story of relativity through its history, and it stands on its own: from the first lecture it needs only one sentence, which Isaac recalls at the start (three ideas that seem obvious cannot all be true at once).

The question, alone on screen and in silence, after two objects that prepare it (noise-cancelling headphones and Boyle’s watch in a jar with no air): if light is a wave, what is it that waves? It is answered in the last chapter.

The message, what listeners take away for their lives: having the formulas is not understanding them: sometimes, to move forward, you have to let go of something everyone takes for granted. And a “no” from nature is not a failure: it is an answer, and sometimes the best one.

Objective. By the end, students will know why almost all the physicists of 1880 believed in the ether (if light is a wave, something has to wave); how Michelson and Morley looked in 1887, with a race of light, for the ether wind that ought to be blowing on the Earth, and didn’t find it; how FitzGerald, Lorentz and Poincaré saved the ether with formulas that still hold today; and why the missing step, Einstein’s in 1905, was not a formula, but letting go of the idea that someone is really at rest.

The physics, measured; the reading, Isaac’s, and it shows. His assessments are given as his own: “But look at what he had in his hands. It wasn’t a failed experiment”, “what I like most about this story” (Lorentz’s note) and “what this story teaches me most” (the message). Where the history isn’t settled, he says so: whether Michelson influenced Einstein, “historians still argue about it”, with a pair of scales on screen that doesn’t tip either way.

No formulas. On screen there are one-line sums, “30 ÷ 300 000 = 1/10 000”, “1/100 000 000”, and two sums in words, “onda + onda = silencio” (wave + wave = silence) and “luz + luz = oscuridad” (light + light = darkness). The Lorentz transformations go in one line in words: “distancias y horas: de quien está quieto → a quien se mueve” (distances and times: from whoever is at rest → to whoever is moving). The boats are told with their numbers (50, 62.5 and 40 seconds). How much what moves shrinks, and how each person’s time can be true, is lecture 3.

The route, in nine chapters:

  • What waves? Noise-cancelling headphones: a microphone listens to the drone of the plane and the headphone makes the opposite wave; the two add up and cancel out. That is interference, and “we’re going to need it today”. Robert Boyle, in 1660, pumps the air out of a jar with a watch inside: the ticking fades, and the watch can still be seen going. Sound doesn’t cross a vacuum, and sunlight crosses 150 million kilometres of it. The question, on its own. The map of the series and lecture 1 in one sentence: around 1880, almost everyone had their way out, and it was hidden in that question.
  • The ether. Thomas Young, in 1803, puts into a sunbeam a strip of card thinner than a millimetre: fringes appear in the shadow, and they disappear if he blocks one of the sides. Light plus light, darkness: only waves do that. If it is a wave, something waves, and they called it the ether. An impossible material: a wave runs faster the stiffer and lighter the thing that vibrates, light travels almost 900,000 times faster than sound, and Young wrote that it would have to be “absolutely solid”. Yet the planets pass through it without slowing down. And one thing more: it would be at rest, and it would be the right point of view. The Earth moves through it at about 30 kilometres per second: an ether wind ought to be blowing on it, like the air in the face of someone cycling on a calm day. The effect, on a round trip, is one part in 100 million. Maxwell wrote in 1879 that it was “quite too small to be observed”, and a young United States Navy officer, Albert Michelson, thought the opposite.
  • Two boats on a river. The same idea with water: two identical boats, each out to a buoy 100 metres away and back; one up and down the river, the other from bank to bank. The one that crosses gets back first: after 50 seconds, and the one along the river, after 62.5. With no current, together, after 40. The difference measures the current.
  • 1887. The interferometer is that race, with light: a half-silvered mirror splits the beam in two, one goes along the wind and the other across it, and when they meet they make fringes. A fringe shifts with a delay of about 2 millionths of a billionth of a second. In Cleveland, Michelson and the chemist Edward Morley mount the mirrors on a stone slab 1.5 metres across that floats in mercury and turns once every 6 minutes, with 11 metres of outward path in each arm. They expected four tenths of a fringe. They saw less than one twentieth of that, and probably less than one fortieth: if there was a wind, it was blowing at less than 5 kilometres per second, not 30. Four days in July; they promised to repeat it every three months and didn’t. “It answered no.”
  • Saving the ether. Halfway through, Isaac on camera: “A very clear no. And now comes the hard part: what do you do with it?”. FitzGerald (1889) and Lorentz (1892), separately: everything that moves through the ether shrinks a little in the direction of motion, by just the right amount. The whole Earth, by about 6 centimetres, and the ruler that would measure it, too. Lorentz goes further with his local time, “an aid to the calculations”; Poincaré sees that it is the time of clocks set with light while the wind is blowing, writes out in full in 1905 the formulas he christens “transformation de Lorentz”, names the “postulate of relativity”, and back in 1889 had written that the ether would end up discarded “as useless”. They had almost everything, and they stuck with the ether and with one true time.
  • Letting go of the ether. Einstein, in Bern, in 1905: on the second page of his paper, the ether will prove “superfluous”. Without it, the three ideas of lecture 1 fall into place: the first holds for light too, the third stays and the second goes. And each person’s time is as true as anyone else’s. Did he do it because of Michelson and Morley? His paper doesn’t name them; in 1954 he wrote that the experiment had not had a considerable influence on him, and in 1921, in Chicago, he had said that as a student he already knew of Michelson’s experiments. It is disputed. What isn’t disputed: FitzGerald and Lorentz proposed their shrinking to explain that no.
  • Where they fell short. Lorentz, with the Nobel Prize already won, writes in 1915 that the chief cause of his failure was his clinging to universal time as the only true one. Michelson, Nobel Prize in 1907, would answer that he measured light “because it is such good fun”, and in 1927, at seventy-four, spoke of “the beloved old ether”, to which he still clung a little. The same word, in English, in both.
  • How do we know today? Ultra-cold sapphire crystals and glass blocks that turn for a year: no wind, to one part in a billion billion, more than 100 million times finer than in 1887. And the apparatus’s grandchildren: the two LIGO detectors, with arms 4 kilometres long, which in 2015 detected a tremor of spacetime (lecture 7).
  • Listening to a no. The answer: nothing lying underneath waves; what waves is the electric and magnetic field itself, the relay of lecture 1, which carries itself along. There is nothing at rest for a wind to blow against. The shrinking comes back next time, and shrinks by the same amount, but without the ether. The message, a question for each listener, and the series’ question, on its own.

What it doesn’t do. It doesn’t write out the Lorentz transformations or the contraction formula, nor explain simultaneity or why each clock shows a true time (lecture 3). LIGO appears in passing: it is lecture 7. It doesn’t say that the result was zero, nor that it proved there is no ether, nor that it inspired Einstein. It doesn’t go into the “ether” Einstein spoke of in 1920, which is something else (it is in “Going further”). And it doesn’t say that Michelson felt disappointed, because that isn’t documented.

Where it falls in the curriculum

The Lectures sit outside the curriculum: they neither follow the syllabus nor replace it. But this one falls within it (Spain’s Royal Decree 243/2022, annex II; the decree’s wording, in our translation of the official Spanish text):

Subject and block What the decree says What the lecture adds
Physics, 2nd year, block D, “Relativistic, quantum, nuclear and particle physics” “Fundamental principles of special relativity and their consequences: length contraction, time dilation, relativistic energy and mass.” Where contraction and local time come from: from an experiment that answered “no” and from the attempt to save the ether. Why the principle of relativity, which Poincaré already called a “postulate”, has to hold for light too. It doesn’t calculate any consequence: it states that the shrinking comes back “without the ether, and with a different meaning”
Physics, 2nd year, block C, “Vibrations and waves” “Nature of light: historical controversies and debates. Light as an electromagnetic wave. Electromagnetic spectrum.” The ether controversy, from Young (1803) to Einstein (1905), with its documents. And today’s answer to “what waves?”: the electromagnetic field, which needs no medium
Physics, 2nd year, block C “Wave phenomena: natural situations and contexts in which different wave phenomena become apparent, and applications. Sound waves and their qualities. […]” Interference with an object of today, noise-cancelling headphones, and then with Young’s fringes and those of the interferometer. Sound needs a medium (Boyle’s watch) and light doesn’t
Physics, 2nd year, block B, “Electromagnetic field” “Electric and magnetic field lines produced by simple charge distributions, magnets and current-carrying wires in different geometric configurations.” The field, told for those who haven’t studied it (“that invisible something around a magnet or a wire carrying a current”), with iron filings on screen: it is what waves
Physics and Chemistry, 1st year, block D, “Kinematics” “Relationship between the trajectory of a compound motion and the magnitudes that describe it.” The composition of motions in the boat race: the one that crosses has to aim upstream (about 37°) to go straight across, and that is why it takes 50 s and not 40. It is the kinematics the interferometer rests on

Competences and criteria for 2nd-year Physics. The lecture serves above all specific competence 6 (“To recognise and analyse the multidisciplinary nature of physics, considering its significant historical path and its contributions to the advancement of scientific knowledge as a process in continuous evolution and innovation […]”) and its criterion 6.1 (“To identify the main scientific advances related to physics that have contributed to the formulation of the laws and theories accepted today […]”): few stories teach so well that science also advances by letting go of ideas. The 1887 result, a bound and not a zero, provides material for criterion 5.1 (“To obtain relationships between physical variables, measuring and processing experimental data, determining errors and using graphical representation systems.”). The misreadings below serve criterion 3.1 (“[…] critical analysis of physical processes in the environment, such as those observed and those published in different media […]”), and the headphones and LIGO, criterion 2.3 (“To know practical applications and products useful to society in the technological, industrial and biomedical fields […]”).

  • Nine chapters, each with something that moves: the two curves of the noise and the anti-noise flattening into a line; the specks of air leaving Boyle’s jar while the watch’s hand keeps going; the strip of card in the sunbeam, with its fringes that go out when one side is blocked; three bars, of air, of steel and of ether, with their pulse; the series’ boy on his bike, with the air in his face; the boat race, sped up, with its stopwatches; the river that turns into the interferometer; the fringes that slide; the slab turning in its mercury; the curve of what was expected over the almost flat line of what was measured; the arm that shrinks and the ruler that shrinks with it; the flash that goes back and forth between two clocks; the haze of the ether dissolving; the precision staircase, with today’s glass block turning, seen from above; and the magnet with its filings, which gives way to the relay of lecture 1.
  • The object, before the apparatus. Interference comes in with headphones everyone has seen, not with Young; and the interferometer, with a boat race. When the apparatus arrives, it is already understood, and the voice says so: it “is that race, with light”. The race isn’t decoration: with 5 m/s and a current of 3 (β = 0.6), the river boat takes 1/(1 − β²) = 1.5625 times what it would take with no current, and the one that crosses, 1/√(1 − β²) = 1.25 times. These are the formulas for the interferometer’s two arms.
  • The question, at the beginning and at the end. It is heard alone before the first history chapter, and comes back, with its answer, in the last: “Back to the question from the beginning”. In between, each chapter is an attempt to answer it.
  • A “no” said twice. Nature “answered no”, in the 1887 chapter, and the “no” stays on screen, huge. In the last chapter it comes back with what it lacked: “it is an answer”. The farewell repeats it: “A no is an answer too”.
  • The scales that don’t tip. Michelson’s influence on Einstein comes with two opposing quotations, 1954 and 1921, and a pair of scales that stays still. The lecture doesn’t choose: it separates what is disputed from what isn’t (that the shrinking was born to explain the 1887 result).
  • Clinging. The two confessions, Lorentz’s and Michelson’s, share the verb in English, clinging and cling, and the screen marks them at the same time. It is the bridge to the message: the hard part wasn’t the formula, but letting go.
  • Ten pauses, of 3.9 seconds each, without warning and without a countdown. What each one is for, and how to make use of it if the lecture is watched in class:
    1. If light is a wave, what is it that waves? The lecture’s question, prepared by two objects: a wave needs something that waves, and light crosses a vacuum. In class: everyone answers it in one line and keeps it for the end.
    2. Right now, you’re going at that speed. Do you feel any wind? It draws out the naive answer (“no, so there’s no ether”), which the lecture qualifies straight away: the ether would pass through matter, and what would notice it is light. In class: ask what apparatus could detect it.
    3. Which one gets back first? A kinematics prediction. Many people say “at the same time” or “the river one”. In class: it is the ten-minute activity below.
    4. What do you think they saw? A bet before the result, with the curve of what was expected on screen. In class: by show of hands, “what was expected”, “less” or “nothing”.
    5. And you, after years of preparing it, would you count that as a failure? To put yourself in the shoes of someone who gets a negative result. Michelson had been at it since 1881. Isaac answers afterwards, as his own opinion. In class: there is no need to share it.
    6. A wind that no apparatus could ever detect: would you say it exists? The lecture’s philosophical question, right after the ruler shrinks along with what it measures. It prepares Einstein’s “superfluous”. In class: a short debate on what makes a hypothesis testable (Popper and Grünbaum, in “Going further”). It doesn’t have a single answer.
    7. They had almost everything. What do you think they were missing? With four pieces lit up (contraction, local time, the transformations, the postulate) and one empty. The answer isn’t a formula, and that is the message. In class: everyone writes one word for the empty piece before hearing “they still had to let go of something”.
    8. And you, when you find out you were wrong, do you say so? After Lorentz’s note. For each person’s life; the silence is enough. If another example is wanted, Michelson corrected in writing the error in his 1881 apparatus (“Going further”).
    9. What do you take for granted that you’d find hard to let go of, if one day the evidence told you no? The message’s question. There is no need to share this one either.
    10. Does time run the same for everyone? The series’ question, alone and large. This lecture leaves it ready: “each person’s time is as true as anyone else’s”.
  • Each quotation, with its work on screen: Young’s “absolutely solid!!!”, with his supplement to the Britannica; Maxwell’s “quite too small to be observed”, with his 1879 letter; Poincaré’s “transformation de Lorentz”, “Postulat de Relativité” and “un jour viendra sans doute où l’éther sera rejeté comme inutile” (a day will doubtless come when the ether will be discarded as useless), with his three publications; Einstein’s “überflüssig” (superfluous), and p. 891 of his paper with the “unsuccessful attempts”; the 1954 letter and the 1921 talk; Lorentz’s note 72*, and Michelson’s “the beloved old ether”. The translations are ours. Isaac reads them; the authors have no voice. Einstein’s paper is protected in Spain until the end of 2035: its pages appear as a quotation (Article 32 of Spain’s Intellectual Property Act).
  • Words, with their names: interference, ether, ether wind, interferometer, local time and universal time, Lorentz transformations, postulate of relativity and field. Each is said when it comes up and explained in the same sentence.
  • Round figures, and where they come from. “Just over 8 minutes”: 499 s, 8 min 19 s. “More than 15 times”: about 5,900 m/s in steel against 343 in air, 17 times. “Almost 900,000 times”: 874,030. “About 30 kilometres per second”: 29.78. “One ten-thousandth”: β = 9.93 × 10⁻⁵. “One part in 100 million”: β² = 9.87 × 10⁻⁹. “12.5 seconds later”: 62.5 − 50. “About 2 millionths of a billionth of a second”: the period of yellow light, 1.97 × 10⁻¹⁵ s, which is to one second what one second is to 16.1 million years. “About four tenths”: 0.37 with sodium light (589.3 nm) and 0.39 with 550 nm; they write 0.4. “Less than 5 kilometres per second”: √(1/40) × 29.78 = 4.7 (they write “probably less than one sixth”). “Almost 13,000 kilometres”: 12,742. “About 6 centimetres”: 6.29. “Seventy-four”: his age on 4 February 1927. “One part in a billion billion”: (9.2 ± 10.7) × 10⁻¹⁹, at 95%. “More than 100 million times”: between 1.25 × 10⁸ and 2.5 × 10⁸, depending on which 1887 bound is taken. And “4 km” against “11 m”: 364 times.

Using it in class, if you wish

The lecture sets no homework. If teachers want to use it, here are four ways, from shortest to longest. They are suggestions: adapt them to the group; each says how to shorten it.

  • Reading aloud, three minutes. Lorentz’s 1915 note, which is on the student sheet in its original English and in Spanish. Two questions for afterwards: what was Lorentz clinging to? (To the idea that only one time, the ether’s, is the true one.) And why does he call “failure” a theory whose formulas still hold? If there isn’t time, the central sentence is enough, the one about the “chief cause”.
  • With 1st year, ten minutes: the boat race, before hearing the answer. Stop the video at the third pause. Data: boats at 5 m/s relative to the water, a current of 3 m/s, a buoy 100 m away. Upstream they go at 2 m/s and take 50 s; downstream, at 8 m/s, 12.5 s: 62.5 s in all. The one that crosses has to aim upstream so that the current doesn’t sweep it away; its speed relative to the bank is √(5² − 3²) = 4 m/s, and it takes 2 × 100 ÷ 4 = 50 s. The angle, with sin θ = 3/5, is 36.9°. With no current, both take 40 s. Then carry on watching: the voice gives the same numbers. For those who want more: 62.5/40 = 1/(1 − 0.6²) and 50/40 = 1/√(1 − 0.6²); replacing 0.6 with the Earth’s speed divided by that of light, these are the interferometer’s two arms. It only needs paper. If there isn’t time, a prediction by show of hands will do.
  • With 2nd year, fifteen minutes: the numbers of 1887. A calculation in four lines, with the paper’s data. With an arm of D = 11 m, β = 29.78 ÷ 299,792 ≈ 10⁻⁴ and yellow light of 589.3 nm, the expected shift when the slab turns is N = 2Dβ²/λ = 0.37 fringes (they round D to 2 × 10⁷ wavelengths and write 0.4). They measured with certainty less than 1/20 of that, and probably less than 1/40. Since N grows with the square of the speed, the wind’s speed is less than √(1/40) × 29.78 = 4.7 km/s; with 1/20, less than 6.7 km/s, and they write “certainly less than one-fourth”. That is criterion 5.1: a measurement that doesn’t give zero, but a bound with its error. And a question about the graph: in figure 6 of the paper, the curve of what was expected is drawn at one eighth of its height (the caption says so). How would what was measured look if it were drawn in full? If there is only time for one thing, the step from 1/40 to 4.7 km/s.
  • With 2nd year, one session: did Michelson influence Einstein? Three documents, all quoted in “Going further”: p. 891 of the 1905 paper, which speaks of “unsuccessful attempts” without names; the notes taken by someone in the audience at the Chicago talk, in 1921; and the 1954 letter, almost half a century later. In groups, each one defends a reading using only what the documents say, and notes what weight it gives each one and why (a listener’s notes, a memory from 49 years later, a paper that cites nobody). At the end, what isn’t disputed: FitzGerald and Lorentz proposed the shrinking to explain the 1887 result. Nobody “wins”: historians still don’t agree. If the session runs short, reading the two quotations and voting will do.

Misreadings the lecture avoids or takes apart

What is often said What the measurements and the sources say
“The result was zero” It was a bound: a shift certainly smaller than 1/20 of what was expected, and probably smaller than 1/40. They translated it into a speed, “probably less than one sixth” of the Earth’s. In 1928, Dayton Miller insisted that it had never been numerically zero. The voice says “they saw almost nothing”
“They repeated it six months later, in another season” They made a single series, in July 1887: six sessions of six turns, about three and a half hours of turning. They promised to repeat it every three months and didn’t. Others repeated it: Morley and Miller (published in 1905: at most a hundredth of what was expected), Miller at Mount Wilson, and Michelson, Pease and Pearson (1926-1928)
“The mercury was there so that the apparatus wouldn’t turn with the Earth” It was there to turn it. The slab floated, on a wooden ring, in mercury, and with a push it made one turn every 6 minutes, without jolts, while the observer walked round with it. That way the two arms swapped roles
“They used a laser” It was an Argand lamp, with white light. Sodium light was used only to find the fringes when adjusting the apparatus
“In 1887 it became clear that the ether doesn’t exist” They concluded that, if there was any motion relative to the ether, “it must be small”, small enough to refute Fresnel’s explanation, which assumed an ether at rest. In 1903, Michelson still considered it “practically certain” that there was a medium for light. The voice tells what happened next: the first thing was to try to save the ether
“Einstein proved there is no ether” He wrote that introducing it “will prove superfluous”: that his theory has no need of it. That is something else. In 1920 he went as far as saying that, according to general relativity, space has physical qualities, and that “in this sense, therefore, an ether exists”, although the idea of motion cannot be applied to it
“The experiment inspired Einstein”, or “Einstein didn’t know about it” Both are too categorical. The 1905 paper doesn’t name Michelson or Morley. In 1954 he wrote that the result had not had “a considerable influence” on him and that he didn’t even remember if he knew of it. In 1921, according to the notes taken by someone in the audience, he had said that as a student he knew of those experiments. And from 1887 to 1905 there are eighteen years, not eight
“Contraction was a patch made up ‘by hand’, with no basis” It came with a physical reason: the forces that hold matter together might change with motion, as electric forces change. Lorentz admitted in 1892 that it couldn’t be tested, and in 1915 that he found the argument about the forces after having the hypothesis. Popper gave it as an example of a hypothesis with no testable consequences, and in 1959 added a note: “This is a mistake”
“Contraction is an illusion” It isn’t an optical effect. In relativity, it is what someone measures who sees the object go past with their rulers and clocks, and whoever travels with the object notices nothing. The lecture leaves it said like that (“what goes past you, as seen by you”) and tells it in the third
“FitzGerald proposed it in 1892”, or contraction as Lorentz’s alone FitzGerald, in 1889, in Science; Lorentz, in 1892, on his own. In 1895, Lorentz wrote that, as he learnt later, FitzGerald had arrived at the same thing; in print, he had only seen it mentioned by Oliver Lodge
“Poincaré spoke of a conspiracy of nature to hide the ether” The word isn’t in his texts. He spoke of “ce singulier concours”, a coincidence too convenient to be true. “Conspiracy” is Eddington’s (1920), and he used it to say there isn’t one: “there is nothing to conceal”
“The ether was stiffer than steel” With Maxwell’s numbers, steel would be about a billion times stiffer. What was impossible was the stiffness for how extremely little it weighed: per gram, about 9 billion times that of steel. That is why the voice says “extremely stiff for how extremely little it weighed”
“Maxwell said it was impossible to measure the wind” He wrote that, with methods on Earth, the effect was “quite too small to be observed”. Michelson took it as a challenge
“Young did the double slit in 1801” In 1803 he used a strip of card 1/30 of an inch thick (0.85 mm); he read his paper on 24 November and published it in 1804. He describes the two slits in 1807
“The greatest failed experiment in history” The lecture turns it round: they measured with enormous care and nature answered. That this isn’t a failure is said by Isaac as his own opinion, and the facts back it up: that “no” is the one FitzGerald and Lorentz set out to explain

Going further: what the lecture doesn’t say aloud

In depth, for teachers. Each point has its source in “Sources”.

  • The apparatus, in its numbers. The slab was of sandstone, 1.5 m on each side and 0.3 m thick. It rested on a ring-shaped wooden float, which floated in mercury inside a cast-iron trough with sixteen marks, where the fringes were read; the mercury was Morley’s idea. There were four mirrors at each corner, and the light, from an Argand lamp, travelled about 11 m out along each arm. They set it up in the basement of the Adelbert student residence, at Western Reserve University, because in the autumn of 1886 a fire had destroyed the main building of the Case School, Michelson’s school. In July 1887, Michelson was 34 and Morley, 49. In figure 6 of the paper, the curve of what was expected is drawn at one eighth of its height; that is why the lecture redraws it to scale and doesn’t copy it.
  • The first apparatus had an error, and Michelson corrected it in writing. In 1880-1881 he worked in Helmholtz’s laboratory, in Berlin, on leave from the Navy; the apparatus was paid for by Alexander Graham Bell. The city’s vibrations forced him to move it down to the basement of the Potsdam observatory, where footsteps about 100 metres away were enough to wipe out the fringes. He signed the 1881 paper as “Master, U. S. Navy”. He had forgotten that the wind also lengthens the path of the crosswise arm: Alfred Potier pointed it out to him, and Lorentz analysed it in 1886. In 1882 he wrote to the Paris Academy of Sciences: “j’ai oublié l’effet du mouvement sur le rayon bc. La correction m’a été signalée par M. Potier” (“I forgot the effect of the motion on the ray bc; the correction was pointed out to me by M. Potier”). The expected effect was half as large, and the 1881 one fell right at the limit of the errors: that is why the 1887 path was made ten times longer. It is a good example for the eighth pause.
  • The three-month promise, and why it wasn’t kept. The reason, in the paper: combined with the motion of the solar system, “concerning which but little is known with certainty”, the Earth’s speed might have come out small just in July by chance; by repeating it every three months, “all uncertainty will be avoided”. Moyer (1987) gives four reasons why they didn’t: they were moving laboratories, classes were starting, the apparatus went on to measure the metre with light (in fact, it had first been used for that, in June), and in the supplement to the paper itself they already saw it as “hopeless” to settle the motion of the solar system that way. The voice gives the first three. In 1933, Miller put it in writing: “Michelson and Morley made only the one series of observations, in July, 1887”. Miller thought he saw at Mount Wilson a wind of about 10 km/s; for 4,400 turns he walked, in the dark, a total of one hundred miles (about 160 km) around the apparatus. In 1955, Shankland and others attributed his signals to chance in the readings and to the temperature of the site. And the 1887 paper already proposed trying it on top of a mountain, with a glass cover.
  • Michelson, in his own words. A month later he wrote to Rayleigh that the result was “decidedly negative”. In 1903, that the problem, by leading to the invention of the interferometer, “more than compensated” for the experiment giving a negative result, and on the same page, that it appeared “practically certain” that there must be a medium for light. The 1907 Nobel Prize, the first in the sciences for an American (Theodore Roosevelt had received the Peace Prize in 1906), was “for his optical precision instruments and the spectroscopic and metrological investigations carried out with their aid”: it doesn’t mention the ether. “Because it is such good fun” was his answer in 1924 to a member of the U.S. Coast and Geodetic Survey, and in 1926 he told how a colleague had asked him during a game of billiards; it wasn’t Einstein who asked him. In January 1931, at a dinner in Pasadena, Einstein told him that he had begun that work when Einstein was “only a little youngster, hardly three feet high”, and that without his experiments relativity would today be “scarcely more than an interesting speculation” (in the English translation published in Science). That evening, Michelson replied that in making the experiment there had been no conception of its tremendous consequences.
  • The boats aren’t Michelson’s. In 1903, Michelson explained his idea with a man rowing on a river compared with one on a pond: twelve miles at four miles an hour, six hours in still water and 6.4 with a current. The full race, with one going up and down and another crossing, is in Eddington, Space, Time and Gravitation (1920), with two swimmers, and in Russell (1925), with a boat. The lecture doesn’t attribute the image: it uses it because its arithmetic is exactly that of the apparatus.
  • Contraction, neither trick nor illusion. FitzGerald wrote that it “seems a not improbable supposition” that molecular forces are affected by motion, and that the size of a body therefore alters; he only said that the change went with the square of the ratio of the velocities. The amount, to first order, was given by Lorentz in 1892, who added: “Since we know nothing about the nature of molecular forces, it is impossible to verify the hypothesis” (in the English translation on Wikisource). In a draft letter to Einstein, of January 1915, he admitted that he had made that observation “only after I had found the hypothesis” (the editors’ English translation). Popper (§ 20 of The Logic of Scientific Discovery) gave it as an example of an unsatisfactory auxiliary hypothesis; in 1959, following Grünbaum, he added: “This is a mistake”. Grünbaum showed that it was testable: with unequal arms, contraction alone no longer hides the wind, and that is what Kennedy and Thorndike measured in 1932. Brown (2001) and Janssen (2002) argue that it wasn’t as ad hoc as is often said; that contraction can also be read “from the inside”, from the forces of matter, is defended by Bell (1976) and Brown. What changes with Einstein is who sees it. Poincaré called it “une hypothèse surprenante” (a surprising hypothesis) and, in La Science et l’hypothèse, poked a little fun at the method: “on en trouve toujours ; les hypothèses, c’est le fonds qui manque le moins” (“one can always be found; hypotheses are the one stock that never runs short”). FitzGerald said that people had laughed at him, and in 1894 he didn’t even know whether his note had been published.
  • Local time and Poincaré’s clocks, to first order. In 1900, Poincaré imagines observers who set their clocks with light signals and, since they don’t know they are moving, assume that light takes as long to go as to come back: “Le temps local t′ est le temps marqué par les montres ainsi réglées” (the local time t′ is the time shown by the clocks set in this way). He does it neglecting v² (“Cette compensation n’a lieu qu’en négligeant v²”: this compensation only occurs if v² is neglected): the offset of those clocks is local time to first order in v/c. At 30 km/s and one metre, the round-trip offset, vL/(c² − v²), and that of local time, vL/c², differ by one part in 100 million. Notice the word Poincaré uses for the other time: “temps vrai”, true time. On 5 June 1905, in the Comptes rendus, he writes out the transformations in full, “which I shall call by the name of Lorentz”, and shows that they form a group; Einstein’s paper reached the journal on 30 June. Lorentz later acknowledged that Poincaré “found the formulae (4) and (7)” that he himself had lacked, and that “postulate of relativity” were “terms he was the first to use”. A nuance for the classroom: Poincaré had already said “principe de relativité” in 1904, in St. Louis; and Einstein, on p. 891 in 1905, already speaks of the “Prinzip der Relativität”, weeks before the Palermo paper.
  • Did Poincaré take the step? It is disputed. The voice follows the common reading, “neither of them took the step”, and supports it with facts that can be checked (Poincaré keeps the ether and a “temps vrai”). The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy takes it as undisputed that Poincaré discovered central points of special relativity: the synchronisation of clocks to first order, the relativistic composition of velocities and the structure of the Lorentz group. Some go further: Edmund Whittaker and Gerald Holton were the two sides of a controversy over what each one contributed (it is collected in Pearce Williams’s anthology, in “Reading them”), and J.-M. Ginoux (2024) defends Poincaré’s priority, although the encyclopedia itself faults him for not engaging with the historians who think otherwise. And in 1889 Poincaré had written, with indifference: “Peu nous importe que l’éther existe réellement ; c’est l’affaire des métaphysiciens” (“It matters little to us whether the ether really exists; that is the metaphysicians’ business”), just before the sentence about the “useless” ether.
  • Did Michelson influence Einstein? The exact words. 1905, p. 891: “Examples of a similar kind, together with the unsuccessful attempts to detect a motion of the Earth relative to the ‘light medium’, lead to the conjecture that no properties of the phenomena correspond to the concept of absolute rest”. 1921, Chicago, according to the notes in German taken by someone in the audience: “But when I was studying, I saw that experiments of this kind had already been done, in particular by your countryman, Michelson”. 1954, to F. G. Davenport: “In my own development Michelson’s result has not had a considerable influence. I even do not remember if I knew of it at all when I wrote my first paper on the subject (1905).” Van Dongen (2009) studies it together with the Chicago talk. For the classroom, the underlying question is a different one: what is worth more, what someone says thirty or fifty years later, or what they left in writing at the time.
  • What is measured today is the round trip. When the voice says that “light travels just as fast in every direction”, what is measured is the round trip in two perpendicular directions: the speed in one direction alone depends on how the clocks are set, which is exactly Poincaré’s matter (and that of the lecture 1 sheet). The staircase on screen compares all the experiments with the same quantity, Δc/c; in the classical ether, Δc/c = β²/2. Thus 1887 gives less than 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁰; Joos (Jena, 1930), less than 1.3 × 10⁻¹¹; Brillet and Hall (Boulder, 1979), about 4 × 10⁻¹⁵; Herrmann (Berlin) and Eisele (Düsseldorf), in 2009, about 10⁻¹⁷; Nagel and others (Berlin, 2015), (9.2 ± 10.7) × 10⁻¹⁹. Herrmann’s was a block of fused silica 55 × 55 × 35 mm that turned every 45 s, more than 130,000 turns in thirteen months; Nagel’s, two sapphire crystals at 4 K, with 12.97 GHz microwaves, which turned every 100 s for a year, using about 200 litres of liquid helium every three weeks, and their worst enemy was the Earth’s magnetic field. Nagel’s own table gives a billion times better than 1887, because it inflates the old interferometers; with the consistent conversion, about 100 million (Schiller, in 2009, said the same of his).
  • And today’s ether? On 27 October 1920, in his inaugural lecture at Leiden (the title page says 5 May, the planned date), Einstein said: “according to the general theory of relativity, space is endowed with physical qualities; in this sense, therefore, an ether exists. […] But this ether must not be thought of as endowed with the quality characteristic of ponderable media, that of consisting of parts that can be followed through time: the concept of motion must not be applied to it”. And, half in jest, that immobility was “the only mechanical property” Lorentz had left the ether. It doesn’t contradict the lecture: there is nothing at rest for a wind to blow against. If a student brings up that “the ether is back”, this is the quotation to talk it over with.
  • The ether, extremely stiff for what it weighed. With Maxwell’s numbers (1878), the ether’s rigidity would be about 843 in CGS units and that of steel, about 8 × 10¹¹: a billion times more. But its density would be 9.36 × 10⁻¹⁹ g/cm³, and per gram it comes out about 9 billion times stiffer than steel. Maxwell summed it up with humour: “Aethers were invented for the planets to swim in”. Young’s “absolutely solid!!!” is from 1823, in his addition to Arago’s article on polarisation: a complaint against Fresnel’s hypothesis, in which light vibrates sideways, a “lateral” resistance that until then had been attributed only to solids.
  • The two objects at the start, told properly. Headphones cancel above all low, steady noise: the zone of silence measures roughly one tenth of the wavelength (34 cm at 100 Hz, 3.4 cm at 1,000 Hz), and high-pitched sounds are stopped by the cushion. The idea was patented by Paul Lueg, who applied for it in Germany in January 1933; Bose took it into pilots’ headsets (prototypes in 1986, the first on sale in 1989). Boyle didn’t just stop hearing the watch: he writes that the second hand could be seen moving, so it hadn’t stopped, and when the air was let back in, the ticking returned. With a bell he noticed nothing clear: that is why it is a watch, and not the bell of so many books.
