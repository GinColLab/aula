Riding a beam of light

In a nutshell

It is a lecture, not a class: about eighteen minutes to listen and think, with no exercises, no countdowns and no questions to take home. It is given by Isaac, a guest science communicator, from the school’s assembly hall, with a station clock on the stage. It is the first in a series of seven, “Does time run the same for everyone?”, which tells the story of relativity through its history, and it stands on its own.

The question, asked in full at the start, before the first idea: what would you see if you ran alongside a beam of light, as fast as it goes? Albert Einstein asked it of himself at sixteen, as he himself recalled many years later.

The message, what listeners take away for their lives: dare to ask the obvious: when two things you take for granted don’t fit, don’t look the other way, because that is where it gets interesting. What is obvious is, often, just what nobody has stopped to check.

Objective. By the end, students will know why that question had no easy answer: because three ideas that seem obvious (the laws are the same for everyone, speeds add up, and light has a speed written into the laws) cannot all be true at once. They will know which one Einstein let go of in 1905, how we know he was right, and why that forces time not to run the same for everyone.

The physics, measured; the reading, Isaac’s, and he says so. At the start he warns: “The physics you’re going to hear has been measured and tested, and I’ll show you how as we go. What I take from it is mine: argue with me about it.” His own is the view that “relativity” is a misleading name (labelled on screen “lo que pienso yo”, what I think), and his own is the final message.

No formulas. On screen there are only additions and subtractions of speeds (“300 + 5 = 305”) and one sum written in words, “velocidad = distancia ÷ tiempo” (speed = distance ÷ time). The lecture sets out the problem and says what has to give. How much it gives, and how that is worked out, is lecture 3.

The route, in seven chapters:

  • It is in your pocket. The blue dot on the phone’s map. The GPS satellites carry atomic clocks and spend all day repeating what time they have; with four of them, the phone knows where it is. Left to themselves, those clocks would run 38 millionths of a second fast every day, and in that time light covers more than eleven kilometres: the system takes it into account. When it was being set up, some people didn’t believe it. Then, the map of the series, today’s question and the warning about what is measured physics and what is Isaac’s reading.
  • Galileo’s ship. First idea. “How fast are you going?”: it depends on the frame of reference, and you don’t feel it. The stone that would land far from the tower, the argument of those who believed the Earth stood still, and Galileo’s answer in 1632: the cabin below the deck of a ship, with butterflies, fish and a bucket dripping into a narrow-mouthed jar. With uniform motion, everything stays the same: “from none of them will you be able to tell whether the ship is moving or standing still”. Today’s plane, and the Earth, which is “almost” a ship like that. The principle of relativity, by name. Second idea: on a train at 300 km/h, someone walking at 5 goes by at 305. Speeds add up.
  • Light, by surprise. Third idea. Around 1860, Maxwell gathers what was known about electricity and magnetism, and out of his laws comes a wave that travels on its own. He calculates its speed from measurements of charges and currents, without using light: about 310,000 km/s. Fizeau had measured the speed of light, with light: about 315,000. Light is an electromagnetic wave, like radio, wifi or X-rays. And the detail on which everything depends: in the laws, that number “just comes out on its own”, without saying relative to what.
  • The question at sixteen. Aarau (Switzerland), autumn 1895. The question, told as what it is: a memory of Einstein’s. A surfer sees the wave carrying her as still; with the train rule, someone running alongside light would see it the same way, frozen. Nobody has seen it like that, and Maxwell’s laws don’t allow it. With a lantern on Galileo’s ship there would be a “speedometer made of light”. Three ideas that cannot all be true at once, and three ways out: the ether, which was almost everyone’s; that light inherits the speed of its torch; and Einstein’s in 1905, letting go of adding speeds. Nobody rides a beam of light.
  • How do we know? At CERN, in 1964, particles at 99.975% of the speed of light gave off a flash forwards. With the train sum, it would travel at almost double. They measured the usual speed, with a margin of just over one ten-thousandth. It is “half the proof”: light does not inherit the speed of whatever sends it out. The other half is left for lecture 2. And with trains, the sum still works.
  • What has to give. Adding speeds takes for granted the same metre and the same second for everyone. Both fail, and time is the hardest to believe. The 1977 test satellite, with the first caesium clock put into orbit, went up with the adjustment switched off; after about twenty days, it was running fast by what relativity predicted, with a difference of under 1%. There are two effects in that gain: 7 millionths of a second a day lost to speed and 45 gained from height. And Isaac’s reading of the theory’s name.
  • Asking the obvious. At sixteen he didn’t have the answer, nor had he yet studied Maxwell’s laws: “it was just curiosity”. What he did differently was not to look the other way. Asking isn’t being contrary: it’s going to check, and accepting whatever comes out. A question for each listener, and the series’ question, on its own.

What it doesn’t do. It doesn’t tell the Michelson-Morley experiment (lecture 2), nor simultaneity, time dilation or the twins (lecture 3). It doesn’t explain why height makes clocks run fast (lectures 5 and 6): it only splits it, −7 and +45, so as not to mislead. And it doesn’t say that GPS “would be eleven kilometres off”.

Where it falls in the curriculum

The Lectures sit outside the curriculum: they neither follow the syllabus nor replace it. But this one falls within it (Spain’s Royal Decree 243/2022, annex II; the decree’s wording, in our translation of the official Spanish text):

Subject and block What the decree says What the lecture adds
Physics, 2nd year, block D, “Relativistic, quantum, nuclear and particle physics” “Fundamental principles of special relativity and their consequences: length contraction, time dilation, relativistic energy and mass.” Where the principles come from: the principle of relativity, from Galileo’s ship, and that of light, from Maxwell’s laws. Why they clash with adding speeds, and which experiment settles it. It doesn’t calculate any consequence: it states that the metre and the second give way
Physics, 2nd year, block C, “Vibrations and waves” “Nature of light: historical controversies and debates. Light as an electromagnetic wave. Electromagnetic spectrum.” How we came to know that light is an electromagnetic wave: Maxwell’s number, obtained from charges and currents, next to Fizeau’s, measured with light. The spectrum, from radio to X-rays, with the GPS signal in its place
Physics, 2nd year, block D “[…] Particle accelerators.” An accelerator used to test relativity: CERN’s “torch”, in 1964
Physics and Chemistry, 1st year, block D, “Kinematics” “Kinematic variables as a function of time in the different motions an object can have, with or without external forces […]” and “Relationship between the trajectory of a compound motion and the magnitudes that describe it.” The frame of reference (“how fast are you going?” depends on where it’s measured from) and the train’s addition of speeds.   • Seven chapters, each with something that moves: the GPS orbit to scale and the four arcs that cross at the blue dot; the three speedometers that appear as the view pulls back; the seventeenth-century ship in cross-section, with its butterflies, its fish and a drop in slow motion; the relay of electricity and magnetism, which leaves Maxwell’s table as a wave; the wave seen from the beach and from the board; the wave that freezes beside the running boy; the three boxes and the crack that opens between them; the two races of the CERN flash; and the switch on the 1977 satellite, with its two arrows, −7 and +45.
  • The question, at the start. It is heard in full before minute two, and the three ideas take up the next six. Without the question in front of them, those would be six minutes of ship, plane, train and Maxwell without knowing what for. That is why Isaac asks it in full while showing the map, and warns: “to see that it isn’t [silly], you need three ideas, and all three will sound obvious to you”. What follows is heard as a road, and “how fast are you going?” is no longer mistaken for the boy’s question.
  • Three ideas, each in its box. They come in numbered, one at a time, and each is fully believed before moving on to the next: they are obvious, and that is the message. The third closes with a question nobody answers in passing: “For whom does that number hold?”.
  • The clash, with an object. The contradiction isn’t stated: it is seen. First, the train subtraction applied to light (“300,000 minus 300,000, zero”) gives a wave standing still. Then, a lantern lit on Galileo’s ship: if its light travelled more slowly forwards than backwards, there would be an experiment to know that the ship is moving, and the principle would hold “for the drops, and not for light”.
  • The trilemma, with a pause. The three boxes come back together and crack: “all three cannot be true at once”. Before giving the answer, Isaac asks “Which one do you trust least?” and falls silent. Then he names the three ways out, because all three existed: whoever chooses the first chooses like almost all the physicists of 1900 (the ether); whoever chooses the third, like the theories in which light inherits the speed of its source; and the second is Einstein’s. Nobody who has chosen is left without an answer.
  • Box 2 doesn’t fall: it cracks. On screen a word is added, “las velocidades casi se suman” (speeds almost add up), and later the voice drives it home: with trains, 300 plus 5 is still 305. So nobody leaves believing that the sum “no longer works”.
  • CERN, as “half the proof”. The question was how we know that light travels the same for everyone, and the 1964 experiment moves the source, not whoever measures. What it proves is that light doesn’t inherit the speed of whatever sends it out: it closes the third way out, and with it the train sum applied to light. Saying it like this is exact, leaves the other half (someone running to meet the light) for lecture 2, and anticipates an attentive student’s objection: sound doesn’t inherit the ambulance’s speed either.
  • The 1977 clock opens and closes. The hook leaves a debt (“some people didn’t believe it. At the end I’ll tell you how they settled it”) and chapter 6 pays it. There Isaac separates the two effects, “so as not to mislead you”: today’s, speed, makes the clock lose time; what makes it gain is height, which comes later in the series.
  • Eight pauses, of 3.5 to 3.9 seconds, without warning and without a countdown. What each one is for, and how to make use of it if the lecture is watched in class:
    1. Would you have said that time passes the same for everyone? It puts the obvious on the table before arguing about it. Isaac answers for himself: “I would have.” In class: a show of hands, and again at the end.
    2. Right now, as you listen to me, how fast are you going? It draws out the naive answer (“not at all”) to give the frame of reference its name. In class: everyone writes down a number, and relative to what.
    3. 300,000 kilometres per second. For whom does that number hold? It is the question that separates Maxwell from Einstein. In class: collect the answers without correcting them (“relative to the ground”, “relative to the torch”); each one is one of the ways out to come.
    4. You’re running alongside a beam of light, as fast as it goes. What do you see beside you? It puts the listener in the boy’s place, before the wave. In class: draw it in ten seconds.
    5. One of the three has to go. Which one do you trust least? The central one. It is the activity proposed below.
    6. What do you think they measured? A prediction before the result. In class: bets by show of hands, “almost 600,000”, “300,000” or “something else”.
    7. What do you take as obvious, without ever having checked it? For each person’s life. In class: there is no need to share it; it is enough to leave the silence.
    8. Does time run the same for everyone? The series’ question, alone and large. It isn’t answered: the six remaining lectures answer it.
  • Each quotation, with its work on screen: Galileo’s sentence, with his book and its day; “no es afortunado” (it is not fortunate), with Einstein’s 1921 letter; and “No, era sólo curiosidad” (No, it was just curiosity), with Wertheimer’s book and its page. The translations are ours. Isaac reads them; the authors have no voice. The beam-of-light question is in reported speech, because Einstein told it in other words, which are given below.
  • Words, with their names: frame of reference, uniform motion, principle of relativity, electromagnetic wave, thought experiment and ether. Each is said when it comes up and explained in the same sentence.
  • Round figures, and where they come from. “More than 1,200”: 1,209 km/h at the northernmost point of Spain. “More than 100,000”: 107,208 km/h. “38”: 38.6 millionths of a second, which at the speed of light makes 11.6 km. “About 310,000” and “about 315,000”: 310,740 and 315,324 km/s. “Less than a thousandth short”: CERN’s particles were 0.00025 short of the speed of light. “Just over one ten-thousandth”: ±0.013%. “Under 1 per cent”: between 0.4 and 0.9%, depending on the source. And −7 and +45 are −7.2 and +45.7.

Using it in class, if you wish

The lecture sets no homework. If teachers want to use it, here are four ways, from shortest to longest. They are suggestions: adapt them to the group; each says how to shorten it.

  • Reading aloud, three minutes. The passage about Galileo’s ship, which is on the student sheet in English and in Italian. Two questions for afterwards: why does Galileo ask you to shut yourself in “below deck”? (He says so himself: because there the air also goes along with the ship.) And why does he insist that the motion be “uniform”, without rocking? If there isn’t time, the final sentence is enough.
  • With 1st or 2nd year, ten minutes: the trilemma, before hearing the answer. Stop the video at the fifth pause, with the three boxes on screen. In groups of three or four, each group chooses which idea it would let go of, writes in one line why, and says what would have to be measured to prove it right or wrong. Then carry on watching: the lecture answers all three choices, and the CERN experiment is exactly what had to be measured for the third. Nobody “fails”: the first was the way out of almost all the physicists of 1900, and the third, one that Einstein himself considered seriously. It needs no materials. If the group doesn’t lend itself to working like this, a show of hands will do; and whoever doesn’t want to say their choice can write it down for themselves.
  • With 1st year, fifteen minutes: how fast is your school going? The second pause, with your own numbers. With the school’s latitude, which any map gives, the speed at which the ground turns is 1,674 km/h times the cosine of the latitude: 1,275 in Madrid, 1,256 in Barcelona, 1,330 in Seville, 1,477 in Las Palmas de Gran Canaria. The 1,674 km/h are those of the equator, and come from two NASA figures: a radius of 6,378.137 km and one turn every 23.9345 hours. Then, the lecture’s three answers for the same student: 0 km/h from their chair, that figure for someone who doesn’t turn with the Earth, and about 107,000 km/h from the Sun. And the question that matters: why don’t we notice? Because the curve is very wide: the acceleration of the spin is at most 0.034 m/s² at the equator, three thousandths of gravity. No calculators needed: reading the four figures is enough.

  • With 2nd year, one session: the speed of light, with the numbers of those who measured it. Three calculations with published data, each in two lines. They aren’t homework from the lecture: they are a way of checking what has been heard.

    1. Fizeau, 1849. A beam leaves through the gap between two teeth of a spinning wheel, travels to a mirror 8,633 m away and comes back. The wheel had 720 teeth, and the beam was first blocked at 12.6 turns per second: in the time the light went and came back (17,266 m), the wheel moved from a gap to the next tooth. That is 1 ÷ (2 × 720 × 12.6) = 55 millionths of a second, which gives 313,000 km/s. Fizeau published 70,948 leagues per second, about 315,000 km/s (a league “of 25 to the degree” is 4.444 km): it was the mean of twenty-eight observations, and ours comes from the round figures in his note. Both are about 5% above today’s value.
    2. CERN, 1964. The two detector positions were 31.450 m apart, and the accelerator’s radio frequency was 9.53220 MHz. Multiplied, they give 299,788 km/s. That is no coincidence: they chose the distance so that light would take as long to cover it as the gap between two bunches of protons, about 105 billionths of a second. If speeds added up, the flash would have taken half that. They measured (2.9977 ± 0.0004) × 10¹⁰ cm/s.
    3. GPS and the train. The official offset of the GPS clocks is 4.4647 × 10⁻¹⁰. Times the 86,400 seconds in a day, that is 38.6 millionths of a second; times the speed of light, 11.6 km. And with Einstein’s addition, (u + v) ÷ (1 + uv/c²), 300 km/h plus 5 km/h gives 305 minus one part in about 10¹⁵: that is why you can’t tell on a train.

    If there is only time for one, Fizeau’s: it can be understood without having studied relativity. All three calculations have been run, and their input data are in “Sources”.

Misreadings the lecture avoids or takes apart

What is often said What the measurements and the sources say
“Everything is relative” The theory starts from what is the same for everyone: the laws and the speed of light. What belongs to each of us is our own time. Einstein didn’t choose the name: “Relativtheorie” is Planck’s (1906) and “Relativitätstheorie”, it seems, Bucherer’s; he adopted it in 1907. In 1921 he wrote that it “is not fortunate and has given rise to philosophical misunderstandings”. That the name is misleading is said by Isaac as his own opinion
“Einstein would have preferred to call it the ‘theory of invariants’” Not quite. “Theory of invariance” was proposed to him in 1921 by a glass engineer, Eberhard Zschimmer. Einstein replied that it might perhaps be better, but that it named the method and not the content of the theory, and that changing a name already accepted would cause confusion. That is why the voice only says that he admitted it wasn’t fortunate
“Without relativity, GPS would be 11 km off every day” What is true: left to themselves, the satellites’ clocks would run 38.6 millionths of a second fast every day, and in that time light covers 11.6 km. That is where the phrase comes from: the physicist Neil Ashby wrote in 2002 that, uncorrected, there would be “navigational errors of more than 11 km” in one day. But it is a back-of-the-envelope figure, not a simulation of an uncorrected GPS. The receiver doesn’t trust its own clock: it works it out with the fourth satellite, and an offset that is the same in every satellite would be absorbed by that calculation. Nobody has published how far the position would really drift. The lecture states only the two certain things, and that the system “has to take it into account”
“GPS clocks run fast because the satellites move very fast” The other way round. Speed makes them lose 7 millionths of a second a day; height makes them gain 45. This lecture’s part, special relativity, is the −7. The +45 belongs to general relativity. The 1977 clock tested the sum of the two effects, not one
“Einstein failed maths” False, and it doesn’t come up. In his final Aarau marks (September 1896) he has a 6, the highest mark, in Algebra and in Geometry, and his average in the written school-leaving examinations was 5⅓ out of 6, the best in his class. What did happen: a year earlier, aged sixteen, two years younger than the rule required, he failed the entrance examination for the Zurich Polytechnic; according to the editors of his writings, apparently because of the general part, not the scientific one. That is why he ended up in Aarau
“At sixteen, Einstein already saw that Maxwell’s equations forbade a light standing still” He said so in 1946, half a century later. He didn’t study Maxwell’s laws until university, around 1898. When a friend asked him whether he already suspected then that light travels the same for everyone, he answered: “No, it was just curiosity.” The lecture does the reasoning on its own account (“put yourself in his place”) and at the end says that the boy had not yet studied them
“Adding speeds no longer works” It works for everything that moves slowly. With a train at 300 km/h and a pace of 5, it is off by one part in about 10¹⁵, and the closer to the speed of light, the more it fails. That is why the box says “almost add up”
“The 1964 experiment proves that light travels the same for someone running towards it” No. At CERN it was the source that was running, not whoever was measuring. It proves that light doesn’t inherit the speed of whatever sends it out: the paper itself says so, and the lecture calls it “half the proof”. The other half is Michelson and Morley’s, in lecture 2
“Nobody has measured the one-way speed of light” There is some truth in it, and it doesn’t touch what the lecture claims. To time light from A to B you need two clocks, one in each place, already synchronised, and synchronising them requires a rule. Einstein fixed it in 1905 “by definition”: light takes as long from A to B as from B to A. Whether that rule is a convention or the only possible one is an open debate in the philosophy of physics (Reichenbach and Grünbaum on one side; Malament’s theorem, of 1977, on the other). But theories in which light travels differently in each direction, and the same on the round trip, are “experimentally indistinguishable” from relativity: they give the same result in every experiment. And the lecture doesn’t rest on a single direction: Fizeau measured the trip there and back, and the voice says so; and CERN didn’t compare one direction with another, but the light of a running source with what the addition of speeds required
“Galileo did the ship experiment”, “Galileo wrote the formula for adding speeds” Neither. The ship is a thought experiment: he thought it through, and says he is sure of the result “without experiment”. And the formula we now call the “Galilean transformation” received that name in 1908

Going further: what the lecture doesn’t say aloud

In depth, for teachers. Each point has its source in “Sources”.

  • Three memories, not a fact. The beam-of-light question is known only from what Einstein told later, three times. To the psychologist Max Wertheimer, in conversations that began in 1916 and which Wertheimer published in 1945: “What if one were to run after a ray of light? What if one were riding on the beam?” (hence the title). In his Autobiographical Notes, written in 1946 and published in 1949: “If I pursue a beam of light with the velocity c (velocity of light in a vacuum), I should observe such a beam of light as a spatially oscillatory electromagnetic field at rest. However, there seems to be no such thing, whether on the basis of experience or according to Maxwell’s equations” (Schilpp’s translation, 1949). And in a sketch from 1955: “During this year in Aarau the following question came to me […]. This was the first child-like thought experiment related to the special theory of relativity” (Norton’s translation). All three agree on the age or on Aarau. Only the 1946 one names Maxwell’s equations, which Einstein didn’t study until around 1898. Darrigol (1996) calls the anecdote “either false or misdated”, and J. D. Norton thinks it likely that the draft of Wertheimer’s chapter, which Einstein received in 1943, influenced how he told it in 1946. That is why the voice says “many years later he recalled”.
  • With the ether, the question created no problem. This is Norton’s objection. A physicist of 1900 would have answered without effort: we don’t see frozen light because we don’t travel at that speed through the ether; and, for someone who did, the equations themselves give a wave standing still (“Maxwell’s equations predict that light becomes frozen for observers moving at c through the ether”). The question only bites if the laws hold the same for whoever is running: that is, if the principle of relativity includes light. The lecture doesn’t skip that step. It says it (“for you, the one running, Maxwell’s laws would have stopped holding”), turns it into the lantern on the ship, and gives the ether as the first way out. A note for the classroom: “frozen” light is not a standing wave. In a standing wave the nodes don’t travel, but the field still changes with time at each point; the wave in the question would have fields “whose strengths varied with position but were constant in time” (OpenStax).
  • “No, it was just curiosity”, in full. “When I asked him whether, during this period, he had already had some idea of the constancy of light velocity, independent of the movement of the reference system, Einstein answered decidedly: ‘No, it was just curiosity. That the velocity of light could differ depending upon the movement of the observer was somehow characterized by doubt. Later developments increased that doubt.’” In the book, the answer doesn’t come right after the beam question: it is a page later (p. 170 of the 1945 edition). Wertheimer also says that he turned it over for seven years; the Notes say “after ten years of reflection”, and that is what the voice says.
  • Why he was in Aarau. In October 1895 he sat the entrance examination for the Zurich Polytechnic and failed; he himself later wrote that he found it “wholly justified”. He was advised to finish secondary school at the cantonal school in Aarau, and joined at the end of October, with the school year already under way: there the year ended in April. Of the school he wrote that it left him “an unforgettable impression through its liberal spirit and through the simple seriousness of teachers who relied on no outward authority”. That it followed the ideas of the educator Pestalozzi is said by historians; Einstein doesn’t name him. Aarau then had fewer than eight thousand inhabitants. The photo shown is from the autumn of 1896: he was already seventeen.
  • Galileo didn’t board the ship. The cabin is another thought experiment. Of the stone dropped from the mast, Salviati, who speaks for Galileo, says: “I, without experiment, am sure that the effect will be as I tell you, because it is necessary that it be so.” And of the cabin, Sagredo admits that while sailing it never occurred to him to make those observations on purpose, but that he is “more than sure” they would turn out that way. His condition, “provided the motion is uniform and not swaying this way and that”, is the seed of the inertial frame. But his principle was not quite ours: he seems to have regarded a uniform rotation, like the Earth’s, as indistinguishable from rest too. That is why the voice says “today we say it like this: in a straight line and always at the same speed”.
  • Two names that aren’t Galileo’s. “Principle of relativity” was coined by Henri Poincaré in 1904, almost in the lecture’s words: the laws of physical phenomena “must be the same for a fixed observer as for an observer carried along in a uniform motion of translation”. Lorentz acknowledged in 1914 that Poincaré was the first to use the term. The idea is Galileo’s, and that is why it bears his name; he was speaking of mechanical experiments, and extending it to all laws, those of light included, is the step of 1904 and 1905. The “Galilean transformation”, the formula for adding speeds, was named in 1908 by the physicist Philipp Frank, in Vienna, “in memory of Galileo’s law of inertia”, to compare it with Lorentz’s.
  • The Earth, “almost”. OpenStax says the same: “Earth is nearly an inertial frame of reference.” The delicate experiment is Foucault’s pendulum, from 1851: until then there had been no clear dynamical proof of the rotation, and the slightest push was enough to ruin it. On a large scale, the spin can be noticed without instruments: it is what makes storms swirl. And the GPS receiver has to correct for how much the Earth turns while the signal travels (the Sagnac effect).
  • Maxwell, with his numbers. The electrical measurement was Wilhelm Weber and Rudolf Kohlrausch’s (1856). They charged a Leyden jar; part of the charge they measured by the force with which two balls repel each other, and the rest, discharging it through a coil, by how much that current deflected a magnetised needle. The ratio of the two measurements has units of speed: 310,740 km/s. Maxwell sums it up in 1865: “The only use made of light in the experiment was to see the instruments.” The Fizeau figure he records, 314,858 km/s, comes from 70,843 leagues; Fizeau had printed 70,948, which is 315,324. “A few laws” were, in 1865, twenty equations. For him the wave travelled through a medium, not through empty space: in 1862 he writes that light consists in undulations “of the same medium which is the cause of electric and magnetic phenomena”. Nobody had seen it: Hertz produced radio waves at the end of the 1880s. And the “relay” is an image, the usual one in textbooks too: in the wave, the two fields don’t take turns, but rise and fall together. What the image gets right is what the lecture needs: without change there is no wave.
  • The third way out, double stars and why CERN. That light inherits the speed of its source is what emission theories say. Einstein “gave long and serious consideration” to them before 1905 (Norton). In 1913, the astronomer Willem de Sitter tested them with double stars: if the light of the star coming towards us travelled faster than that of the one moving away, their orbits wouldn’t appear to follow Kepler’s laws, and they do. His argument was later questioned because of extinction: the gas along the way absorbs the light and re-emits it, and the new light no longer “remembers” the star’s speed. The CERN experiment sidesteps it: its gamma rays, above 6 GeV, would have needed about 5 km of air to “forget” their source, and they crossed about 60 m. Its result, if light travelled at c + kv: k = (−3 ± 13) × 10⁻⁵. The clean astronomical version came in 1977, with double stars that emit X-rays: k less than 2 × 10⁻⁹. A caveat for anyone reading the paper: the particles’ speed, 0.99975 c, wasn’t measured; it is the one that corresponds to their energy “according to special relativity”.
  • The metre, which isn’t in the voice. Since 1983 the speed of light hasn’t been measured: it has been fixed. That year, the General Conference on Weights and Measures defined the metre as “the length of the path travelled by light in vacuum during a time interval of 1/299 792 458 of a second”, keeping the value it had recommended since 1975, so that the metre wouldn’t change. The measurement that made it possible was from 1972: 299,792,456.2 ± 1.1 m/s. That wording was abrogated in 2018. Since 20 May 2019, the metre “is defined by taking the fixed numerical value of the speed of light in vacuum c to be 299 792 458 when expressed in the unit m s⁻¹”: the number is the same. It isn’t in the lecture because it proves nothing: it takes for granted what is being proved, and an attentive student would say that, if the metre is defined with light, “of course it always comes out the same”. If it comes up in class, it makes a good discussion.
  • GPS, told properly. Four satellites are needed because the phone’s clock can’t be trusted: three give the place and the fourth, the time. The adjustment is made on the ground, before launch: the clock is tuned to 10.22999999543 MHz instead of 10.23. It isn’t applied to rubidium clocks: their rate is measured once in orbit, and the correction goes in the message the satellite broadcasts. The 1977 satellite was NTS-2, launched on 23 June with two caesium clocks, the first caesium clocks in orbit (the first atomic clock in orbit, a rubidium one, flew in 1974). It went up with a switch for the correction: according to David Kaiser (2026), some of the project’s engineers didn’t trust the physicists’ prediction. It measured +442.5 parts in 10¹² against about 445 or 446.5 predicted, depending on the source. And a caution: GPS isn’t an experiment designed to test relativity, but a system that works because it takes relativity into account.
  • Why does the satellite’s clock lose time, and not the one on the ground? It is the question a good student may ask: if nobody has the right point of view, who decides which one is slow? The two figures, −7 and +45, are calculated in a frame of reference centred on the Earth that doesn’t turn with it. In that frame both clocks move, but the satellite’s much faster: 3.87 km/s, against less than half a kilometre per second for the ground. And neither travels in a straight line: they go round and meet again, so it isn’t the symmetrical case of two of Galileo’s ships passing each other. It is the matter of the twins, in lecture 3. For the +45, what counts isn’t how strong gravity is up there, but the gravitational potential: how much higher the clock sits in the field (lectures 5 and 6).
  • What the sum took for granted is three things. The voice names two: the same metre and the same second. The third is that “at the same time” means the same for everyone. It was the key in 1905: two frames moving relative to each other can only agree on the speed of light if they disagree on simultaneity. The screen leaves it noted, with a “¿a la vez?” (at the same time?) between the two clocks, and it is the core of lecture 3.
  • The 1921 letter, in full. “Now to the name ‘theory of relativity’. I admit that it is not fortunate and has given rise to philosophical misunderstandings. The name ‘theory of invariance’ would describe the theory’s method of research, but unfortunately not its material content (constancy of the speed of light, essential identity of inertia and gravity). Even so, the name you propose might perhaps be better; but I think that changing the name now accepted by everyone would cause confusion.” In the same letter he says something useful for the classroom: replacing “reference systems” with “observers” has given rise to “regrettable misunderstandings”. The lecture speaks of “who measures” because that is easy to understand, and gives the frame of reference its name.
