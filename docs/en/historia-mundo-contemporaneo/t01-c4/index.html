1789: did everything change for everyone?

Watch the class on YouTube (in Spanish, with English subtitles)Download the sheet (PDF)Student worksheet (PDF)Discuss it with other teachers in the Ágora (in Spanish)Versión en español

In a nutshell

Two more tools of the craft: seeing what changes and what stays the same, and deciding which events matter. The case is the date the textbooks start with: 1789. In August, at Versailles, an assembly writes "Men are born and remain free and equal in rights"; French women will wait 155 years for a law to give them the vote. Did everything change, and for everyone?

The class puts the date to the test with documents. Everything? The August decree "entirely destroys the feudal regime", but in the same article it declares the other dues "redeemable", and says they "will continue to be collected"; the law of May 1790 sets the price (twenty or twenty-five times what was paid each year); many peasants stop paying, and the law does not abolish the dues without compensation until July 1793. The announcement, the law and the people: each at its own pace. At the top, regimes change again and again; at the bottom, the France that lives in the countryside hardly moves (Braudel, from class 3). For everyone? The Declaration says "all citizens", but Sieyès separates active and passive citizens; the vote, counted out of every hundred people, goes from 16 (1791) to 0.3 (1817) and to 28 (1848), and women do not have it until 1944; slavery, abolished in 1794, returns in Guadeloupe in 1802. With that, the typical mistake falls, to change is to improve, with Condorcet's text in front of us and the fact separated from the judgement. Why 1789? Four questions for arguing significance (Seixas and Morton, building on Partington), what did not last (decimal time, the calendar) and what did (the metre; the sentence of 1789, in 1948 and in 1978), the ages as a convention (1789 here, 1945 in English) and significance that changes with our questions. The answer: not everything, not for everyone, and not all at once; but it changed so much, for so many people and for so long, that many historians start a new age there.

Objective. By the end, students will be able to recognise what changes and what stays the same in a historical process, and at what pace; tell change from progress; and argue with criteria why an event is significant, and why the textbooks open the Contemporary Age with 1789.

A note on the answer. The class does not decide whether 1789 "deserves" to open the Contemporary Age: it gives the criteria for arguing it and shows that it is historians who draw the line. Exercise 3 and the open question leave the judgement to the students.

For readers outside Spain: the Bachillerato is the last two years of secondary school (ages 16–18). Contemporary World History is an elective in the first year of the Humanities and Social Sciences track. Spanish (and French) textbooks divide history into ages and usually start the Contemporary Age (Edad Contemporánea) in 1789; in English, "contemporary history" usually starts in 1945, and the class makes a point of it. The video is in Spanish, with English subtitles; the images on this sheet come from the video and keep their Spanish text. The translations of French and Spanish documents (the decrees, the cahiers de doléances, Sieyès, Condorcet, the curriculum, the newspapers) are our own and literal; the Declaration of 1789 is quoted in the official English translation of the Conseil constitutionnel, and English texts are quoted in the original.

Typical mistakes and how they are handled

Mistake What the research says How the class handles it
"To change is to improve": history always moves forward "There is some evidence from research that students tend to think of the direction of change as automatically involving progress"; that idea can lead to "a condescending attitude toward the past", which is seen only in terms of deficits (Lee, 2005, pp. 44–45). Seixas and Morton: progress and decline are "broad evaluations of change over time", and "progress for one people may be decline for another" (2013, p. 10) Condorcet, with his text, set against the vote (from 16 in every 100 to 3 in every 1,000) and slavery returning in Guadeloupe; the arrow of progress bends. And the fact, separated from the judgement: "step backwards" is a judgement with a criterion
"One event changed everything at a stroke" ("feudalism was abolished on 4 August") "If students see changes as events", the idea of "gradual, unintended changes" "is not available to them", and change is seen as "episodic" and "intentional" (Lee, 2005, p. 44). The most-watched videos repeat the sentence: Crash Course #29 (3:10–3:25), and The Rest Is History has it in an episode title Article 1 of the decree, read in full: "entirely destroys" and, in the same article, "redeemable" and "will continue to be collected". Questions and solutions

Stop and think (in the video)

  • Before you trust a source, you ask it four questions. What are they? (0:51) Who made it, when and where, what for and for whom (class 1). Today they come back with the Declaration, and the fourth one with a twist.
  • The bet: a peasant, in 1790: does he stop paying his lord? (4:54) By law, option 2. In August 1789 the dues tied to mortmain and personal servitude fell without compensation, and in March 1790 the banalités (the compulsory mill and oven) and personal labour services. But what was paid for the land (the cens, the champart, the dues on sales) was "redeemable" and went on being collected until 17 July 1793. In practice, many stopped paying earlier: "dès le premier moment de la révolution, le paysan était décidé à ne plus payer" ("from the very first moment of the revolution, the peasant was determined to pay no more"; Lefebvre, 1929, p. 140). Option 1 is what many did, not what the law said. Option 3 has no general evidence, although anyone who redeemed paid all at once and with arrears.
  • Who was it for? Who were all the citizens? (8:34) The Declaration does not say. The clue is Sieyès's draft: "passive" citizens, protected but without a vote (women, "at least in the present state", children, foreigners and those who did not contribute to sustaining the State), and "active" citizens, who elect. The rule came with the Constitution of 1791: men aged 25 or over who paid a contribution worth three days' work, were not servants and had taken the oath; about 16 in every 100 inhabitants. It does not mention women when it grants the vote: it was taken for granted that they did not vote.

From the student worksheet

  • While you watch the video. 1) That the dues tied to mortmain and personal servitude are abolished without compensation; everything else is "rachetable", redeemable (it has to be bought out), and meanwhile "will continue to be collected". 2) Twenty times what was paid each year; twenty-five if it was paid in kind (letters patent of 9-5-1790, art. XXI). Without compensation, on 17-7-1793. 3) The announcement, in one night (4-8-1789); the law, almost four years (1,443 days); many peasants, before the law. 4) Fast, the regime; slowly, where people live: in the countryside, about 80 in every 100 around 1789 (an estimate) and about 76 in 1846. 5) Women, children, foreigners and those who did not contribute to sustaining the State; it was a draft, not an approved text. 6) How much it changed people's lives; how many people it reached; for how long; what it reveals to us, about the past or about today.
  • The vote, out of every hundred. 1) 16; "?" (nobody counted: "aucun document ne fixe le total des électeurs inscrits", "no document establishes the total number of registered voters", Meuriot, 1916, p. 159); 0.3; 28. 2) Backwards: from 16 in every 100 to 3 in every 1,000. 3) Forwards: from fewer than 2 in every 1,000 to about 3 in every 1,000, almost double (Meuriot, pp. 158 and 221–233; calculated). The same facts give "step backwards" or "step forwards" depending on the criterion: what counts is that students say which one they are using, not that they declare one of them "the true one".
  • Fast, slow or hardly at all? 1) F (on paper). 2) S: it went on being collected until 1793, although many stopped paying earlier. 3) F: fast time. 4) H: from about 80 to about 76 in every 100 in 57 years; "hardly at all" is not "not at all". 5) B, by the criterion "who is free": abolished in 1794 and restored by force in 1802. 6) S: law of 1795, the only measure allowed from 1-1-1840. The final question is open: what counts is that students use a criterion (for example, "how many people": what was paid for the land affected the majority).

Practice (the solutions open class 5)

  1. An oral source: a day in the life of an older person in the family when they were your age. Open-ended. Criteria: three changes and three continuities, each with its reason; if something has got "worse", with its criterion and for whom; and some sign of pace (what changed at a stroke and what little by little). It is a source, and it is questioned: memory reconstructs. With permission, and without publishing anything private.
  2. Spain: women's first vote in a general election (1933) and the years without free general elections, between those of February 1936 and those of June 1977. The timeline, where it went backwards and by what criterion. Fact, criterion and judgement, separately.
  3. The facts, without adjectives. 19-11-1933: the first general election in which women vote (Constitution of 1931, art. 36: "los ciudadanos de uno y de otro sexo, mayores de veintitrés años", "citizens of either sex, over twenty-three years of age"). 16-2-1936: the last general election before the war. Between it and the June 1977 election there are no free general elections: the parties, dissolved (decree of 19-4-1937); referendums in 1947 and 1966 (men and women aged 21 or over) and, from 1967, the "family third" (tercio familiar: heads of family and married women), without parties. 15-6-1977: free general elections, with men and women of full age voting (then, 21).
  4. A criterion, stated: for example, "how many people can freely elect their deputies". With it, the line drops to zero after February 1936, for women and for men, and comes back in 1977. The judgement: calling it a "step backwards" is a judgement with that criterion; with another ("who votes in any vote at all"), the referendums included men and women. That is why the exact word is "free".
  5. What does not work: "there was never any voting"; "Franco took the vote away from women" as a sentence on its own (free elections disappeared, for everyone); and "the 1936 elections were the last free elections" as a closed statement, because they have their own debate among historians: what is certain is that between them and those of 1977 there were none. If someone goes further back, the line also comes and goes: 1868, men aged 25 or over; 1878, only those who paid a tax quota; 1890, all men again; 1923–1930, no general elections.
  6. The four questions of significance, applied to the opening of the Wall. As significant as 1789? Open-ended. How much it changed: for East Germans, a great deal and fast (free elections on 18-3-1990 and a single country on 3-10-1990, class 3). How many people: the two German states straight away; beyond that, it depends on how it is related to the end of the Eastern bloc regimes and of the USSR (topic 16), and that is an interpretation. For how long: Germany has remained united since 1990. What it reveals to us: one possible reading is that a regime can fall without a war, or that chance sometimes decides the when and the how (class 3). The comparison has no textbook answer: what counts is that students use the same criteria for both and say where they are looking from, because significance "varies over time and from group to group" (Seixas and Morton, 2013). For the debate: in the small hours of that same night, ABC wrote that "con este hecho histórico comienza un período fundamental, a juicio de los observadores internacionales, dentro de la historia contemporánea" ("with this historic event begins a fundamental period, in the view of international observers, within contemporary history"; 10-11-1989, p. 1). Contemporaries also decide what is "historic"; later, historians revise it.

Keep thinking. In a hundred years' time, a historian writes about our era. Of everything you have lived through, which event would they choose, and by what criterion? It has no textbook answer. Some pointers, without closing it: the "for how long" question cannot be answered yet, and that is why historians need distance; Partington added a criterion that the class does not use, the importance people at the time gave it, which for our own era we can answer (as reported by Hunt, 2000, and Phillips, 2002); significance "is constructed" and "varies" (Seixas and Morton, 2013). If someone chooses a personal event, it is fair to ask them how many people it affected: the criterion is discussed, not their private life. The teacher moderates and does not give a verdict.

A classroom activity: what two villages asked for

Duration: 16 minutes. Materials: two cahiers de doléances (lists of grievances) from April 1789 and the laws that answered them, open on Persée and the Internet Archive; below, the excerpts with their translation, for printing. The French of the 1879 edition, with modernised spelling, reads easily with the glossary.

The documents

  • A. Gres-en-Brie ("paroisse de Grés en Brie, bailliage de Paris"), cahier approved at the inhabitants' assembly on 13 April 1789. Archives parlementaires, 1st series, vol. IV (1879), pp. 592–594: https://www.persee.fr/doc/arcpa_0000-0000_1879_num_4_1_2205
  • "Il est de la dernière importance que les droits de champart, dans les lieux où ils sont étendus, soient totalement abolis" ("It is of the utmost importance that the champart dues, in the places where they extend, be totally abolished").
  • "La dîme […] se perçoit partout inégalement […]. Elle devrait être perçue uniformément, et à raison de quatre gerbes par arpent" ("The tithe […] is collected unequally everywhere […]. It ought to be collected uniformly, at the rate of four sheaves per arpent").
  • "le lapin et les bêtes de grande chasse ravageaient nos récoltes" ("the rabbit and the big game were ravaging our crops").
  • To the nobility and the clergy: "répartir sur vous comme sur nous l'impôt en parfaite égalité, et chacun en proportion de ses possessions" ("to apportion the tax on you as on us, in perfect equality, and each in proportion to what he possesses").
  • B. Sceaux-Penthièvre, "près Paris", general assembly of the parish on 12 April 1789, Easter Sunday. Archives parlementaires, 1st series, vol. V (1879), pp. 115–116: https://www.persee.fr/doc/arcpa_0000-0000_1879_num_5_1_2410
  • Art. 5: "Que la suppression de tous les privilèges soit opérée" ("That the abolition of all privileges be carried out").
  • Arts. 8, 11 and 12: that the hunting preserves ("capitaineries") and the right to keep dovecotes be abolished, and that the rabbits be destroyed.
  • Art. 9: "Qu'il soit permis de faire le rachat des dîmes en un abonnement en argent" ("That it be permitted to redeem the tithes by a fixed payment in money").
  • Art. 10: "Qu'il en soit de même pour le rachat des champarts et surcens seigneuriaux, et pour le rachat de toutes rentes seigneuriales" ("That the same apply to the redemption of seigneurial champarts and surcens, and to the redemption of all seigneurial rents").
  • Art. 18: "Que toutes les mesures soient réduites à une seule" ("That all measures be reduced to a single one").
  • At the foot, it is signed by the inhabitants "qui le savent, et les autres ont déclaré ne le savoir" ("who know how [to sign]; the others declared that they did not know how").
  • C. The law. The August 1789 decree, arts. 1, 2, 3, 5, 6 and 9 (Archives parlementaires, vol. VIII, pp. 397–398: https://www.persee.fr/doc/arcpa_0000-0000_1875_num_8_1_4833_t2_0397_0000_3); the poster of 9 May 1790, art. XXI, with the price of redemption (https://archive.org/details/case_oversize_frc_10460_no_03); the law of April 1790, which stops collecting the tithes from 1 January 1791; the decree of 17 July 1793, art. 1 (Archives parlementaires, vol. LXIX, p. 98: https://archive.org/details/archivesparlemen69pariuoft); and the metre, law of 1795, compulsory from 1840.

Glossary: champart, the share of the harvest that the lord took; dîme, tithe; capitaineries and remises, hunting preserves and game cover; rachat, redemption (paying to be free of a charge); surcens, an additional cens, on top of the first; arpent, a measure of area that each place measured in its own way.

Steps

  1. (2 min) Groups of three; half get Gres-en-Brie, and the other half Sceaux-Penthièvre. The four questions to the cahier: who made it? when and where? what for? for whom? Who signs? Who does not appear?
  2. (7 min) With documents C, a table: grievance · what the village asked for · what the law said, and when · at a stroke, slowly or not at all? · more than, the same as or less than what was asked?
  3. (4 min) Pooling: the champart of the two villages, side by side. Gres asked for it to be abolished; Sceaux, to be able to redeem it. Which one did the law of 1789–1790 agree with? And that of 1793? What does that say about "the peasants" as if they were a single bloc?
  4. (3 min) Significance with a criterion: which change was the most significant for these villagers? Each group chooses one and defends it with one of the four questions; on the board, each group's criterion.

Wrap-up. The cahiers do not speak for "the peasants": two parishes on the outskirts of Paris asked for different things for the same charge. The law answered at different paces: hunting and dovecotes, at a stroke and on paper; the tithe, in a year and a half; the champart, in four years; a single measure, in half a century. And not always for everyone: the right to hunt on one's land belonged to the owner. For context: in 748 parish cahiers, eight of the ten most frequent grievances were about the state's taxes: "d'abord les impôts, encore les impôts, toujours les impôts" ("taxes first, taxes again, taxes always"; Markoff, 1991, p. 59).

Do it yourself: the Archives parlementaires collection on Persée has, in its first volumes, the 1789 cahiers de doléances of many parishes, with text search. Search for "cahier" and the name of a village, and repeat the table. Poster variant: project the 1790 poster (Newberry Library) and zoom in to find article XXI: how much would it cost to be free of a rent of 10 livres a year? (200 livres at once, plus arrears; if it was paid in grain, twenty-five times one year's payment.)

For teachers: what does not fit in the video

  • Condorcet himself defended votes for women. In 1790: "Ou aucun individu de l'espèce humaine n'a de véritables droits, ou tous ont les mêmes ; et celui qui vote contre le droit d'un autre, quels que soient sa religion, sa couleur ou son sexe, a dès lors abjuré les siens" ("Either no individual of the human species has true rights, or all have the same; and he who votes against the right of another, whatever his religion, his colour or his sex, has from that moment abjured his own"; Journal de la Société de 1789, no. V, 3-7-1790; Œuvres, vol. X, 1847, p. 122). In the end he proposed starting with women who owned property. It helps not to turn him into a straw man. And his "jamais rétrograde" speaks of the future, of a perfectibility "désormais indépendante de toute puissance qui voudroit les arrêter" ("henceforth independent of any power that might wish to halt [its progress]"; Esquisse, 1795, p. 4): the decades that followed are a fair test of his prediction.
  • The twist of the criterion. In 1791, the 16 in every 100 only voted at the first level: they chose some 43,800 electors, fewer than 2 in every 1,000, who elected the deputies; in 1817, some 102,000, more than 3 in every 1,000, elected them directly (Meuriot, 1916, pp. 158 and 221–233; calculated). By the criterion "who elected the deputies", the change went upwards: it is the best test of "by what criterion?", and it is on the worksheet. And another nuance: in 1791 there were some 4.3 million active citizens against 2.7 million passive ones, and the website of France's Assemblée nationale warns: "Il est faux d'affirmer que 'seuls les riches pouvaient voter'" ("It is false to claim that 'only the rich could vote'").
  • Tocqueville, the classic of continuity. In 1856 he wrote that in 1789 the French made the greatest effort ever "afin de couper pour ainsi dire en deux leur destinée" ("in order to cut their destiny in two, so to speak"), but that, "à leur insu" ("without knowing it"), they kept from the Ancien Régime most of the feelings, habits and even ideas with which they had made the Revolution (L'Ancien Régime et la Révolution, foreword; 7th ed., 1866, pp. i–ii). The title of his book II, chapter 2, sums it up: administrative centralisation is "une institution de l'ancien régime, et non pas l'œuvre de la révolution et de l'empire, comme on le dit" ("an institution of the Ancien Régime, and not the work of the Revolution and the Empire, as people say"). A good text to comment on at the end.
  • Hansa Mehta, in detail. On 12-12-1947, in Geneva, she asked for "all men", which could be read as excluding women, to be changed to "human beings" or "persons"; Eleanor Roosevelt, who was chairing, replied that "men" already included everyone, and a note with that reading was approved (E/CN.4/SR.34, pp. 4–6). In 1948, the Commission on the Status of Women proposed "All people" (E/CN.4/81); India and the United Kingdom, "All people, men and women" (E/CN.4/99); and the Belgian delegate Lebeau, as a compromise, "all human beings", with the support of that commission (E/CN.4/SR.50, pp. 9–15). The UN says that Mehta "is widely credited" with the change, and also highlights Bodil Begtrup (Denmark) and Minerva Bernardino (Dominican Republic): that is the collective part of criterion 8.2. That is why the class says that Mehta was the first to ask for it, not that she did it alone.
  • What a peasant really paid. There is no serious overall figure, and it is best not to give one. What went to the lord varied enormously from one region to another: in the Midi and south of Paris, some 3 or 4 hundredths of the gross product; in Brittany or Burgundy, much more (Goubert, p. 149). The tithe was almost never a tenth: the usual rate was one sheaf in twelve or thirteen (Marion, 1923, p. 174). In Chalifert-en-Brie, in 1789, it was paid "à la onzième gerbe" ("at the eleventh sheaf") and, on wine, "la vingt et unième pièce" ("the twenty-first cask") (Archives parlementaires, vol. IV, p. 392).
  • From the 1789 decree to the 1793 one. The king did not sign the August decrees: he only ordered them to be published (20 September), and they were promulgated on 3 November. In March 1790 the banalités and personal labour services fell without compensation, but it was the peasant who had to prove that a charge did not come from the land; in May, redemption was made indivisible and with arrears; in August 1792 the burden of proof was reversed; and in July 1793 everything fell without compensation, with the title deeds to be burned on 10 August. On how many people redeemed, Lefebvre had already warned in 1929: "il faut renoncer à dresser une statistique" ("we must give up on drawing up any statistics", p. 138); regional studies agree that very few did.
  • Did any woman vote in 1789? A few, through a man: the regulation of 24-1-1789 allowed noblewomen who held a fief and communities of nuns to be represented by a proxy (arts. 11 and 20). The safe sentence is another one: the deputies were all men.
  • Backwards, after 1848 too. The law of 31 May 1850 required three years' residence, proven through taxes, and the electorate fell from about 10 to about 7 million until December 1851 (Archives de Maine-et-Loire; Musée du compagnonnage). That is why the video does not say "for good".
  • Slavery, colony by colony. The 1794 abolition was not applied in Martinique (occupied by the British), Réunion or Mauritius, and "free" meant obliged to stay on the plantation. In 1802, the law of 20 May "maintained" it where it had not been abolished; in Guadeloupe it was restored by a decree of 16 July, and in French Guiana by one of December, applied in 1803; in Saint-Domingue it was not, and Haiti became independent on 1-1-1804. The decree of 27-4-1848 freed some 250,000 people in the Antilles, Réunion and Senegal. The Bois-Caïman ceremony is a disputed tradition, not a proven fact.
  • The figures change depending on who is counting. Sieyès counted "pas deux cent mille privilégiés" ("not two hundred thousand privileged") among "vingt-cinq à vingt-six millions d'âmes" ("twenty-five to twenty-six million souls"; ch. III): fewer than 1 in every 100 (calculated); Piketty, 1.5. A question for the classroom, with the four from class 1: why might it suit Sieyès to count few?
  • The new time. By the calendar's official rule, the equinox one, 21 October 2026 was 29 Vendémiaire of year 235, the day of barley. The metre, compulsory in France from 1840, became compulsory in Spain from 1860 (law of 1849), with more than forty years of actual implementation (Centro Español de Metrología); and its definition changed in 1889, 1960 and 1983 (BIPM): the name lasts, not the definition. The Sceaux-Penthièvre cahier was already asking for it in April 1789.
  • The ages. The dictionary of the Real Academia Española does not give 1789 ("desde fines del siglo XVIII o principios del XIX", "from the end of the 18th century or the beginning of the 19th"); Ribot (2016): in Spain 1789 "se utiliza habitualmente" ("is commonly used"); the Cambridge Faculty of History: contemporary history "lacks firm boundaries" (1918, 1930 or 1945). And Hobsbawm starts in 1789 because of a "dual revolution", the French one and the British Industrial Revolution (The Age of Revolution, 1962).
