Condemned to be free

In a nutshell

It is a lecture, not a class: about fifteen minutes for listening and thinking, with no exercises, no countdowns and no questions to take home. Javier speaks from the school assembly hall. It is the fifth in a series of eight, “If God is dead, what is left?”, and it stands on its own.

The question: if no one made you with a plan, who answers for what you are? The chair is the series’ image: for centuries God occupied it, and he answered what is true, what is right and what we live for. In lecture 4, with Heidegger, we saw who sits in it if you do not: the “one”, what one does, which is nobody. This one takes the next step, from the “one” to “you”.

The message, what listeners take away for their own lives: no one made you with a plan: you are what you do, and not choosing is also choosing. Don’t hide behind “that’s just how I am”, and don’t let anyone tell you what you have to be because of how you were born. And being free means wanting others to be free as well.

It is Javier’s reading, and he says so. He sets out his thesis in advance (“you; you always choose, even when you say you are not choosing”) and marks as his own what is his own: that what happens to you is often not something you choose, but what you do with it is; that one is not born a man either (“my reading, not a sentence of hers”); and that, for him, the most important thing today is Beauvoir’s sentence about the freedom of others. “Bad faith, imposed from outside” is not an extension of his own: Beauvoir writes it in the introduction to The Second Sex (see “Going further”).

Sartre and Beauvoir, for what they thought. When introducing them, Javier says in one sentence, with no figures or dates and without dwelling on it, why bringing them in is hard: that Beauvoir had relationships with very young former students of hers, and that both of them signed in support of some men on trial for abusing minors. They are brought in “only for their philosophy”. The lecture comes back to it only once, at the end of the chapter “Willing oneself free”: neither she nor Sartre always lived up to what they wrote, and that is why the idea is worth more than those who wrote it. The note “Why bringing them in is hard”, below, gives the facts with their sources, in case students ask.

Without anguish. In Existentialism Is a Humanism and in Being and Nothingness, freedom goes hand in hand with anguish, abandonment and despair (“man is anguish”, “man is a useless passion”). The lecture, designed for sixteen-year-olds, does not go there: it stays with responsibility and with what can be done with it. It is a partial reading, and it is said here for anyone who wants to complete it.

Girls, boys and those who do not recognise themselves in either of the two boxes. The Second Sex is about women, and its thesis is an asymmetry: a man is not defined by his sex; a woman is. The lecture says so. It adds, with a sentence from Beauvoir herself (“a man doesn’t cry”, a little boy is told), that boys get labelled too, “even if it is not the same”, and, in another sentence, that there are those who do not recognise themselves in either of the two boxes. It does not enter into debates or put labels on anyone: “No one should have to live locked inside a role they didn’t choose”. The questions speak of “because of how you were born”, not of “girl or boy”.

The journey, in eight chapters:

  • “That’s just how I am”. The phrases we use so as not to choose (“I had no choice”, “it is what it is”, “it’s my character”) and the tests and videos that tell you in ten seconds what type of person you are, as if you came factory-made. The map of the series, lecture 4 in one sentence and today’s question. Sartre and Beauvoir, introduced, and why bringing them in is hard.
  • You exist first. Paris, Monday 29 October 1945: Sartre’s lecture at the Club Maintenant, packed (Beauvoir recounts that there was pushing and shoving and that some women fainted), which came out as a book the following year. The paper knife: the craftsman knows what it is and what it is for before making it; its essence precedes its existence. Without a God who has conceived us, with us it is the other way round: first you exist, and afterwards you define yourself. “Man is nothing other than what he makes of himself”, the sum of his actions.
  • No excuses. The sentence Sartre attributes to Dostoevsky, “if God did not exist, everything would be permitted”, as the starting point. With no values written in the heavens, there are no excuses, neither behind us nor in front of us: “man is condemned to be free”. It is impossible not to choose: “if I do not choose, I am still choosing”; in Being and Nothingness, not choosing is “to choose not to choose”. The student from lecture 1, who asked Sartre for advice: choosing whom you ask for advice is already choosing. Today, we ask a friend, a video, an account or an artificial intelligence, like Javier himself.
  • He is playing at being a waiter. Bad faith: lying to oneself, being at once the deceiver and the deceived. The waiter in Being and Nothingness: “he is playing at being a waiter”, as if he were one in the way an inkwell is an inkwell. There is nothing wrong with being a waiter: bad faith is believing that you are that and nothing more. “That’s just how I am” is turning yourself into a thing so as not to carry the weight of what you choose. “There is no such thing as a cowardly temperament”: the coward makes himself a coward, and can always stop being one. And Javier, as his own view: what happens to you, you often do not choose; what you always choose is what you do with it.
  • Hell is other people. No Exit (Huis clos, 1944): three characters in a hell that is a room, with no fire and no torturers. The sentence is usually read the wrong way round, as if other people were a nuisance. In 1965 Sartre made it clear: if relationships are twisted, the other becomes a hell, because we know ourselves through what others think of us; and there are many people in hell because they depend too much on other people’s judgement. Today that judgement comes with numbers: likes, followers, comments.
  • One is not born a woman. Beauvoir, a philosopher with her own body of work, not just Sartre’s partner. The Second Sex (1949): “One is not born a woman: one becomes one”; no destiny decides what a woman is in society: society as a whole does. “He is the Subject, he is the Absolute: she is the Other.” It is bad faith imposed from outside, and Beauvoir calls it that: taking that “are” as something fixed, when “to be is to have become”. Boys get labelled too; and there are those who do not recognise themselves in either of the two boxes. Today, accounts with millions of followers that make a living from telling you how a girl has to be and how a boy has to be, and that often teach people to despise whoever doesn’t fit.
  • Willing oneself free. Isn’t anything allowed, then? Beauvoir turns Dostoevsky’s sentence around: without God, no one forgives or erases for us; our acts are definitive commitments. Sartre: “in choosing myself, I choose man”, and that is why we are responsible for everyone. Beauvoir: “to will oneself free is also to will others free”; being free is not being able to do just anything (“I am oppressed if they throw me into prison; not if they stop me from throwing my neighbour into it”). And the echo: neither she nor Sartre always lived up to it; the idea also serves to judge them.
  • What is left? The three questions: what is true is not touched on today; what is right and what we live for are no longer answered by anyone from the chair, not God, nor a human nature written in advance, nor the “one”, nor what people say about you, nor the role you are given because of how you were born: you answer them, with what you do. The chair is left empty, and that is why everything you do weighs more. Today’s answer: you; the empty chair does not leave you without answers, it leaves you without excuses. In lecture 6, Albert Camus and Sisyphus. The series’ question, on its own, and the message.

For readers outside Spain: the Bachillerato is the last two years of secondary school (ages 16–18); Philosophy is compulsory in the first year for every track, and History of Philosophy in the second. On this sheet, “1st year” and “2nd year” (1.º and 2.º in Spanish) are those two years. Royal Decree 243/2022 sets the minimum curriculum of the Bachillerato for the whole of Spain; the Philosophy Lectures are one of the channel’s series and sit outside it. The video is in Spanish, with English subtitles. The “one” is Heidegger’s das Man, from lecture 4: el “se” in the Spanish of the video. On this sheet the quotations are given in our own English translation, made from Sartre’s and Beauvoir’s French (and, for Dostoevsky, from the Russian). A comentario de texto, or text commentary, is the classic Spanish classroom exercise of explaining a philosophical passage in writing.

  • Eight chapters, each with something that moves: a cardboard label, “factory-made”, that falls onto the shoulder of a girl with her phone; the hall of 1945, filling up; the paper knife, first an idea and then an object, and a person who is drawn, little by little, by their own acts; the parchment of values, blank; the student facing his two paths and the doors he can knock on; the Paris café waiter, illustrated, with his tray held high and his excess of zeal; the room in No Exit, with three different sofas and the looks that pass from one to another; the boxes you are put in from outside; the chalk line that the board eraser does not wipe away; and the series’ chair, which is left empty at the end.
  • The visual thread is the cardboard label: “factory-made” at the start, the one you put on yourself (“that’s just how I am”) with the waiter, and the one others put on you from outside (“women are like that”) with Beauvoir. And, as throughout the series, the chair and the three cards with its questions.
  • Nine pauses, without warning and without a countdown:
  • How many times have you said “that’s just how I am” so as not to change something?
  • And you: with what you do every day, what are you making of yourself?
  • When you ask for advice, whom do you ask? And why that one in particular?
  • What are you choosing when you say you have no choice?
  • How much of what you think you are have others told you?
  • What have you been told you had to be, because of how you were born?
  • Is there anyone whose freedom you are taking away without realising it?
  • The series’ question: if God is dead, what is left?
  • And you: what are you going to make of yourself?
  • Each quotation, with its work on screen. Javier reads them; Sartre and Beauvoir are given no voice. The translations into Spanish are our own and literal, from the French: Existentialism Is a Humanism from the first edition (Nagel, 1946), checked against Victoria Prati de Fernández’s translation; Being and Nothingness from the 1943 Gallimard edition; No Exit from the Folio edition; The Ethics of Ambiguity from the 1947 Gallimard edition; The Second Sex from the Gallimard edition, checked against Alicia Martorell’s (Cátedra). What Javier says in his own words goes in chalk, not as a quotation.
  • Vocabulary. Mauvaise foi is mala fe in Spanish, “bad faith” on this sheet: it is not deceiving someone else, but lying to oneself. “Thrown into the world” is Sartre’s jeté dans le monde; in Being and Nothingness he links it to Heidegger: “thrown into freedom or, as Heidegger says, ‘abandoned’” (p. 565). It is the “thrownness” of lecture 4. L'Autre, in Beauvoir, is lo Otro, “the Other”, a category (Martorell writes la Alteridad, “Alterity”; Palant, el Otro, with a masculine article). In the video, the quotation from The Ethics of Ambiguity is spoken so that it is clear when heard: “authorise all licence” becomes “permit everything”, and “man’s faults are inexpiable” becomes “no one erases what you do”; the literal translation is on the student worksheet.
  • The student from lecture 1. In lecture 1, Sartre’s reply was heard with the informal Spanish tú: eres libre, elige; es decir, inventa. Here it is with the formal usted, usted es libre, elija, es decir, invente, which is how Sartre writes it (vous êtes libre, choisissez, c'est-à-dire inventez). It is the same sentence (in English, both are “you are free, choose, that is to say, invent”): if students notice the difference, today’s is the one faithful to the original.

Using it in class, if you wish

The lecture sets no task. If teachers want to use it, here are four ways, from least to most time:

  • Reading aloud. The waiter passage (Being and Nothingness, pp. 98-99), which is on the student worksheet, fits into three minutes. Then a question: what does the waiter do that gives away that he is “playing” at being one? How is it like saying “that’s just how I am”?
  • With 1st- or 2nd-year students, ten minutes: “I had no choice”. Each student writes, without signing it, a sentence they have said at some point so as not to choose. A few are read out, without saying whose they are, and each one is asked the question from pause 4: what were you choosing when you said it? And Javier’s sentence: which part of it happened to you, and which part was what you did with it?
  • With 1st-year students, fifteen minutes of dialogue. Three questions, with no single answer:
  • Sartre says there is no such thing as a cowardly temperament. Do you agree? What part of what you are have you not chosen, and what part have you?
  • If “Hell is other people” does not mean that other people are a nuisance, what does it mean? Can you think of an example from today?
  • “I am oppressed if they throw me into prison; not if they stop me from throwing my neighbour into it.” Where does your freedom end? Who decides where?
  • With 2nd-year students, a text commentary. The end of the first part of Existentialism Is a Humanism, from the paper knife to the “first principle” (pp. 17-22 of the Nagel edition), in Victoria Prati de Fernández’s translation (Edhasa, in the school library: the work is under copyright; in English, Carol Macomber’s, Yale, 2007). The lecture serves as an introduction and does not do the commentary for them: it leaves out the distinction between Christian existentialism (Jaspers, Marcel) and atheist existentialism, anguish and despair, and the objections from communists and Christians with which Sartre opens his lecture. To contrast with Javier’s reading, Arlette Elkaïm-Sartre’s preface in the same edition: she recounts that Sartre soon regretted having allowed it to be published, because it was read as a summary of Being and Nothingness, which it was not.

Misreadings the lecture takes apart

What is often said What the texts and the sources say
Dostoevsky wrote “if God does not exist, everything is permitted” It is not in The Brothers Karamazov in that form. It is a “summary” quotation of what Ivan and other characters say (“there is no virtue if there is no immortality”, “everything is permitted”); the closest thing is Mitya’s question: “Without God and without a future life? So now everything is permitted?”. That is why the voice says “a sentence that Sartre attributes to Dostoevsky”
For Sartre, “Hell is other people” because other people are a nuisance It is said by a character, Garcin. And Sartre, in 1965: “‘Hell is other people’ has always been misunderstood”; he meant that, if relationships are twisted, the other becomes a hell, because we know ourselves through their judgement. And he adds that other relationships are possible (Un théâtre de situations, p. 238)
If there is no God, anything goes: existentialism is nihilism Beauvoir says just the opposite: “far from the absence of God authorising all licence”, without God acts are definitive commitments, and “man’s faults are inexpiable” (The Ethics of Ambiguity, p. 23). And Sartre: choosing is affirming what you believe is valuable for everyone (EH, pp. 25-27)
Being free is being able to do whatever you want “To be free is not to have the power to do just anything”; the freedom of others is “the condition of my own freedom” (PMA, p. 127). The claim that other people’s freedom limits mine is the sophism Beauvoir refutes
For Sartre everything is chosen: if things go badly for you, it is your fault The lecture does not put it that way. Sartre says that it is impossible not to choose and that the coward makes himself a coward through his acts, not through his temperament. Javier qualifies it for today, as his own view: what happens to you, you often do not choose; what you always choose is what you do with it
The Second Sex begins with “One is not born a woman” That is how the second volume, “Lived Experience”, begins. The first begins with the introduction, the one about the Subject and the Other
Beauvoir wrote that one is not born a man either She did not write it. She writes that a little boy is told “a man doesn’t cry”, but her thesis is the asymmetry: “a man never begins by presenting himself as an individual of a certain sex: that he is a man goes without saying”. Extending it to boys is Javier’s reading, and he says so
Sartre wrote “we are condemned to be free” In Existentialism Is a Humanism it is in the singular: “man is condemned to be free” (p. 37); in Being and Nothingness, “I am condemned to be free” (p. 515) and “we are condemned to freedom” (p. 565). The lecture’s title is ours
“Not choosing is also choosing”, as a quotation from Sartre It is a summary of two sentences: “what is not possible is not to choose […] if I do not choose, I am still choosing” (EH, p. 73) and “not to choose is, in fact, to choose not to choose” (EN, p. 561). The voice says them like that, each with its book

Going further: what the lecture doesn’t say aloud

  • Bad faith in The Second Sex. Beauvoir uses Sartre’s word for what the lecture calls “bad faith, imposed from outside”. In the introduction to the first volume, to those who say that woman “is” inferior, she replies that we must agree on the scope of the word “to be”: “bad faith consists in giving it a substantial value, when it has the dynamic Hegelian sense: to be is to have become, to have been made such as one appears”. Yes, she goes on, women as a whole have fewer possibilities today; the question is whether that has to stay that way. In the same volume she comes back to the word twice: each person’s situation “dictates good or bad faith to men and to women”, and “no woman can claim, without bad faith, to place herself beyond her sex”. She explains it with a joke by Bernard Shaw: the white American relegates the Black man to shining shoes and concludes that he is fit only for shining shoes. It is the same circle: someone is kept down and then it is said that they “are” like that (DS, vol. I, introduction; Folio, 1986, p. 25).
  • Sartre had written it too. “To will oneself free is also to will others free” is Beauvoir’s (1947), but Sartre had already said it in 1945, in his own way: as soon as there is commitment, “I am obliged to will, at the same time as my freedom, the freedom of others” (EH, p. 83). That is why the voice says that “Sartre had said it in his own way”, and that the idea is worth more than “those who” wrote it.
  • A philosopher? The lecture presents her as a philosopher with her own body of work, which is today’s reading: that of the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy and that of the curriculum, which places her in History of Philosophy. But she denied it: she called herself a writer and said that her philosophical voice was merely an elaboration of Sartre’s (SEP, “Simone de Beauvoir”). If a student brings it up, it is a good question for the classroom: who decides whether someone is a philosopher?
  • The torturer in No Exit. “With no fire and no torturers” means without the torturer of the traditional hell. In the play, Inès says it before Garcin: “The torturer is each of us for the other two”. And at the end the door flies open, and no one leaves (HC, Folio, pp. 41-42 and 93).
  • More of the waiter. Sartre goes on with the dance of the grocer, the tailor and the auctioneer, and with the soldier standing to attention, who “makes himself a soldier-thing”: “these are many precautions to imprison man in what he is”. And he concludes: however much he carries out the functions of a waiter, he can only be one “in the neutralised mode, as the actor is Hamlet” (EN, pp. 99-100).
  • The 1945 lecture, with its limits. The date, the place (the Salle des Centraux, rue Jean-Goujon), the crowd and the fainting are in two independent sources: Beauvoir’s memoirs (Force of Circumstance) and the next day’s headline in Combat, “Too many people to hear Jean-Paul Sartre”. The broken chairs and the smashed ticket office are reported only by his biographer, Annie Cohen-Solal; the “fifteen fainting fits” and the “thirty seats” are phrases from the press that she quotes without saying which newspaper. That is why the voice does not mention them. The date given by Contat and Rybalka, the 28th, is a mistake: 28 October 1945 was a Sunday.
  • The ethics Sartre promised. Being and Nothingness ends with a promise: the moral questions “can find their answer only on the moral plane. We shall devote a future work to them”. It never came out: he left it half-finished, as the video says in one sentence. His notes, from 1947 and 1948, were published in 1983, after his death: the Cahiers pour une morale (Notebooks for an Ethics). Beauvoir’s The Ethics of Ambiguity (1947) is often read as that missing ethics; in her memoirs, Beauvoir said it was the book of hers that irritated her most, although she still stood by “the polemical part”.
  • How The Second Sex was received. According to her own account, the first volume sold twenty-two thousand copies in the first week; the second caused a scandal. In 1956 the Holy Office put it on the Index of Forbidden Books, together with The Mandarins (Acta Apostolicae Sedis 48, p. 508).
  • “Accounts with millions of followers”. The sentence names no one, on purpose: it speaks of a behaviour, not of a person. If it comes up in class, the group can look at which accounts reach each of them and what they say about how a girl or a boy has to be, without judging whoever follows them.
  • Why bringing them in is hard. The lecture says it in one sentence and does not dwell on it, on purpose: it brings Sartre and Beauvoir in for what they thought. If students ask, these are the facts.
  • The former students. Beauvoir had love affairs with two of her former lycée students, when she was already over thirty: Bianca Bienenfeld, from June 1938, at seventeen, on finishing her baccalauréat, and Nathalie Sorokine, from late 1939, at eighteen. With a third, Olga Kosakiewicz, her student at seventeen, she had a passionate friendship; there is no record that it was sexual. Sartre was Bianca’s lover in 1939, when she was seventeen or eighteen, and left her by letter in February 1940. There was no conviction and, under the laws of the time, there is no record that it was a crime; what is debated today is that she used her position as a teacher with girls who admired her. Bianca told her story in 1993, after reading the letters published in 1990: she said she had been a “victim” of Sartre’s Don Juan impulses and of the “ambivalent and shady protection” that Beauvoir gave them. And Beauvoir had written to Sartre in the winter of 1945-1946: “she is the only person we have really hurt, but we did hurt her”. (B. Lamblin, Mémoires d'une jeune fille dérangée, 1993; Beauvoir’s letters to Sartre, 1990; K. Kirkpatrick, Becoming Beauvoir, 2019; H. Rowley, Tête-à-tête, 2005; D. Bair, Simone de Beauvoir, 1990.)
  • The 1977 petition. On 26 January 1977, Le Monde published «À propos d'un procès», in support of three men who had spent more than three years in pre-trial detention for “indecent assault without violence on minors under fifteen”. It was signed by sixty-nine people, among them Sartre and Beauvoir. It said: “Three years in prison for caresses and kisses: that is enough”. According to the same newspaper’s report on the trial, the minors were aged twelve to fifteen. In May 1977, both of them also signed a letter to the commission revising the penal code that asked for “the consent of the minor” to be taken into account. There is no record that either of them ever said anything about it afterwards. In 2001, Le Monde wrote: “far from ‘caresses and kisses’, it was a sordid affair”. («À propos d'un procès», Le Monde, 26/01/1977, and the report on the trial, 29/01/1977; the May letter, in the Françoise Dolto archive; P. Georges, «Autre temps…», Le Monde, 22/02/2001.)
  • What is not said, because it is not accurate. That Beauvoir was dismissed from teaching “for corrupting a minor”: in 1943 the Vichy government removed her, but the criminal case, a complaint by Sorokine’s mother, had been dropped in 1942 for lack of evidence; the rector asked for her dismissal all the same, because of her life and what she taught, and she was reinstated in 1945 (I. Galster, Beauvoir dans tous ses états, 2007; G. Joseph, Une si douce Occupation, 1991). That she “passed students on to Sartre” as a rule: that is Bianca’s reading, and it is documented only in her case. Calling her a “paedophile”: it does not fit the ages.
  • The good, with the same rigour. They denounced torture in Algeria (Sartre, «Une victoire», 1958; Beauvoir, for Djamila Boupacha, 1960), and the far right planted two bombs in the building where Sartre’s mother lived (1961 and 1962). Beauvoir drafted the final version of the manifesto of the 343 women who declared they had had an abortion, when it was a crime, and signed it with them (1971); and she was the first president of Choisir, the association she founded with Gisèle Halimi. Sartre turned down the Nobel in 1964: “the writer must refuse to let himself be turned into an institution”. What is not said is “heroes of the Resistance”: Sartre’s part was modest, and it is debated.
