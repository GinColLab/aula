Happy Sisyphus

In a nutshell

It is a lecture, not a class: almost eighteen minutes for listening and thinking, with no exercises, no countdowns and no questions to take home. Javier speaks from the school assembly hall. It is the sixth in a series of eight, “If God is dead, what is left?”, and it stands on its own.

The question: if life does not come with a given meaning, can you be happy? The chair is the series’ image: for centuries God occupied it, and he answered what is true, what is right and what we live for. In lecture 5, with Sartre and Beauvoir, we saw that you are what you do, and that the empty chair does not leave you without answers, but without excuses. This one takes the next step: if what you do repeats itself every day without anyone telling you what for, can you be happy like that?

The message, what listeners take away for their own lives: life does not come with a meaning written in, and even so you can be happy. Look the “what for?” in the face, without buying every answer from anyone. Make your rock your own: a routine you like today, tomorrow and the day after, and that tramples no one; in a world that always pushes you towards the next thing, choosing it is already rebelling. And that revolt, with others and never against them.

It is Javier’s reading, and he says so. He sets out his thesis in advance (“yes, you can; and not only in spite of everyday life, but also within it”) and marks as his own, with the label “what I think”, what is his own:

  • the first way of escaping the question, never asking it, filling every gap (and he makes clear that he is not talking about having fun, “which is great”);
  • today’s leap, which “has nothing to do with faith”: whoever promises that everything will make sense if you follow them;
  • that Sisyphus’s contempt is contempt for the punishment, not for people;
  • that the absurd takes the joy of living away from no one;
  • and the whole chapter on routine: “I like routine, when the routine is one I like”, the today, tomorrow and the day after test, and that choosing a routine you like is almost rebellious. In the summary at the end, the test of the days comes back with its “I would tell you” and its label.

None of these sentences is put in Camus’s mouth. What is Camus’s goes as a quotation, with his work on screen. And the other way round: that rebelling is saying no to what humiliates even when it is not happening to you is not Javier’s, but Camus’s. He writes it in chapter I of The Rebel (L'Homme révolté): “revolt is not born only, nor necessarily, in the oppressed, but can also be born at the sight of the oppression of which another is the victim”. The lecture gives it to him, with his quotation.

Routine, from bad reputation to almost rebellious. The word does not appear at the start, neither in the list of what a boy does every day nor in Camus’s. It arrives with Sisyphus (“he pushes it up, the rock falls, he goes down to fetch it, and up again: does that remind you of anything?”), with its bad reputation today said out loud: today what changes is what wins, and “boring” is almost an insult. And it ends up winning: in a world that always pushes towards the next thing, choosing a routine you like is saying no and yes at once, which is exactly what Camus calls rebelling. Not just any “I like it” will do: the test is whether you would want it today, tomorrow and the day after, whether it tramples no one and whether you can explain why you like it.

Faith, with respect. Camus does not take the leap towards God that he sees in Kierkegaard, and he says so with respect, before some Dominican friars in Paris: “I shall never start from the principle that Christian truth is illusory, but only from this fact, that I have not been able to enter into it”. Javier adds that those who have faith live it differently, and that they will be given the floor in lecture 7, “The chair that will not be emptied”, with Unamuno, Simone Weil and María Zambrano. When he talks about those who today promise meaning in exchange for something, he makes clear that this has nothing to do with faith, and the image is a phone, with no religious symbol at all.

Camus, for what he thought and for how he lived. His life goes in one sentence, without dwelling on it: the poor neighbourhood of Algiers, the half-deaf mother who could not read and cleaned houses, her family, from Minorca, the schoolteacher who prepared him for a scholarship, the football goalkeeper, the Nobel at forty-three and the letter to that teacher. Unlike Heidegger, or Sartre and Beauvoir, there is nothing in his life that makes bringing him in hard, and so the lecture does not carry the “why bringing him in is hard” sentence (verificacion-vidas.md, point 9, in Spanish). What is debated about him are ideas, and the most debated, his position on Algeria, is further down, in “Going further”.

What the lecture does not cover. The Myth of Sisyphus comes in through routine, the absurd, the leap, Sisyphus and the three consequences. Left out are the absurd man (Don Juan, the actor, the conqueror), absurd creation and the appendix on Kafka. Of The Rebel, only the first part and the fifth: it does not go into metaphysical or historical revolt, nor into the critique of revolutions that set him against Sartre.

The journey, in eight chapters:

  • Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday. A boy’s day (alarm clock, bus, classes, lunch, homework, a bit of time on the phone, dinner, sleep) and the list Camus published in 1942: “Getting up, tram, four hours of office or factory, meal, tram, four hours of work, meal, sleep and Monday Tuesday Wednesday Thursday Friday and Saturday to the same rhythm”. One day the why arises, “and everything begins”: that weariness awakens consciousness. The map of the series, lecture 5 in one sentence and today’s question, with Javier’s thesis. Camus, introduced, and the honest warning: “no, I am not an existentialist”.
  • The absurd. Asking why is the first sign. But the absurd does not mean that the world is absurd, and Camus makes this precise: “this world in itself is not reasonable, that is all that can be said of it”. It gives no reasons; we carry “this frantic desire for clarity”. The absurd lies in the two together: it “is born of this confrontation between the human call and the unreasonable silence of the world”. And it is not a conclusion, but “a starting point”.
  • The two ways out. Never asking yourself the question, filling every gap (“between one video and the next, there is often not a single second of silence left”), or leaping, like Kierkegaard towards God. Camus does not leap, and he says so with respect. The honest thing is to stay on the edge: “knowing how to hold oneself on this dizzying ridge, that is honesty”. And Camus warns that there are many ways of leaping: today, for Javier, one of them is that of whoever promises that everything will make sense if you follow them, and who often asks in return that you stop thinking for yourself or that you despise someone. Camus proposes staying with the question, with your eyes open.
  • Sisyphus. The myth, on a Greek amphora from around 530 BC: the gods condemn him to push up a rock that falls back down, because there is no more terrible punishment than “useless and hopeless labour”. Why they punished him, according to the version Camus takes up: because he did not want to leave “the water and the sun, the warm stones and the sea”, nor “the smiles of the earth”. He is the absurd hero, “as much through his passions as through his torment”. The rock, which goes up and down every day, is a routine, and the word arrives, with its bad reputation; Camus builds the bridge: “the worker of today works, every day of his life, at the same tasks”. What interests Camus is the walk down, “this hour which is like a breath”, the hour of consciousness: “he is stronger than his rock”. Javier, to camera: Sisyphus does not have another rock; he has another way of looking at it.
  • One must imagine Sisyphus happy. “The clairvoyance that was to be his torment consummates his victory at the same stroke”: seeing clearly what is happening to you is what makes you win. “There is no destiny that is not surmounted by contempt”: contempt for the punishment, Javier reads, not for people. Joy, a word that “is not superfluous”: “his rock is his thing”, and “one must imagine Sisyphus happy”. It is not “put up with it and smile”: from the absurd Camus draws “my revolt, my freedom and my passion”. And earlier, in Nuptials, among the ruins of Tipasa: “there is no shame in being happy”.
  • I like routine, when the routine is one I like. The whole chapter is Javier’s, and it is labelled. Sisyphus did not choose his rock; your routine, to a large extent, is something you put together yourself. Not just any “I like it” will do: eating all the pastries is tempting today, but not today, tomorrow and the day after; laughing at someone does not pass Kant’s test, because other people exist. Kant multiplied by everyone and Nietzsche by eternity (lecture 3); today’s test, more humble, multiplies by the days. A routine can change over the years, not every day. And the twist: choosing it, in a world that pushes you towards the next thing, is saying no and yes at once.
  • I rebel, therefore we are. The Rebel (1951): “What is a rebel? A man who says no”, and who also says yes. The slave who one day judges an order unacceptable: “up to there, yes; beyond, no”. Revolt, like Descartes’s “I think, therefore I am” (lecture 2), is the first self-evident truth, but it draws each person out of their solitude: “I rebel, therefore we are”. That is why not everything goes: the revolt that denies solidarity “loses the name of revolt”, and the rebel “humiliates no one”. Today, laughing at the weakest is not rebelling.
  • What is left? The three questions: what is true, the world does not answer, and Camus stays with the question; what is right, whatever humiliates no one; what we live for, it does not come written: for what you would make your own today, tomorrow and the day after, with your eyes open. The chair, empty, and even so one lives, with joy and with others. Today’s answer: yes, you can be happy. In lecture 7, those who believe the chair will not be emptied. The series’ question, on its own, and the message. And the farewell: Camus left us Sisyphus at the foot of the mountain, master of his days.

For readers outside Spain: the Bachillerato is the last two years of secondary school (ages 16–18); Philosophy is compulsory in the first year for every track, and History of Philosophy in the second. On this sheet, “1st year” and “2nd year” (1.º and 2.º in Spanish) are those two years. Royal Decree 243/2022 sets the minimum curriculum of the Bachillerato for the whole of Spain; the Philosophy Lectures are one of the channel’s series and sit outside it. The video is in Spanish, with English subtitles. On this sheet the quotations are given in our own English translation, made from Camus’s French; where the sheet discusses the published Spanish translations, the Spanish words are given in italics or in square brackets. A comentario de texto, or text commentary, is the classic Spanish classroom exercise of explaining a philosophical passage in writing.

Where it falls in the curriculum

The Lectures sit outside the curriculum: they neither follow the syllabus nor replace it. Camus is not named in the decree, but the lecture falls within it (Spain’s Royal Decree 243/2022, annex II; the decree’s wording, in our translation of the official Spanish text):

Subject and block What the decree says What the lecture adds
Philosophy, 1st year, block A.2 “Philosophy and human existence”. And specific competence 1: “to ask questions about the foundation, value and meaning of reality and human existence”. The question of meaning, “what for?”, and whether one can be happy without a given answer. The absurd as the clash between that question and the silence of the world
Philosophy, 1st year, block B.2 “The philosophical problem of the existence of God. Theism, atheism and agnosticism”. Kierkegaard’s leap towards God and the position of Camus, who does not take it and does not start from the premise that Christian truth is illusory, “but only from this fact, that I have not been able to enter into it”
Philosophy, 1st year, block C.1 “The ethical problem: how we ought to act”. “The possibility of a rational ethics. […] The debate around moral relativism”. Without a given meaning, is anything allowed? Camus: revolt founds solidarity, and the rebel “humiliates no one”. Javier’s test for an “I like it” that makes sense: today, tomorrow and the day after, and without trampling on anyone, alongside Kant’s
History of Philosophy, 2nd year, block C “Existentialism: Martin Heidegger and Jean Paul Sartre”. Camus, who is brought in alongside them and rejected the label (“no, I am not an existentialist”, 1945). The Myth of Sisyphus criticises, precisely, the philosophers it calls existential
History of Philosophy, 2nd year, block B “Rationalism and empiricism: René Descartes and David Hume”. “I rebel, therefore we are”, modelled on the cogito: revolt is, in everyday life, what the “I think” is in the order of thought, “the first self-evident truth”

  • Eight chapters, each with something that moves:
  • the boy’s day, going round in a ring, and the ribbon of the days, without commas, as Camus writes them;
  • a map of the Mediterranean, from Algiers to Minorca;
  • the boy who asks “what for?” and the world, at night, which stays silent;
  • the two doors: one with a phone from which videos keep pouring out; the other with a leap and its light. And, between the two, the crest, the edge where one stays standing, firm;
  • the Munich amphora; the slope where the rock goes up and falls back down, again and again; the gulf, with the water, the sun, the warm stones and the sea; Mercury, who comes down to fetch Sisyphus; and Sisyphus, who walks slowly down to fetch his rock;
  • Sisyphus, first serious and then smiling, and the photo of the ruins of Tipasa;
  • the boy’s afternoon (the guitar, training, reading, cooking) and the three boxes for today, tomorrow and the day after;
  • the slave who raises his hand, and the people who join him from behind (“we are”); the shove, whose “revolt” label falls off;
  • and the series’ chair, which is still empty at the end.
  • The visual thread is the rock: it arrives with Sisyphus, becomes routine, stays with whoever pushes it and comes back in the last question (“how are you going to push your rock?”). And, as throughout the series, the chair and the three cards with its questions.
  • Nine pauses, without warning and without a countdown:
  • Have you ever asked yourself what all the things you do every day are for?
  • If the world could answer just one question for you, which one would you ask it?
  • Who has ever promised you all the answers? And what did they ask of you in return?
  • And you: what is your rock?
  • What fulfils you, even if it leads to no summit?
  • What do you do today that you would also want to do tomorrow and the day after?
  • What would you rebel against? And with whom?
  • The series’ question: if God is dead, what is left?
  • And you: tomorrow, how are you going to push your rock?
  • Each quotation, with its work on screen. Javier reads them; Camus is given no voice. The translations into Spanish are our own and literal, from the French:
  • The Myth of Sisyphus, from Gallimard’s «Les Essais» typesetting, checked against the Idées one and against the translations by Luis Echávarri (Alianza) and Esther Benítez;
  • The Rebel, from the Idées collection, checked against the Pléiade and against Josep Escué’s translation (Debolsillo);
  • Nuptials, from the Folio edition, checked against the Pléiade;
  • Actuelles and the letter to Louis Germain, from their Gallimard editions.

What Javier says in his own words goes in chalk, not as a quotation. Where a quotation is cut, the screen marks it with “[…]”.

  • Vocabulary.
  • Clairvoyance is clarividencia in Spanish, “clairvoyance” on this sheet: seeing clearly what is happening to you, and not lucidez, “lucidity”, as it is often quoted.
  • Il faut imaginer Sisyphe heureux is hay que imaginar a Sísifo feliz, “one must imagine Sisyphus happy”, without the reflexive se; Echávarri and Benítez, in their Spanish translations, write imaginarse.
  • Révolte is rebeldía in Spanish, “revolt” on this sheet, and révolté, rebelde, “rebel”. Echávarri sometimes writes rebelión, “rebellion”.
  • Je me révolte, donc nous sommes is me rebelo, luego somos, “I rebel, therefore we are”. Escué, in his Spanish translation, writes luego existimos, “therefore we exist”; the lecture keeps somos, “we are”, which is literal, and which sounds better as a reply to pienso, luego existo, the Spanish for “I think, therefore I am”.
  • Lassitude is cansancio in Spanish, “weariness” on this sheet (Echávarri writes lasitud, “lassitude”), and un jour seulement is sólo que un día, “only, one day”, not un solo día, “a single day”.
  • Arête, the “ridge” of honesty (arista in Spanish), is the sharp edge of a mountain crest. The lecture’s image is a broad, firm crest, with no drop.
  • L'homme, in Camus’s French, is “man” (el hombre): the human being, men and women.
  • The “yes, you can” is a reading. Camus does not answer the title’s question, which is ours, in those words. What he writes is “one must imagine Sisyphus happy”, and that life “will be lived all the better” for not having a given meaning (see “Going further”). The voice puts it like this: “Camus answers yes: one must imagine Sisyphus happy”.

Using it in class, if you wish

The lecture sets no task. If teachers want to use it, here are four ways, from least to most time:

  • Reading aloud. The passages from the chapter “The Myth of Sisyphus” on the student worksheet, from the condemnation to the end, fit into five minutes. Then a question: why is Camus more interested in the walk down than in the climb up?
  • With 1st- or 2nd-year students, ten minutes: “today, tomorrow and the day after”. Each student writes down, without signing it, something they like doing. A few are read out and put through the lecture’s test: would you want to do it today, tomorrow and the day after? Does it trample on anyone? Could you explain why you like it? Whatever fails on the third day: why does it fail? It is worth saying that the test is Javier’s, not Camus’s, and comparing it with Kant’s.
  • With 1st-year students, fifteen minutes of dialogue. Three questions, with no single answer:
  • Javier distinguishes between having fun and never asking yourself the question. Where is the difference? Can you think of an example of each?
  • Camus does not share the Christian faith, and yet he says he does not start from the premise that it is illusory, “but only from this fact, that I have not been able to enter into it”. Can you not share a belief and still respect it? What changes depending on how you say it?
  • Is rebelling the same as just being contrary? What is the difference between Camus’s rebel and someone who laughs at the weakest?
  • With 2nd-year students, a text commentary. The last paragraph of chapter I of The Rebel, the one with “I rebel, therefore we are” (Idées, pp. 35-36), in Josep Escué’s translation (Debolsillo, in the school library: the work is under copyright; in English, Anthony Bower’s). It serves to link block B of History of Philosophy (Descartes’s cogito) with block C. The lecture serves as an introduction and does not do the commentary for them: it leaves out metaphysical and historical revolt, measure, and the 1952 controversy with Sartre.

Misreadings the lecture takes apart

What is often said What the texts and the sources say
Camus is an existentialist In 1945 he told Les Nouvelles littéraires: “No, I am not an existentialist. Sartre and I are always surprised to see our two names linked together”. And he added that The Myth of Sisyphus “was directed against the so-called existentialist philosophers”. In lecture 1 he was named alongside them; this one corrects that, as “an honest warning”
For Camus, the world is absurd He corrects himself: “I was saying that the world is absurd, and I was going too fast”. The world is not reasonable, “that is all that can be said of it”. The absurd is neither in man nor in the world, but “in their common presence”: in the confrontation between the human call and the silence of the world (MS, pp. 37, 44-45 and 48)
The absurd is a pessimistic conclusion Camus warns in the note that opens the book: the absurd, “taken until now as a conclusion, is considered in this essay as a starting point”
A happy Sisyphus means “resign yourself and put on a brave face” His Sisyphus is “powerless and rebellious” (impuissant et révolté), and he teaches “the higher fidelity that denies the gods and raises the rocks”. From the absurd, Camus draws “my revolt, my freedom and my passion” (MS, pp. 88-89, 164 and 166)
“The lucidity that was to be his torment…” The word is clairvoyance [clarividencia] (MS, p. 164)
Hay que imaginarse a Sísifo feliz, as it is quoted in Spanish The original has no pronoun: il faut imaginer, “one must imagine” [hay que imaginar] (MS, p. 166). Imaginarse, with the reflexive pronoun, is the form in the published Spanish translations: not a serious mistake, but it strays from the text
“I rebel, therefore I exist” [me rebelo, luego existo] It is plural: donc nous sommes, “therefore we are” [luego somos]. That is the idea: revolt draws each person out of their solitude (HR, Idées, pp. 35-36)
The Rebel begins with “What is a rebel?” That is how the first part begins. The book opens with an introduction
Camus’s mother was Spanish, from Majorca She was born in Birkadem, near Algiers. Her family came from Minorca: the Sintès, from Ciutadella, and Camus’s grandmother, Catherine Cardona, from Sant Lluís (Lottman; Todd)
He won the Nobel at 44 He was 43 when it was announced, on 16 October 1957, and 44 when he received it in Stockholm, on 10 December. He is the second-youngest Nobel laureate in Literature, after Kipling
“Everything I know about morality I owe to football” In 1953, in «La belle époque», a piece for his old club, the RUA, he wrote “to sport” and “what I know most surely”, not “everything”. The sentence with football is from 1959, on television, and more modest: “the little morality I know, I learned on football pitches and theatre stages, which will remain my true universities”
“In the midst of hate, an invincible love… and that makes me happy” It is not Camus’s: it is a self-help addition that has been circulating since 2014. What he wrote, in “Return to Tipasa” (1952, in Summer, 1954), is a single sentence: “In the middle of winter, I at last learned that there was in me an invincible summer”
He dedicated his Nobel speech to his teacher In Stockholm he did not name him. He dedicated the book of his speeches, Discours de Suède (1958), “to Mr Louis Germain”, and wrote him the letter that the lecture reads

Going further: what the lecture doesn’t say aloud

  • The question, turned around. In the chapter “Absurd Freedom”, Camus writes: “Previously it was a question of knowing whether life had to have a meaning to be lived. Here it appears, on the contrary, that it will be lived all the better for having no meaning”. And: “To live is to make the absurd live. To make it live is, above all, to look at it” (MS, p. 76). It is the closest Camus comes to answering the title’s question, and what Javier calls “staying with the question, with your eyes open”.
  • The leap, in the book. Camus analyses it in Jaspers, Shestov and Kierkegaard, and then in Husserl and the phenomenologists, a leap of reason. He defines it as “the movement by which a thought denies itself and tends to surpass itself in what makes its negation”, and points out that the name he gives it “does not imply a judgement”. And he writes: “there are several ways of leaping, the essential thing being to leap” (MS, pp. 61-62). Javier takes that sentence to talk about today, as his own reading.
  • Camus before believers. The quotation in the lecture comes from “The Unbeliever and Christians”, fragments of a talk at the Dominican friary of Latour-Maubourg, in Paris, collected in Actuelles (1950). Just before, he says that he does not feel he is in possession “of any absolute truth or of any message”. The date of the talk is disputed: 1948, according to Actuelles; February 1946, according to the critic Pierre-Henri Simon. That is why the voice does not give it. Camus knew Christianity well: his higher-studies dissertation (1936) deals with Plotinus and Saint Augustine.
  • Contempt. “There is no destiny that is not surmounted by contempt” (MS, p. 164). In the same chapter, among Sisyphus’s passions is “his contempt for the gods” (p. 162). Javier reads it as contempt for the punishment, not for people: the gods wanted the rock to crush him, and he does not give them that satisfaction. It is a reading, and the voice says so.
  • The end of the chapter. After the walk down, Camus leaves Sisyphus “at the foot of the mountain”: “one always finds one’s burden again”. Sisyphus “knows himself to be the master of his days”, and his life, “this series of actions without a link”, becomes “his destiny, created by him”. The penultimate sentence is “the struggle itself towards the summits is enough to fill a man’s heart”, and the last, “one must imagine Sisyphus happy” (MS, pp. 165-166). Javier’s farewell, “he left us Sisyphus at the foot of the mountain, master of his days”, is made from those two sentences.
  • Tipasa, with its condition. In Nuptials (1939), the full sentence is: “why should I deny the joy of living, if I know how not to enclose everything in the joy of living? There is no shame in being happy” (NE, p. 18). It is not praise of pleasure, full stop: the joy of living is not everything, but it should not be denied. In The Plague (1947), Camus adds the condition: Rambert, the journalist who wants to leave the city, tells Rieux that “there can be shame in being happy all by oneself”. It is the same idea as the end of the lecture: with joy, and with others.
  • I rebel, therefore we are, in full. The paragraph starts earlier: “In the absurd experience, suffering is individual. From the movement of revolt onwards, it is aware of being collective, it is the adventure of all”. And, before the cogito: “The ill that one man alone was suffering becomes a collective plague”. The word “plague” (peste), four years after the novel, is Camus’s own (HR, Idées, pp. 35-36).
  • Measure. In the last part of the book, “Midday Thought”, Camus writes that “measure is not the opposite of revolt. It is revolt that is measure”, and that the logic of the rebel is “to serve justice so as not to add to the injustice of the [human] condition, to strive for clear language so as not to thicken the universal lie, and to wager, in the face of the pain of men, on happiness” (HR, Idées, pp. 342 and 361, and the end of the book). It is the same “with others and never against them” as in the message.
  • The break with Sartre (1952). The Rebel set him against Sartre. In May 1952, Les Temps modernes, the journal Sartre edited, published a very harsh review by Francis Jeanson. Camus replied with a letter to the editor, dated 30 June, and Sartre answered him in the August issue. Camus’s main reproach was that one cannot talk about revolution and keep silent about the camps of the Soviet Union. For 2nd-year students it is a good contrast with lecture 5.
  • Algeria, the most debated. Camus wrote about the poverty of Kabylia in 1939; in January 1956, in Algiers, he called for a “civil truce”, for both sides to keep the civilian population out of the war; he condemned the torture carried out by the French army and the terrorism of the FLN, and proposed a federal Algeria. On 12 December 1957, at a discussion with students in Stockholm, a young Algerian reproached him for his position. According to Le Monde of 14 December, which he accepted as accurate, he replied: “I have always condemned terror. I must also condemn a terrorism that is exercised blindly, in the streets of Algiers for example, and that one day may strike my mother or my family. I believe in justice, but I shall defend my mother before justice”. He did not say “between justice and my mother, I choose my mother”, as is often repeated. In his letter to Le Monde he added that he felt closer to that young man, whose face “was not that of hatred, but of despair and misfortune”, than to many French people who talked about Algeria without knowing it. Today some read it as meaning that he sided with the French of Algeria, and others as a refusal to justify violence against innocent people: that is interpretation, and it is kept separate from the fact (verificacion-vidas.md, point 7, in Spanish).
  • Spain. His mother’s family was Minorcan, from Ciutadella and from Sant Lluís, although, according to his biographers, he never visited the island. Today Sant Lluís has a Sala Albert Camus and literary meetings that bear his name, the Trobades & Premis Mediterranis Albert Camus. And in 1936, with his theatre group in Algiers, he wrote Révolte dans les Asturies, about the revolution of the Asturian miners in 1934; the mayor of Algiers banned its performance.
  • The goalkeeper. He played in the junior team of the Racing Universitaire d’Alger (RUA). In December 1930, just after turning seventeen, he fell ill with tuberculosis, and gave up playing in goal.
  • Kant, Nietzsche and the test of the days. The “today, tomorrow and the day after” test is Javier’s, not Camus’s. The lecture places it beside the two seen in lecture 3: Kant’s law for everyone and Nietzsche’s eternal return. It is more humble, because it does not ask you to think of everyone or of eternity, only of the days; and that is why it fits into what one does each afternoon.
