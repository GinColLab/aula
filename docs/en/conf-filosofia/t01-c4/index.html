What “one” does

In a nutshell

It is a lecture, not a class: about thirteen minutes for listening and thinking, with no exercises, no countdowns and no questions to take home. Javier speaks from the school assembly hall. It is the fourth in a series of eight, “If God is dead, what is left?”, and it stands on its own.

The question: if no one sits in the chair, who decides what you do? The chair is the series’ image: for centuries God occupied it, and he answered what is true, what is right and what we live for; lecture 3 ended on “if no one dictates to you what has value, dictate it to yourself”. This one looks beneath that sentence: who is that “you”?

The message, what listeners take away for their own lives: if you don’t choose, the “one” chooses, and it is nobody; and often someone puts the words into that nobody’s mouth: ask yourself who, and what they gain. Your life is yours: no one can live it for you; choose it yourself. And choosing is not enough: think, too, from the place of others.

It is Javier’s reading, and he says so. He sets out his thesis in advance (“almost always, you don’t decide”), he marks with “for me” and with the label “what I think” what is his own (that the phone’s recommendation is “the ‘one’ turned into calculation”, that it is often whoever has a megaphone who puts the words into the mouth of the “one”, that choosing is not enough), and he says where he does not follow Heidegger.

Heidegger, only for his philosophy. When introducing him, Javier says once, and without dwelling on it, why bringing him in is hard: “Heidegger was a convinced Nazi”. That is why, he adds as his own opinion, he would deserve to be passed over lightly in the history of philosophy, and he is brought in only for his philosophy and for the value that has been placed on Being and Time, on which Sartre, the philosopher of lecture 5, draws. The lecture does not come back to it. The note “Why ‘a convinced Nazi’”, below, gives the facts with their sources, in case students ask.

Without death. In Being and Time, the path to authenticity goes through being towards death and anxiety (§§ 40 and 46-53). The lecture, designed for sixteen-year-olds, does not go there: it reaches authenticity through § 9, each person’s life is their own, and through the call of conscience (§§ 54-60). It is a partial reading, and it is said here for anyone who wants to complete it.

The journey, in seven chapters:

  • What one wears. A bus today: everyone on their phone, each with their own thing and, underneath, “people like you also liked this”. The map of the series, lecture 3 in one sentence and today’s question. Heidegger, introduced: bringing him in takes an effort, because he was a convinced Nazi, and he is brought in only for his philosophy, above all for Being and Time.
  • The “one”. Being and Time (1927), § 27: on the tram and with the newspaper, “every other is like the other”. The German word man, which in Spanish is se (in English, “one”): we enjoy ourselves as one enjoys, we judge as one judges, and even the rebel withdraws from the masses “as one withdraws”. Who is it? Not this person or that, nor the sum of everyone: it is “the nobody”. And it takes away responsibility: “it always ‘was’ the ‘one’, and yet it can be said that it was ‘no one’”. It is not an insult: Heidegger says it is not “less being”, and it is how we all live almost all the time.
  • What one says. The three ways of living in the “one” (§§ 35-37): idle talk, which is not gossip but talking from hearsay (“the thing is so because one says it”); curiosity, which jumps from one novelty to the next and “is everywhere and nowhere”; and ambiguity, the one-minute video that makes you believe you already know. And Javier’s reading, given as his own: the recommendation is the “one” turned into calculation.
  • Who speaks for the “one”. The “one” is nobody and, even so, it rules: “Inconspicuous and impossible to pin down, the ‘one’ unfolds its true dictatorship” (§ 27). From there on, Javier speaks for himself, and he says so (“that is his; what follows is mine”): what one says does not come from nowhere, and often someone puts the words into that nobody’s mouth: whoever has a megaphone. A century ago, a newspaper; today, also an account with millions of followers, or the money that pays for a message to come up first. Having a megaphone is not bad (Javier has one); what is bad is hiding it: passing off what suits one person alone as what everyone thinks. And there are those who make a living from setting us against each other, because what outrages tends to be shared more: they teach people to despise women, those who come from elsewhere, those who think differently, and they boast of saying out loud what everyone thinks. It closes with Heidegger, who wrote it of the “one”, “One is oneself part of the others and strengthens their power”, and with the listener: every time you repeat it without thinking, you are the megaphone.
  • Authenticity. Javier, to camera: there is something the nobody cannot do for you, live your life. Each person’s life is their own (§ 9): each of us can “choose” ourselves, win ourselves, and also lose ourselves, letting the “one” decide. We are thrown: no one chose to be born, or when, or where. The call of conscience, which, “strictly speaking”, says nothing (§ 56): it only wakes you up. Resoluteness and authenticity (eigentlich: the German word means “one’s own”, what is yours), which is not being odd, or original, or your true self, or the best version of yourself: it is living in the “one” in another way, “a modification of the ‘one’” (§ 27). The same, but chosen.
  • Thinking from the other’s place. Where Javier does not follow him, as his own opinion and without caricature: choosing is not enough. Heidegger wrote that we always exist with others, but his call says nothing, not even whom you are going to hurt, and you can choose the worst with complete resolve: trampling on someone else. What that call lacks had already been written by Kant, from lecture 2, among his maxims for thinking (§ 40 of the Critique of Judgement): the first, to think for oneself; the second, to think in the place of every other person, which is not doing what others do. Every day, the group chat that laughs at someone. Other people exist.
  • What is left? On paper, the three questions have answers; in everyday life, the “one” answers them: what one says, what one does, what one wears. If no one sits in the chair, the nobody sits there. In lecture 5, Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir: not choosing is also choosing. The series’ question, on its own; and the message, which adds the idea of the new chapter: often, someone puts the words into that nobody’s mouth; ask yourself who, and what they gain.

For readers outside Spain: the Bachillerato is the last two years of secondary school (ages 16–18); Philosophy is compulsory in the first year for every track, and History of Philosophy in the second. On this sheet, “1st year” and “2nd year” (1.º and 2.º in Spanish) are those two years. Royal Decree 243/2022 sets the minimum curriculum of the Bachillerato for the whole of Spain; the Philosophy Lectures are one of the channel’s series and sit outside it. The video is in Spanish, with English subtitles. Heidegger’s das Man is el “se” in the Spanish of the video and the “one” on this sheet and in the subtitles. On this sheet the quotations are given in our own English translation, made from Heidegger’s and Kant’s German. A comentario de texto, or text commentary, is the classic Spanish classroom exercise of explaining a philosophical passage in writing.

Where it falls in the curriculum

The Lectures sit outside the curriculum: they neither follow the syllabus nor replace it. But this one falls squarely within it (Spain’s Royal Decree 243/2022, annex II; the decree’s wording, in our translation of the official Spanish text):

Subject and block What the decree says What the lecture adds
History of Philosophy, 2nd year, block C “Existentialism: Martin Heidegger and Jean Paul Sartre”. The Being and Time of everyday life: the “one” (§ 27), idle talk, curiosity and ambiguity (§§ 35-37), being “in each case mine” (§ 9), thrownness, the call of conscience (§ 56) and resoluteness (§ 60), the authentic and the inauthentic, with their texts. It does not go into being towards death or anxiety (because of the audience’s age), nor into the question of being or the later turn: the lecture stays with what answers its question
History of Philosophy, 2nd year, block C “The critical philosophy of Immanuel Kant”. The maxims of § 40 of the Critique of Judgement: to think for oneself, which already came up with sapere aude in lecture 2, and to think in the place of every other person
Philosophy, 1st year, block A “Philosophy and human existence”. “The problem of personal identity”. Who decides what you do? The “one” set against authenticity, and why being authentic is not being original or the best version of yourself
Philosophy, 1st year, block C “The ethical problem: how we ought to act. […] Moral deliberation. The conditions of judgement and of ethical dialogue”. Choosing is not enough: Kant’s second maxim, to think in the place of every other person, as a condition of judgement, and the trap of confusing it with doing what others do

  • Seven chapters, each with something that moves: today’s bus, with a phone in every hand and the recommendation card, which turns into the tram of 1927 with its newspapers; a row of different people making the same gesture; the series’ chair under its spotlight, with a figure made only of chalk dots, faceless, sitting in it: the “one”; the backpack of responsibility that is dropped and dissolves into the dotted figure; the speech bubbles of idle talk; the megaphone that, outside the light, puts the words into the dotted figure’s mouth, and the row that splits in two; winning or losing oneself, with the person who lights up and the dotted chair; the street that is already in motion when you arrive; the call as bell-like waves, and its empty speech bubble; the portraits of Heidegger and Kant; the two maxims, numbered, each number with its own; the group chat; and the three cards of the series.
  • The visual thread is the three cards of lecture 1 and the chair: at the end, beneath each answer that lecture 3 left, the answer of everyday life, in yellow: “what one says”, “what one does”, “what one wears”.
  • Eight pauses, without warning and without a countdown:
  • How many of the things you have done today did you choose yourself?
  • What do you do just because one does it?
  • When was the last time you stayed with something, without jumping to the next thing?
  • That thing you repeat: who did you hear it from? And what do they gain from your saying it?
  • Of what you do every day, what would you go on doing if you chose it yourself?
  • Have you ever hurt someone just because it was what one did? And have you ever kept quiet while others did it?
  • The series’ question: if God is dead, what is left?
  • And you, tomorrow: who is going to decide what you do?
  • Each quotation, with its work on screen. Javier reads them; Heidegger is given no voice. The translations into Spanish are our own and literal: Being and Time, from the German of the Niemeyer edition, checked against the translations by Jorge Eduardo Rivera (Trotta) and José Gaos (FCE); Kant, from the Academy edition. What Javier says in his own words goes in chalk, not as a quotation.
  • Vocabulary. Das Man is called el “se”, the Spanish impersonal “one” (Rivera and Gaos write el uno, “the one”: it is the same word); this sheet says the “one”. Dasein, “the one who is there”, is named once and not translated. Eigentlich is propio, “one’s own”, with the note that it is usually translated as “authentic”, the word this sheet uses.

Using it in class, if you wish

The lecture sets no task. If teachers want to use it, here are four ways, from least to most time:

  • Reading aloud. § 40 of Kant’s Critique of Judgement, the one with the three maxims, fits into five minutes, and its core passage, from beginning to end, is on the student worksheet. After the lecture, students can tell the second maxim apart from “doing what others do”.
  • With 1st- or 2nd-year students, ten minutes: “who is speaking, and what do they gain?”. Together, the class picks a sentence that “one says” these days in class or on social media (without naming anyone in the group) and asks it the two questions from the pause: who did you hear it from? and what do they gain from your repeating it? Then a third: would you say it the same way with your name in front of it? There is no need to reach an answer; it is enough to see that almost no one knows where they got it from.
  • With 1st-year students, fifteen minutes of dialogue. Three questions, with no single answer:
  • Give three examples of something that “one” does in your class or in your group. Who decided it? Would anything happen if you didn’t do it?
  • If living authentically is not being odd or original, how does it show? Can someone live authentically doing the same as everyone else?
  • You can choose with complete resolve, and choose the worst. What is missing in someone who chooses like that? Is Kant’s second maxim enough, or would something more be needed?
  • With 2nd-year students, a text commentary. § 27 of Being and Time, in Rivera’s or Gaos’s translation (in the school library: the work is under copyright; in English, Macquarrie and Robinson’s or Stambaugh’s). The lecture serves as an introduction and does not do the commentary for them: it leaves out the “who” of Dasein in § 25, the “being-with” (Mitsein) of § 26 and what § 27 says about publicness (Öffentlichkeit). To contrast with the lecture’s interpretation, § 9 (each person’s life, “in each case mine”) and § 26, with the two ways of caring for others: the one that takes their care away from them and the one that helps them take charge of it.

Misreadings the lecture takes apart

What is often said What the texts and the sources say
The “one” is the masses, the common people, the herd It is no one in particular, nor the sum of everyone (§ 27, SZ 126). And even whoever withdraws from the masses does so “as one withdraws”
The “one” is “them”: the media, the powerful, the influencers For Heidegger, the who “is not this one and not that one, not oneself and not some and not the sum of all”, and “One is oneself part of the others and strengthens their power” (§ 27, SZ 126). That it is often whoever has a megaphone who puts the words into that nobody’s mouth is Javier’s opinion, and he says it as his own (“often”, not “always”). That is why the chapter does not end on “them”, but on “you are the megaphone”
Living in the “one” is a flaw, being less Heidegger says that inauthenticity is not “less” being nor a lower degree of being (§ 9, SZ 43), and that the term “falling” expresses no negative evaluation (§ 38, SZ 175-176)
Being authentic is “be yourself”, being original Authentic being-oneself “does not rest on an exceptional state of the subject, detached from the ‘one’, but is a […] modification of the ‘one’” (§ 27, SZ 130)
The call of conscience tells you what is right “What does conscience call to the one called? Strictly speaking — nothing” (§ 56, SZ 273). That is why, Javier says, it does not tell you whom you are going to hurt
Heidegger advised “spending more time in cemeteries” It circulates in many videos, “in a 1961 lecture”, with no source. Neither the text nor the lecture has been found

Going further: what the lecture doesn’t say aloud

  • More of § 27. Besides the tram and the newspaper, Heidegger describes “averageness” (Durchschnittlichkeit), which keeps watch over what one may lay claim to and what not, and the “levelling down” of all possibilities of being. And in Rivera’s Spanish translation, here put into English: “The ‘one’ is everywhere, but in such a way that it has always already slipped away from wherever existence presses for a decision” (SZ 127).
  • What the lecture leaves out of Being and Time. Being towards death and anxiety (§§ 40 and 46-53), which in the book lead to authenticity: the lecture, for sixteen-year-olds, gets there through § 9 and the call (ADR-190). And § 60 (SZ 298): “Resolute Dasein can become the ‘conscience’ of the others”, which qualifies Javier’s criticism.
  • “What outrages tends to be shared more”, with its limits. The sentence sums up a tendency measured with large samples: in 2.7 million posts by US media and members of Congress, those that talked about the political rival were shared roughly twice as often as those that talked about one’s own group (Rathje, Van Bavel and van der Linden, PNAS, 2021); and each additional moral and emotional word increased a message’s spread, by 20% in the original study, with 563,312 tweets (Brady et al., PNAS, 2017), and by 13% in the meta-analysis that accompanies its replication, with 4.8 million messages (Brady et al., PNAS Nexus, 2025). It is not a law, and that is why the voice says “tends to”: almost all of it is Twitter and Facebook, US politics and correlation; in that same replication, outrage measured directly was shared more in three of five datasets and less in one; and in the Spanish campaigns from 2015 to 2019, with more than 41 million tweets, messages hostile to the rival party were retweeted little (Martín-Gutiérrez et al., Scientific Reports, 2024). None of these studies looks at teenagers, or at TikTok, Instagram or YouTube. It is a good example for the classroom of how to state a figure without overreaching.
  • The question the new chapter may raise. How did the man who had described “the dictatorship of the ‘one’” end up in the Nazi party? The lecture neither answers it nor raises it: it stays with what answers its question. If it comes up in class, the facts are in the next note, and the question can be left open: describing something well is no vaccine against it.
  • More of Kant. The third maxim, “always to think in agreement with oneself”, and the name of the second: the “enlarged” way of thinking. The core of § 40, from beginning to end, is on the student worksheet.
  • Why “a convinced Nazi”. The lecture says it in one sentence and does not dwell on it, on purpose: it brings Heidegger in only for his philosophy. If students ask, these are the facts. In 1933 he became rector of the University of Freiburg and joined the Nazi party, in which he remained until 1945; as rector, he publicly supported Hitler; in his private notebooks, published in 2014, he left antisemitic sentences; and he never publicly recanted (H. Ott, 1983 and 1984; Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Martin Heidegger”).
