So, what is left?

In a nutshell

It is a lecture, not a class: about twenty minutes for listening and thinking, with no exercises, no countdowns and no questions to take home. Javier speaks from the school assembly hall. It is the eighth and last in a series of eight, “If God is dead, what is left?”, and it stands on its own.

The question: if we no longer believe in the grand stories, what sits in the chair? The chair is the series’ image: for centuries God occupied it, and he answered what is true, what is right and what is wrong, and what we live for; in 1882 Nietzsche said it had been left empty. In lecture 7, with Unamuno, Weil and Zambrano, we saw that believing is not giving up thinking. This one reaches our own day, which many call postmodern, and looks at it through its traits, which listeners “will recognise straight away, because they live them”. It starts from what one hears: “it’s my truth”, “it’s my opinion, full stop”, and at the same time those who are absolutely sure of everything and say it very loudly. In 2016 Oxford chose post-truth as its word of the year, and the following year the Real Academia Española added posverdad to its dictionary. Between the one and the other an idea goes around, which the lecture puts to the test: if no one has the truth, anything goes. “Some say it gladly, and some say it in fear.”

The message, what listeners take away for their own lives: when no one believes a grand story, the now takes the chair: what’s instant, what’s new, what’s disposable, the pleasure that fades and the next challenge. And the now never fills you. No one having the last word doesn’t mean that anything goes: what is worth something is defended with reasons, silences no one and takes time, your attention, for things and for people. What is left? You are, with others. Where each part comes from:

  • incredulity towards the grand narratives, from Lyotard (1979);
  • the five traits, with the names given to them by those who study them: François Hartog’s presentism, Gilles Lipovetsky’s empire of the ephemeral, Zygmunt Bauman’s liquid modernity, the psychologists’ hedonic adaptation and Byung-Chul Han’s achievement society;
  • that the now never fills you, from Javier, with Zambrano’s “insatiable idol” from lecture 7;
  • that it isn’t true that anything goes, from Lyotard (the temptation of the “grand narrative of the decline of grand narratives”), from Vattimo (violence as silencing, and his criterion, silencing no one) and from Javier (there are facts that can be checked, and all that takes time);
  • and other people, from almost all the voices of the series, in the laboratory at the end.

It is Javier’s reading, and he says so. He sets out his thesis in advance with the label “what I think” (“the now takes it, and the now never fills you. And no, it isn’t true that anything goes”) and marks as his own, with “what I think” or “the way I read it”, what is his own:

  • of the grand stories we no longer believe: “when the future goes, what remains is the now”;
  • of the pleasure that fades: “when the next pleasure is always one tap away, it’s never enough”, and the “insatiable idol” of lecture 7, “what always asks for more and never fills you”;
  • of the five traits together: “today, the now takes it. And the now, however fast it runs, never fills you”; and, to camera, halfway through: “This is how I see it: when no one believes a grand story, the now takes the chair. And the now never fills you”;
  • that all this “isn’t a complaint against your time”, written under the label: today “each of us chooses our own life much more”, and “the problem isn’t freedom. It’s the now filling all of it”;
  • of “it’s my truth”: “some things belong to no one: whether water makes things wet, whether a medicine works, whether a photo has been doctored. That isn’t my truth or yours: it can be checked. And whoever tells you that everything is opinion often just wants their own to be beyond discussion”;
  • of weak thought, that it is more costly “because it gives up a shortcut”; and that, “in everyday words, which are mine”, the first principle that silences “is called ‘just because’, ‘because I say so’, ‘because it’s always been done this way’”;
  • of Vattimo’s criterion: “that’s not ‘anything goes’. It’s harder: you have to listen, give reasons and check the facts. And all that takes time, exactly what the now doesn’t give”;
  • of the “(only) opportunity” of nihilism: “if there’s no truth imposed on everyone, no one can silence you in its name”;
  • in the laboratory, each trait against a voice: “Against immediacy, Weil’s attention, which takes time. Against novelty and the pleasure that fades, Nietzsche’s question: would you want to live it again? Against use and throw away, Kant: every person, always as an end. Against the challenge you set yourself alone, Camus’s ‘we are’. And against ‘it’s my truth’ and the ready-made truth, Vattimo: silencing no one”. And: “Almost all of them look towards the same place: other people, and the time you give them. But I’m not the one who gives you the verdict”;
  • in the closing, after what the two authors leave: “truth is sought by talking, and there are facts that can be checked; no one is to be used and thrown away; and the what-for is up to you, together with others”. He gives the message as “an idea”, after “What to think, or whether to believe or not, I’m not going to tell you”.

None of these sentences is put in the mouth of Lyotard, Vattimo or the authors of the traits. What is theirs goes as a quotation, in blue and with their work on screen, or as the name they gave a trait, with their book in the caption. Nor is the thesis that the now takes the chair that of any of the five: they put names to what is happening, and they do not all judge it in the same way (Lipovetsky, for example, also sees a gain, and the lecture says so). And the other way round: the two sentences in the laboratory, “refining sensitivity to differences” (Lyotard) and “silencing no one” (Vattimo), are not quotations but Javier’s words for what they write, and that is why they go in chalk and not in blue.

No party, and with respect for those who believe. The lecture takes no side in any war or for any party. It criticises what the channel always criticises, force against reason, wherever it comes from: the “just because” of the ready-made truth and the “it’s my truth, full stop” that cuts the conversation short. The traits are not told against the listener: “this isn’t a complaint against your time”, and Lyotard “also saw a gain in the postmodern”. Lyotard’s temporary contract is not presented as a denunciation of any employment policy: he himself calls it “equivocal” (équivoque), and the voice says that “he doesn’t say it only as a complaint”. And in the chair, at the end: “For those who believe, their God, sought with eyes open. And, whether you believe or not, don’t let it be taken by what never fills you”.

Two lives, and why bringing them in is hard, very lightly. Lyotard and Vattimo get their life in one sentence when they are introduced and, within it, a short sentence on why they are hard to bring in, as in earlier lectures. Neither is of the order of Heidegger.

  • Lyotard, with the same words as Sartre and Beauvoir in lecture 5, to measure the same signature with the same yardstick: “he signed, like Sartre and Beauvoir, in support of some men on trial for abusing minors”.
  • Vattimo: “in 2014 he repeated old antisemitic hoaxes”. With no words of his in quotation marks. What he said that year about the Gaza war does not go in: it is a stance on a war, and on that the channel takes no side; what weighs is the hoaxes.
  • Both end the same way: “If we bring him in, it’s for his philosophy”, with the label “we bring him in for his philosophy”. And on reaching the second, Javier says: “Heidegger, Sartre, Beauvoir, Lyotard, and now Vattimo. That they are hard to bring here has come up too often in this series. And it makes you think”. A short silence, with no question and no further explanation. This sheet does not explain it either: it is an observation, not a thesis. The details, with their sources, are in “Going further”.
  • Of Hartog, Lipovetsky, Bauman and Han, only their profession is given and, for Bauman and Han, where they were born (Poland and South Korea): they come in to name the traits, not to tell their lives or comment on their works.

What the lecture does not cover. From Lyotard it takes the Introduction and chapters 1 and 14 of The Postmodern Condition (La condition postmoderne) and a 1984 letter. Left out are The Differend and the differend, language games, consensus and his debate with Habermas, the 1982 “Answer”, data banks, Discours, figure, the sublime, his writings on art and on Algeria, and the exhibition Les Immatériaux. From Vattimo, the preface to Il pensiero debole (Weak Thought), a chapter of La fine della modernità (The End of Modernity), a note in Credere di credere (Belief) and a line from Nichilismo ed emancipazione (Nihilism & Emancipation). Left out are his reading of Christianity (the ice-cream parlour of Credere di credere, kenosis, secularisation), truth as agreement, La società trasparente (The Transparent Society) and the media, his hermeneutics from the inside and his reading of Heidegger. Of the traits, one name and one book per author: left out are Lipovetsky’s L’Ère du vide (“The Era of Emptiness”) and Le Bonheur paradoxal (“Paradoxical Happiness”), Bauman’s Liquid Love, Han’s other books and the other names given to our time, such as hypermodernity or late modernity. The series’ design sheet placed Lipovetsky, Bauman and Han among the commonplaces to be left out; they came in when the lecture was rebuilt around the traits, only to name them, each with their book and properly attributed. What the lecture leaves out is in “Going further”.

The journey, in eight chapters:

  • “It’s my truth”. The phone chat (“it’s my truth”, “it’s my opinion, full stop”) and the video of someone who is “absolutely sure”. Oxford, 2016: post-truth, “when facts carry less weight than emotions and personal beliefs”. The Real Academia, the following year: “deliberate distortion of a reality, which manipulates beliefs and emotions in order to influence public opinion and social attitudes”. The idea put to the test: “If no one has the truth, anything goes”. The map of the series, the chair, lecture 7 in one sentence, and “Many people call our time postmodern. We’re going to look at it through its traits”. The question and the thesis. Lyotard, “who made a word famous: postmodern”, and Vattimo, “who put forward weak thought, and you’ll see it isn’t what it seems”; “I’ll tell you what names others give those traits”; and the laboratory at the end: “you’re the one who takes a stand”.
  • We no longer believe the grand stories. Lyotard, with his life in one sentence: Versailles, 1924; his first teaching post, a secondary school in Constantine, “in what was then French Algeria, and there he saw up close what a colony was”; Algerian independence; Nanterre, 1968. And why bringing him in is hard. In 1979, the report requested by the Council of Universities of the Government of Quebec, which that same year comes out as a book, The Postmodern Condition, and “goes around the world”. The postmodern, “simplifying to the extreme”: “incredulity towards metanarratives is taken to be postmodern”, the grand stories “that explained everything and promised a happy ending”, like that of the Enlightenment (“the hero of knowledge works towards a good end, universal peace”) or that of the development of wealth. The 1984 letter: each grand narrative, refuted “by a name or a date”. “Everything proletarian is communist”: Berlin, 1953; Budapest, 1956. “The free play of supply and demand favours general enrichment”: the crises of 1911 and 1929.
  • What remains is now. A grand story told you where you were heading, and for its happy ending “it was worth waiting, holding out and saving”; if no one believes it any more, “why wait?”. Javier: “when the future goes, what remains is the now”. Lyotard already in 1979, at the end of his book: “the temporary contract is in fact supplanting the permanent institution”, in work, in love, in culture, in the family and even in politics, and he doesn’t say it only as a complaint: it suits the system, being flexible and cheap, but they are also “agreements that can be revised”. And knowledge: it “is and will be produced in order to be sold. It ceases to be its own end”. The traits, one by one: immediacy (“We want everything now”), with the 2012 study of 23 million video views, impatience, and Hartog’s presentism, “the present takes up everything”; the need for novelty (“The new is valued because it is new”), Lipovetsky’s empire of the ephemeral, “what used to happen with fashion […] now happens with almost everything”, and its good side: “so much variety has also made us more tolerant”.
  • Use and throw away. Things, people (“some people break up with someone by message, or by not replying”) and information. Bauman’s liquid modernity, “like water, almost nothing keeps its shape for long”, and his warning, in his own words (on screen, in the published Spanish translation): “disposable objects, objects for one-off use; the whole world – including other human beings”. The pleasure that fades as soon as you get it: hedonic adaptation, “we get used to good things, and our happiness tends to go back to where it was”; “this isn’t new, and it isn’t an iron law”. Javier: “one tap away, it’s never enough”, Zambrano’s insatiable idol.
  • Yes, you can. Challenges of every kind, “the viral challenge, the summer body, reaching a thousand followers, the streak of days in a row, being the best at everything”: “No one forces you: you demand it of yourself”. Han, the achievement society: “Before, someone else gave the orders: you must. Now you give them to yourself: you can. And since it looks like freedom, you never stop”. “The exploiter is at the same time the exploited.” And his book, The Burnout Society (Müdigkeitsgesellschaft).
  • The now never fills. The five traits, together, “live off the now”. Nietzsche’s chair, which in lecture 7 would not be emptied: today the now takes it, and the now never fills you. “Careful, though: this isn’t a complaint against your time”: today each of us chooses our own life much more, and Lyotard also saw a gain in the postmodern: postmodern knowledge “is not only the instrument of the powers: it refines our sensitivity to differences”. Javier, to camera, in one sentence.
  • Does anything go? Two easy ways out: “it’s my truth, full stop” and the ready-made truth, “so you don’t have to think”. Of the first, Lyotard was already warning: “one feels the temptation to give credence to the grand narrative of the decline of grand narratives”; “Saying that nothing holds any more is also telling yourself a grand story”. Javier: there are things that “can be checked”. For the second, Vattimo, with his life in one sentence: Turin, 1936; as a young man, “a very militant Catholic”; Gadamer, in Germany; professor in Turin; “in the seventies, all of Italy learned that he was gay, and he went on calling himself a Christian, in his own way”; twice in the European Parliament. And why bringing him in is hard, with what that makes you think. Weak Thought (1983), with Rovatti: “weak doesn’t mean flimsy”, and “an ethics of weakness, not simple, far more costly, less reassuring”. Violence: “silencing any further question by imposing a first principle with authority”, “the only possible philosophical definition of violence”. His yardstick: “silencing no one”. And the nihilism of lecture 1, seen the other way round: “the consummate nihilist […] is the one who has understood that nihilism is his opportunity. And in brackets he adds: the only one”.
  • What is left? The laboratory of ideas: around the chair, each voice of the series with its own words. Diogenes with his lamp, “I’m looking for a man”; Kant, Nietzsche, Beauvoir, Camus and Weil, each with the sentence from their lecture; and Lyotard and Vattimo. Each of today’s traits, against a voice. “So this is where things stand, after Lyotard and Vattimo”: what is true, “no one has the last word any more”; what is right, “whatever silences no one”; what we live for, “no grand story hands you that ready-made any more”. Javier’s own view, labelled separately. In the chair, “for those who believe, their God”, and, whether you believe or not, “don’t let it be taken by what never fills you”. The series’ question, on its own, the message and the last pause: “And for you, what is left?”. The farewell: “Lyotard helped us look at our time, and Vattimo, to silence no one”. And the label: Fin del ciclo, “End of the series”.

For readers outside Spain: the Bachillerato is the last two years of secondary school (ages 16–18); Philosophy is compulsory in the first year for every track, and History of Philosophy in the second. On this sheet, “1st year” and “2nd year” (1.º and 2.º in Spanish) are those two years. Royal Decree 243/2022 sets the minimum curriculum of the Bachillerato for the whole of Spain; the Philosophy Lectures are one of the channel’s series and sit outside it. The video is in Spanish, with English subtitles. Lyotard, Hartog and Lipovetsky wrote in French, Vattimo in Italian and Han in German: on this sheet their words are given in our own English translation, with the original where the sheet discusses a word; the published English translations are listed in “Sources”. Bauman wrote in English, and his words are given as he wrote them. The Real Academia Española (RAE), the Royal Spanish Academy, publishes the standard dictionary of Spanish. Where it falls in the curriculum

The Lectures sit outside the curriculum: they neither follow the syllabus nor replace it. Lyotard and Vattimo are not named in the decree, but what the lecture brings falls in three places (Spain’s Royal Decree 243/2022, annex II; the decree’s wording, in our translation of the official Spanish text):

Subject and block What the decree says What the lecture adds
Philosophy, 1st year, block B.1 “Knowledge: definition, possibility and limits. Theories of truth. Disinformation and the phenomenon of ‘post-truth’”. Two definitions of “post-truth”: Oxford’s (2016), which describes a situation, and the Academy’s (2017), which adds an intention (“deliberate”). “It’s my truth” and the ready-made truth as the two easy ways out. And a distinction of Javier’s to discuss in class: what can be checked (“whether a medicine works, whether a photo has been doctored”) and what is sought by talking
Philosophy, 1st year, block C.1 “The ethical problem: how we ought to act. […] The conditions of ethical judgement and dialogue” and “The debate around moral relativism. The challenge of a universal ethics of minimums”. The question “does anything go?”, with two answers: the temptation of the “grand narrative of the decline of grand narratives” (Lyotard) and violence as “silencing any further question”, with the criterion of silencing no one (Vattimo). The conditions of dialogue, according to Javier: listening, giving reasons, checking the facts, and time. And a minimum that almost all the voices of the series point towards: other people, with Kant (“always at the same time as an end”) against use and throw away
History of Philosophy, 2nd year, block C “The deconstruction of the Western tradition in Friedrich Nietzsche and the postmodern legacy”. The postmodern legacy in its two major names: incredulity towards the grand narratives (Lyotard, 1979) and nihilism as an “(only) opportunity” (Vattimo, 1985), which goes back to Nietzsche’s. And how that era is named today, with the competing names (postmodernity, liquid modernity, hypermodernity) in “Going further”

  • a phone chat, with its bubbles, and a vertical video with “I’m absolutely sure”; “post-truth → posverdad”, with “deliberate” underlined in the Academy’s definition; the orange pill “if no one has the truth, anything goes”, between a smiling face and one that shrinks back; “postmodern” with five empty card slots beneath it, to be filled with the traits; and the chair with a ring of empty slots around it, to be filled at the end;
  • the maps of France and the Mediterranean, with Versailles, Constantine and Nanterre, and that of North America, with Quebec; the cover of La condition postmoderne and its title in four languages; a fat book with an arrow ending in a sun, “a happy ending”; and the sheet of the letter, dated in Baltimore, with two cards that an orange line crosses out on “rise up” and “the crises”;
  • a timeline with a dot, “today”, and an arrow towards the sun, with “waiting”, “holding out” and “saving”; the sun goes out, the arrow is erased backwards and the dot becomes an orange pill, “the now”; five cards (“work”, “love”, “culture”, “family”, “politics”) with their “expires” tag; for immediacy, a video that won’t start and forty little figures that go dark while a clock runs from 2 to 5 seconds; for novelty, the “new” tag that jumps from one garment to another, and then to a phone, a song and a card;

  • a list of challenges that ticks itself off, except the last, “the best at everything”; and two columns: “before: someone else orders you / you must” and “today: you order yourself / you can”;
  • the five cards in an arc, with their arrows towards the pill “the now”, which sits down in the chair; a glass that fills and empties without reaching the top, “never fills”; a path that forks in two, and “the now” growing until it covers “freedom”;
  • two doors, that of “it’s my truth” and that of the ready-made truth; the fat book, with the arrow pointing down, “another grand story”; the wet sleeve, the medicine and the doctored photo, with the magnifying glass of “it can be checked”; the names “Heidegger, Sartre, Beauvoir, Lyotard, Vattimo”, in silence; the pedestal of “THE TRUTH” that cracks; the winding path and the straight arrow of the shortcut; the pills “just because”, “because I say so” and “because it’s always been done this way”, which get crossed out; and the thermometer that goes down, “less violence”;
  • and the ring of the laboratory, which fills voice by voice around the chair; each trait against the keyword of a voice; the arrows of almost all of them towards a row of ordinary people, “other people”, with an hourglass; and, at the end, the pill “the now”, crossed out.
  • The visual thread is the chair, its ring and “the now”. The slots of the ring are drawn in the first chapter and filled in the last. The five card slots of the traits are filled one by one and come together around the chair. And the orange pill “the now” is born from the “today” of a timeline, sits down in the chair, covers freedom, pulses hurriedly when Vattimo asks for time and is crossed out in the last answer. As throughout the series, the chair carries the three cards of its questions, and this time their answers are those of the end. Javier appears on camera to greet, halfway through (after the traits) and to say goodbye.
  • Seven pauses, without warning and without a countdown:
  • When was the last time you changed your mind because someone gave you a reason?
  • What grand story have you been told about how your life has to go?
  • What did you want badly a month ago that today you don’t even look at?
  • What challenge have you set yourself that, deep down, no one asked of you?
  • Who has ever silenced you with a “just because”? And who have you done it to?
  • The series’ question: if God is dead, what is left?
  • And for you, what is left?
  • Each quotation, with its work on screen and with traducción nuestra, “our translation”, in its caption. Javier reads them; the authors are given no voice.
  • Lyotard’s are our own translation from the French: La condition postmoderne from the Minuit edition (1979), checked against the Quebec report (1979), which is digitised, and the 1984 letter from Le postmoderne expliqué aux enfants (Galilée, 1986).
  • Vattimo’s are our own translation from the Italian: the preface to Il pensiero debole (Feltrinelli, 1983; page from the 2010 reprint), La fine della modernità (Garzanti, 1985), Credere di credere (Garzanti, 1996) and Nichilismo ed emancipazione (Garzanti, 2003).
  • Han’s, from the German of Müdigkeitsgesellschaft (2010): «El explotador es al mismo tiempo el explotado», “the exploiter is at the same time the exploited”, matches Herder’s Spanish translation word for word.
  • Bauman’s is the only one shown in a published translation, that of the Fondo de Cultura Económica, and its caption says so (traducción de FCE). The English subtitles give it in his own words.
  • Of Hartog, Lipovetsky and the psychologists, the lecture only gives the name they gave the trait, with their book or article in the caption: their sentences are on the student worksheet.
  • Those of the laboratory are the ones already checked and spoken in their lectures (1 to 7).
  • Lyotard is under copyright until the end of 2068 and Vattimo until the end of 2093, and so are the texts of Rovatti, Hartog, Lipovetsky, Bauman and Han: their sentences are kept short, as quotations for teaching.

What Javier says in his own words goes in chalk, not as a quotation, with dicho con mis palabras, “in my own words”, in the caption: thus the one-line definition of violence (from Credere di credere, with the literal passage underneath, in blue) and the criterion from Nichilismo ed emancipazione (“silencing no one”). Where a quotation is cut, the screen marks it with “[…]”. Oxford’s goes in chalk and with “(summarised)”: it is the idea, not the wording.

  • The data. There is only one, and it is shown: the study of videos that are slow to start (Krishnan and Sitaraman, 2012), with its caption, “23 million views”. The voice gives no loose figures; the board shows it with forty little figures and a clock that runs from 2 to 5 seconds, and seven go dark: 3 × 5.8% × 40 = 6.96. The hedonic adaptation curve is labelled “diagram, not data”.
  • The images. The portraits are free photos, with their origin on screen: Lyotard, Bracha L. Ettinger’s black-and-white photo (around 1995, CC BY-SA 2.0), which is the only free one of him; Vattimo, his 2009 portrait as an MEP, from the European Parliament, which can be used with the source credited. The authors of the traits appear without a photo, only with their name. The places, in free photos: Constantine around 1899 (photochrom from the Library of Congress), the Parliament Building in Quebec (abdallahh, 2010, CC BY 2.0), Turin (nonmisvegliate, 2018, CC0) and Heidelberg University in 1903 (Rijksmuseum, CC0), with the maps from Natural Earth. In the laboratory, Jean-Léon Gérôme’s Diogenes (1860, Walters Art Museum) and the portraits from earlier lectures. The covers are recomposed, with text only. The rest are illustrations, with figures that have no one’s features: the person speaking in the vertical video, seen from behind, is no one in particular.
  • Vocabulary.
  • A metanarrative (métarécit), in Lyotard, is a narrative that gives meaning to all the others: he himself also calls them grands récits, “grand narratives”, and that is how the voice says it. And he writes on tient pour, “is taken to be”, not “I define”: the “I define” comes from the English translation.
  • Temporary contract and permanent institution: what is agreed for a time and can be revised, as against what was meant to last. Lyotard calls this trend équivoque, “equivocal”: two-sided.
  • Presentism: in Hartog, a “regime of historicity”, a way of living time in which the present takes up everything. The word already existed with other meanings.
  • The ephemeral is what lasts only a short time. For Lipovetsky, fashion is no longer an ornament of collective life: it is its “keystone”.
  • Liquid modernity: Bauman calls it modernity, not postmodernity, because he sees it as modernity taken further, in which almost nothing keeps its shape.
  • Hedonic adaptation: getting used to good things. Brickman and Campbell spoke of a hedonic treadmill; “hedonic adaptation” is the name that became widespread later.
  • Achievement society (Leistung, in German, is “achievement” or “performance”): today’s society, for Han, as against Foucault’s “disciplinary society”, that of hospitals, prisons, barracks and factories. Herder’s 2012 Spanish translation says sociedad de rendimiento; the 2024 one, sociedad del rendimiento, as the lecture does; in English the difference does not show. Self-exploitation is demanding so much of yourself that you exploit yourself.
  • Weak thought (pensiero debole): “weak doesn’t mean flimsy”. It is a way of thinking that does not believe it owns a single, stable truth.
  • Consummate nihilist translates nichilista compiuto. And chance, in Vattimo’s Italian, is a borrowing from French: “opportunity”.
  • Peremptoriness (perentorietà), in the definition of violence, is the quality of what imposes itself without allowing discussion.
  • The “it isn’t true that anything goes”, and the “now that never fills you”, are a reading. Neither Lyotard nor Vattimo answers the title’s question, which is ours, in those words. What they write is that the “grand narrative of the decline of grand narratives” is a temptation and that postmodern knowledge “refines our sensitivity to differences” (Lyotard); and that violence is silencing with a first principle, that its reduction is defined by “the sole criterion of not silencing” and that the ethics of weakness is “far more costly” (Vattimo). That the now takes the chair is Javier’s: the authors of the traits name them, but none of them puts it like that. The voice marks it from the beginning: “I’ll tell you up front what I think”.

Using it in class, if you wish

The lecture sets no task. If teachers want to use it, here are four ways, from least to most time. In a classroom there is a bit of everything: it is best that no one has to say what they believe, or talk about their challenges or their relationships, and there is no need to bring into the classroom the war that comes up in Vattimo’s life. What is discussed are the ideas.

  • Reading aloud. The paragraph on the temporary contract, at the end of The Postmodern Condition, which is on the student worksheet and fits into five minutes. Then two questions: why does Lyotard say the trend is “equivocal”? And why does he write that “we should be glad” that it is?
  • With 1st- or 2nd-year students, ten minutes: “one trait, two sides”. In groups, each takes one of the five traits (immediacy, novelty, use and throw away, the pleasure that fades, challenges), finds an example of their own and, afterwards, what is good about that trait. It is worth recalling that the lecture says “this isn’t a complaint against your time”, and that Lipovetsky, one of the authors, sees a gain in his: “it has made us more tolerant”.
  • With 1st-year students, fifteen minutes of dialogue. Three questions, with no single answer:
  • Lyotard writes that, when the grand narratives lose credit, “one feels the temptation to give credence to the grand narrative of the decline of grand narratives”. Why can saying “nothing holds any more” be another grand story?
  • Vattimo defines violence as “silencing every further question with the authoritarian peremptoriness of the first principle”. Can you silence someone without raising your voice?
  • Javier separates what “can be checked” from what is sought by talking. What things fall on one side, which on the other, and which on both at once (a piece of data, and what we decide to do with it)? It is worth saying that the separation is Javier’s, not Vattimo’s or Lyotard’s.
  • With 2nd-year students, a text commentary. The Introduction to The Postmodern Condition (Minuit, pp. 7-9; in English, in Geoff Bennington and Brian Massumi’s translation), from “simplifying to the extreme” to “the paralogy of inventors”, in the copy in the school library: the work is under copyright, and the student worksheet only includes a few lines. It serves for block C (the postmodern legacy) and, together with «Apologia del nichilismo», the chapter of The End of Modernity from which the “consummate nihilist” comes (in English, Jon R. Snyder’s translation), to see what Lyotard and Vattimo do with Nietzsche. The lecture serves as an introduction and does not do the commentary for them: it only quotes two sentences from those pages.

Misreadings the lecture takes apart

What is often said What the texts and the sources say
Postmodern means “anything goes” Neither of them says so. For Lyotard, the postmodern is “incredulity towards metanarratives” (CP, Introduction, p. 7), and he warns of the temptation to “give credence to the grand narrative of the decline of grand narratives” (“Missive”, p. 53). Vattimo measures what is worth something by the reduction of violence, “defined by the sole criterion of not silencing” (Nichilismo ed emancipazione)
Lyotard “defines” the postmodern as incredulity towards metanarratives “Simplifying to the extreme, incredulity towards metanarratives is taken to be ‘postmodern’” (En simplifiant à l’extrême, on tient pour « postmoderne » l’incrédulité à l’égard des métarécits; CP, Introduction, p. 7). It is impersonal and he warns that he is simplifying; the “I define” comes from the English translation. And it was not in the Quebec report: it was written for the book’s Introduction
Lyotard invented the word “postmodern” He himself writes that the word “is in use on the American continent, in the writings of sociologists and critics” (CP, Introduction, p. 7)
The temporary contract is a complaint of Lyotard’s against precarious work He calls it “equivocal”: it suits the system, for its “greater flexibility” and its “lower cost”, but “we should be glad that the trend towards the temporary contract is equivocal”, because it does not belong to the system’s purpose alone (CP, ch. 14, p. 107). That is why the voice says “he doesn’t say it only as a complaint”
The sentence on the temporary contract is in the Introduction It is at the end of chapter 14, the last: it is the book’s penultimate paragraph (CP, p. 107; in the Quebec report, pp. 94-95)
The use and throw away of people is Lyotard’s “temporary contract” They are two ideas by two authors. The temporary contract is Lyotard’s, in 1979; the world seen as “objects for one-off use”, “including other human beings”, is Bauman’s, in 2000 (Liquid Modernity, p. 162; FCE, p. 172)
Hartog coined the word “presentism”, in 2002 The word already existed, in the philosophy of time (only the present exists) and in history (judging the past through today’s eyes). Hartog gives it a meaning of his own: a “regime of historicity” in which the present takes up everything. His book is from 2003. And he is a historian, not a philosopher
Lipovetsky condemns consumer society He is no doom-monger. In L’Empire de l’éphémère (1987; in English, The Empire of Fashion), as his English publisher sums it up, fashion goes hand in hand with the rise of democratic values and “superficiality fosters tolerance among different groups within a society”. In L’Ère du vide (1983) he also sees “personal fulfilment, respect for singularity”. And he warns of the other side: in Le Bonheur paradoxal (2006), according to its cover, private pleasures end in a “wounded” happiness
Bauman: “nothing keeps its shape for long” What he writes is that fluids “do not keep to any shape for long” (Liquid Modernity, p. 2; FCE, p. 8). “We no longer tolerate anything that lasts” is in his book, but it is Paul Valéry’s epigraph; and pressing “delete”, in Liquid Love, is said by a young interviewee he quotes
For Bauman, we treat people as things because we are consumerists, and that is all He puts it down to precariousness: “Precarious economic and social conditions train men and women […] to perceive the world as a container full of disposable objects” (Liquid Modernity, p. 162). The lecture keeps the second half of the sentence
Bauman was a postmodernist He distanced himself: in 2002 he said that “postmodernity” was “a stop-gap choice”, a provisional solution, and that “postmodernity is modernity minus its illusion”. That is why he spoke of liquid modernity
“He exploits himself believing he is fulfilling himself”, from The Burnout Society It is not from that book: the form «uno se explota voluntariamente a sí mismo figurándose que se está realizando», “one voluntarily exploits oneself, imagining that one is fulfilling oneself”, is from La expulsión de lo distinto (The Expulsion of the Other), and Han repeated it at the CCCB in Barcelona (El País, 7/2/2018). In The Burnout Society, self-exploitation “goes hand in hand with a feeling of freedom”, and “The exploiter is at the same time the exploited” (Herder, 2012, p. 32)
“It is very hard to rebel when victim and executioner […] are the same person”, by Han It is from Herder’s back cover, not from Han’s text. And “today everyone is a worker who exploits himself in his own enterprise” is from Psicopolítica (Psychopolitics, 2014)
For Han, “you can” replaces “you must” “Can does not cancel out must. The achievement-subject remains disciplined” (Herder, 2012). What he describes is a shift, “the social unconscious switches from must to can” (p. 27), and the first verb he gives disciplinary society is “may not”, prohibition. That is why the voice says “Now you give them to yourself”: the order remains, from within
La sociedad de la transparencia (Han) is La sociedad transparente (Vattimo) They are two different books: Han’s (Transparenzgesellschaft) is from 2012; Vattimo’s (La società trasparente), from 1989
Hedonic adaptation says we always return to the same level of happiness The review by Diener, Lucas and Scollon (2006) is entitled “Beyond the hedonic treadmill”: each person’s set point “can change under some conditions”, and we do not all adapt in the same way. And the name came later: Brickman and Campbell (1971) spoke of a hedonic treadmill
23 million people abandon a video at 2 seconds They are 23 million views, from 6.7 million viewers. At 2 seconds people begin to abandon, and the 5.8% per second comes from fitting a straight line to the first few seconds: it does not hold for long waits
Il pensiero debole (Weak Thought) is a book by Vattimo It is a collective book, which he edited with Pier Aldo Rovatti, with essays by several philosophers. The most quoted parts (“a metaphor”, “an ethics of weakness”) are from the preface, which both of them sign
Weak thought means thinking little, or giving up “An ethics of weakness, not simple, far more costly, less reassuring” (un’etica della debolezza, non semplice, assai più costosa, meno rassicurante; preface, p. 10)
For Vattimo, nihilism is our “sole opportunity” It is la sua (unica) chance, “his (only) chance”, with the brackets (FM, p. 27). “Sole opportunity” is the English translation’s wording, and the Spanish sola oportunidad most probably comes from retranslating it. And it is in «Apologia del nichilismo», already published in 1981
Diogenes was looking for “an honest man”, with a lantern “He lit a lamp in daylight and went about saying: ‘I am looking for a man’” (Diogenes Laertius, VI 41). Ánthrōpon is a human being, with no “honest”, and lýchnos is an oil lamp
Post-truth, according to Oxford, is “the deliberate distortion of reality” That is the Real Academia’s definition (2017), which adds an intention. Oxford’s (2016) describes a situation, and it is an adjective: “relating to or denoting circumstances in which objective facts are less influential in shaping public opinion than appeals to emotion and personal belief”
Lyotard was born in Vincennes; he organised the 22 March Movement; he was a painter He was born in Versailles (French civil register, BnF and his Who’s Who entry); Vincennes is the university where he taught. The movement was founded by the students, and he joined it. As a child he wanted to be a painter and said he lacked the talent: he wrote about painting
Vattimo, full professor in 1964 In 1964 he was a lecturer in charge of a course in Turin; full professor of Aesthetics at the end of the sixties (1968 or 1969, depending on the source)

Going further: what the lecture doesn’t say aloud

  • The temporary contract, in full. The paragraph goes on like this (CP, ch. 14, p. 107): “The development is certainly equivocal: the temporary contract is favoured by the system because of its greater flexibility, its lower cost and the effervescence of motivations that goes with it, all factors contributing to better operativity. […] We should be glad that the trend towards the temporary contract is equivocal: it does not belong to the system’s purpose alone, but the system tolerates it”. It is preceded by the sentence on consensus, “consensus has become an outdated and suspect value” (p. 106), with the idea of local and revocable agreements; it is followed by the book’s last paragraph, on computerisation. The original’s list is longer than the voice’s: “professional, affective, sexual, cultural, family, international” matters, and political affairs. In the Spanish translation by Cátedra (Mariano Antolín Rato), around p. 118.
  • The report and the book. The report was entitled Rapport sur les problèmes du savoir dans les sociétés industrielles les plus développées (“Report on the problems of knowledge in the most developed industrial societies”), was «fait au Président du Conseil des Universités auprès du Gouvernement du Québec» (“made for the President of the Council of Universities of the Government of Quebec”) and is dated «Paris, Avril 1979». Lyotard signs it as «Professeur sans chaire», a professor of Philosophy without a chair at Paris VIII (Vincennes), and writes «post-moderne», with a hyphen. It opens with an «Avertissement», a foreword (“its author is not exactly an expert: he is a philosopher”). The Council was the body that Quebec’s ministers of education and of culture consulted. What is on record is the date of the report, not that of the commission. The Minuit book adds the Introduction, with the famous definition and the note on the word: “The word is in use on the American continent, in the writings of sociologists and critics. It designates the state of culture after the transformations that have affected the rules of the games of science, literature and the arts since the end of the 19th century”.
  • The four grand narratives. The Introduction names four: “the dialectics of Spirit, the hermeneutics of meaning, the emancipation of the rational or working subject, the development of wealth”. Further on he gives them names: Hegel, the Enlightenment and Marx, capitalism. Chapter 9 reduces them to two, the speculative one and that of emancipation, and in chapter 10 (p. 63) Lyotard writes: “The grand narrative has lost its credibility” (sa crédibilité, not “all credibility”).
  • Sensitivity to differences, in context. The sentence Javier reads in “The now never fills” closes a debate with Habermas (CP, Introduction, pp. 8-9): “Consensus obtained through discussion, as Habermas thinks? It does violence to the heterogeneity of language games. And invention always takes place in dissent. Postmodern knowledge is not only the instrument of the powers. It refines our sensitivity to differences and strengthens our capacity to bear the incommensurable. It does not itself find its reason in the agreement (homologie) of experts, but in the paralogy of inventors”. Incommensurable is what cannot be measured with the same yardstick; paralogy, the move that breaks the accepted rules and opens up others (it is not “paralogism”).
  • The 1984 letter, a little more. Le postmoderne expliqué aux enfants (1986) brings together ten texts from 1982 to 1985 in the form of letters, each with addressee, place and date; the «Missive» is addressed to Mathias Kahn and appeared earlier in Critique (no. 456, May 1985), as «Histoire universelle et différences culturelles». Its list is longer than in the lecture: it has a line before, which the lecture does not say; another, “May 1968”; two more dates in the communist one, Czechoslovakia 1968 and Poland 1980, and, in the market one, “the crisis of 1974-1979”. Shortening a list without changing what each line says is faithful.
  • Three names for our time. Lyotard, in 1979, called it postmodernity. Bauman, in 2000, preferred “liquid modernity”, and in a 2002 interview he said he had distanced himself from “postmodernity”, which was “a stop-gap choice”, and that “postmodernity is modernity minus its illusion”. Lipovetsky, in 2004 (Les Temps hypermodernes, with Sébastien Charles), spoke of hypermodernity. They are not synonyms: “postmodern” suggests that modernity is over, and Bauman and Lipovetsky say it is modernity taken further. There are more names: “late” or “reflexive” modernity (Giddens, Beck), “supermodernity” (Augé).
  • Hartog and presentism. François Hartog is a historian, a specialist in ancient Greece and in historiography, emeritus director of studies at the EHESS. In Régimes d’historicité. Présentisme et expériences du temps (Seuil, 2003) he calls a way of living time a “regime of historicity”, and ours presentism, in which the present takes up everything; he places the change in the last decades of the 20th century, after 1989. Of that present he writes (p. 217) that it is “at once everything (there is only the present) and almost nothing (the tyranny of the immediate)”, and “a present that we have made our only horizon”.
  • Lipovetsky, in three books. Gilles Lipovetsky is French, a philosopher by training (he taught philosophy in Grenoble) and a sociologist by what he studies; he himself says: “I don’t know whether I am a philosopher, a sociologist or an anthropologist”. In L’Ère du vide (1983) he speaks of “a second individualist revolution” and of the “process of personalisation”, with its hard side, narcissism, when authoritarian capitalism “gives way to a hedonistic and permissive capitalism”; but also of what is gained: “personal fulfilment, respect for subjective singularity”. In L’Empire de l’éphémère (1987), fashion “is no longer an aesthetic ornament, a decorative accessory of collective life: it is its keystone”. And in Le Bonheur paradoxal (2006), the society of hyperconsumption, whose private pleasures, says the book’s cover, “end in a wounded happiness”.
  • Bauman, the liquid and precariousness. Zygmunt Bauman, a sociologist, was born in Poland, in Poznań, in 1925. In 1968, the communist regime’s antisemitic campaign drove him out of the University of Warsaw and he had to leave the country; from 1971 he lived in England, in Leeds, where he taught sociology, and he held British nationality. In 2010 the Princess of Asturias Foundation gave him, together with Alain Touraine, its award for Communication and Humanities. The image of the liquid opens Liquid Modernity (p. 2): fluids “do not keep to any shape for long and are constantly ready (and prone) to change it” (FCE, p. 8). And use and throw away, in the chapter “Work”, is tied to insecurity: it is “precarious economic and social conditions” that teach us to see the world that way (p. 162; FCE, p. 172). In Liquid Love (2003) he calls one kind of relationship “disposable”, the one sought for the moment, not every couple.
  • Han, from Foucault to “Yes, we can”. Byung-Chul Han was born in Seoul in 1959. As a young man he went to Germany to study metallurgy, switched to philosophy (Freiburg and Munich; a thesis on Heidegger in 1994) and writes in German. He taught in Basel, in Karlsruhe and at the Berlin University of the Arts, and in 2025 he received the Princess of Asturias Award for Communication and Humanities. In Müdigkeitsgesellschaft (the pages are those of Herder’s Spanish translation, 2012, by Arantzazu Saratxaga Arregi): “Foucault’s disciplinary society, made up of hospitals, asylums, prisons, barracks and factories, no longer corresponds to the society of today” (p. 25). Disciplinary society is characterised by “may not”, prohibition, and by “must”; today’s, by “the positive modal verb can (können) without limits. Its affirmative and collective plural, ‘Yes, we can’, expresses precisely its character of positivity” (pp. 26-27). Han does not name Obama: he uses it as the slogan of the whole society. And the shift: “the social unconscious switches from must to can” (p. 27), although “can does not cancel out must”. That is why “The achievement-subject is free from any external authority that forces it to work or even exploits it. It is master and sovereign of itself” (p. 31), and at the same time “The exploiter is at the same time the exploited. Victim and executioner can no longer be told apart” (p. 32).
  • Hedonic adaptation, with its dates. Philip Brickman and Donald T. Campbell proposed the “hedonic treadmill” in “Hedonic Relativism and Planning the Good Society” (1971). The classic review is “Hedonic Adaptation”, by Shane Frederick and George Loewenstein (1999), and the one that corrects the idea, “Beyond the hedonic treadmill”, by Ed Diener, Richard E. Lucas and Christie N. Scollon (2006). According to treadmill theory, good and bad events affect happiness only “temporarily”; the review makes five corrections: the set point is not neutral, each person has their own, its components move separately, it can change under some conditions, and people adapt differently. A 2012 meta-analysis (Luhmann, Hofmann, Eid and Lucas), with data from 188 publications and 65,911 people, shows that adaptation varies a great deal depending on what happens to us.
  • The video study, with its limits. S. Shunmuga Krishnan and Ramesh K. Sitaraman analysed, with data from Akamai’s network, more than 23 million views from 6.7 million viewers, 216 million minutes of 102,000 videos, over 10 days. Abandonment is almost zero in the first two seconds and then rises; the 5.8% per second comes from fitting a straight line to that first stretch. People abandon short videos sooner than long ones, and sooner with a better connection. It is a study with a huge sample, presented at the IMC conference in 2012 and published in a journal in 2013.
  • Water that wets. Javier’s first example in “Does anything go?”, “whether water makes things wet”, recalls Maurizio Ferraris’s, a student of Vattimo’s and a defender of a “new realism”, in la Repubblica (8/8/2011): “water wets and fire burns, whether I know it or not, independently of languages and categories. At some point there is something that resists us”. Ferraris was writing against the use of “there are no facts, only interpretations” as an excuse “to say and do what one wanted”. This version of the lecture does not bring him in; to expand on Javier’s “it can be checked”, he is the natural reading.
  • What Vattimo meant (1996). “For me, the expression […] means not so much, or not mainly, the idea of a way of thinking more aware of its limits, which abandons the claims of the great global metaphysical visions, and so on, but above all a theory of weakening as the constitutive character of being in the age of the end of metaphysics” (Credere di credere, pp. 24-25).
  • The 1983 preface, a little more. “‘Weak thought’ is therefore, no doubt, a metaphor, and in a certain way a paradox. In any case, it will not be able to become the label of any new philosophy” (p. 10). And it begins like this: “One begins, perhaps, with a loss or, if one wants to put it that way, with a renunciation”.
  • Violence and its note. The definition the lecture reads is in note 15 of Credere di credere (p. 63), which comments on “the violence implicit in every ultimacy, in every first principle that would silence any further questioning”: “Silencing every further question with the authoritarian peremptoriness of the first principle also seems to me the only possible philosophical definition of violence”. The “seems to me” is his, and the voice picks it up with “for him”.
  • The reduction of violence, assumed and not proved. Vattimo does not present it as a proof: “The guiding thread of the dissolution of principles and of the reduction of violence is not ‘demonstrated’, but assumed as an interpretation” (Addio alla verità, p. 104). On the same page: without an ultimate foundation, “all possible legitimation is taken away from the violent abuse of the other”. The voice says that “progress is measured by how much violence is reduced, even in the law”: the criterion is in the part of Nichilismo ed emancipazione devoted to law, and it lacks the nuance that he assumes it rather than proves it.
  • Post-truth, with its dates. Oxford announced post-truth in November 2016, as an adjective, and places its first use in this sense in an essay by Steve Tesich in The Nation, in 1992. The Real Academia added posverdad in the update of 20 December 2017, which “will be considered electronic version 23.1”, among other new words: “aporofobia, chakra, hummus, posverdad, postureo or vallenato”.
  • Lyotard, his life in more detail. He was born in Versailles on 10 August 1924. He studied philosophy and letters at the Sorbonne, where he became friends with Gilles Deleuze, François Châtelet and Michel Butor. As a child he wanted to be a Dominican monk, a painter, a historian or a novelist: being a painter, he said, was “a hopeless wish because of an unfortunate lack of talent”. In 1950 he passed the competitive examination for philosophy teachers, and his first post was the boys’ lycée in Constantine (1950-1952), in French Algeria. In 1989 he wrote that the school there was the place where the colonial contradiction reached boiling point, and his biographer David Macey sums it up as his political awakening. In Constantine he met the historian Pierre Souyri, who brought him into Socialisme ou Barbarie, a Marxist group that criticised the USSR, which he joined in 1954: he was its voice on the Algerian war, under the pseudonym “F. Laborde” in his first articles, and he defended independence without trusting those who were going to rule afterwards. He taught at the military lycée of La Flèche (1952-1959), at the Sorbonne (1959-1966) and at Nanterre (1966-1968), where he joined the 22 March Movement. From the early seventies he was a professor at the experimental university of Vincennes (Paris VIII), without a chair until the end; emeritus in 1987. With Thierry Chaput, he curated Les Immatériaux at the Centre Pompidou (1985). He wrote a great deal about painting.
  • Vattimo, his life in more detail. He was born in Turin on 4 January 1936. As a child, he himself recounts at the opening of Credere di credere, he got up early “to go to mass, before school”, and at the liceo he was a very active militant of Catholic Action, with his friend Umberto Eco. He graduated in Turin in 1959 under Luigi Pareyson, with a thesis on Aristotle, and with a Humboldt scholarship he studied in Heidelberg with Hans-Georg Gadamer and Karl Löwith; he translated Gadamer’s Truth and Method into Italian. At the University of Turin he taught from 1964, was full professor of Aesthetics at the end of the sixties and full professor of Theoretical Philosophy from 1982, until his retirement in 2008. In the mid-seventies, when his name appeared on an electoral list of the first Italian gay movement, all of Italy learned that he was gay: it was not entirely his decision, and the year (1975 or 1976) is not clear. He was a member of the European Parliament from 1999 to 2004 and from 2009 to 2014. In 2002 he received the Hannah Arendt Prize for Political Thought in Bremen. In 2006 he told his life in Non essere Dio, “an autobiography for four hands” with the journalist Piergiorgio Paterlini.
  • Why bringing Lyotard in is hard, with its sources. In January 1977 he was one of the 69 signatories of a petition published in Le Monde (26/1/1977, «À propos d’un procès») in support of three men tried in Versailles for non-violent sexual abuse of minors under fifteen; the boys were 12 to 15 years old. It is the same petition that Sartre and Beauvoir signed (lecture 5). In May 1977 he also signed an open letter, with some eighty other people, asking the commission revising the French Penal Code for those cases to stop being crimes and become délits, lesser offences. There is no record of him retracting or speaking of it again. There is no accusation about his private life (searches of 20/11/2026, in French, English and Spanish). He signed together with a large part of the Paris intelligentsia: that is context, not an excuse, and with Sartre and Beauvoir it was not used as an excuse either. The other things he is sometimes reproached for are ideas: his revolutionary Marxism and his critical support for Algerian independence.
  • Why bringing Vattimo in is hard, with its sources. In 2008 and in 2014 he publicly repeated old antisemitic hoaxes, which are told here in our own words.
  • In 2008, in the controversy over the Turin Book Fair, he said, as a provocation, that he had reappraised the Protocols of the Elders of Zion, a well-known antisemitic forgery (report of the Observatory of anti-Jewish prejudice of the CDEC Foundation, Milan).
  • In January 2014 he came back to the Protocols, playing down the fact that they were invented, and repeated the old antisemitic cliché of the bankers who run the Federal Reserve (Corriere della Sera, 17/1/2014, according to ANSA and the CDEC). The Israeli ambassador to Italy and the president of the Union of Italian Jewish Communities accused him of antisemitism.
  • Fact, accusation and interpretation, kept apart: what he said is checked in the primary source or in two; the accusation of antisemitism comes from the Italian Jewish community and the ambassador; he said he was “anti-Zionist, not antisemitic”. What is not interpretation is that the Protocols are a forgery and the bankers story an antisemitic cliché. It is not of the order of Heidegger: he held no post in any regime, nor did anything of the kind, and his books (weak thought, Credere di credere) say nothing of this. What weighs is the hoaxes, which clash head-on with his own philosophy, that of reducing violence and silencing no one.
  • What he said in 2009 and in 2014 about the Gaza war is not told: it is a stance on a war, and on that the channel takes no side.
