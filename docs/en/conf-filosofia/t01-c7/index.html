The chair that won't be emptied

In a nutshell

It is a lecture, not a class: about twenty minutes for listening and thinking, with no exercises, no countdowns and no questions to take home. Javier speaks from the school assembly hall. It is the seventh in a series of eight, “If God is dead, what is left?”, and it stands on its own.

The question: can you believe without giving up thinking? The chair is the series’ image: for centuries God occupied it, and he answered what is true, what is right and what we live for, and Nietzsche said it had been left empty. In lecture 6, with Camus, we saw that, even if no one sits in it, one can live with joy and with others; and Camus, with respect, said of Christian truth that he had not been able to enter into it. This one gives the floor to those who did enter, or who stayed at the door, and puts to the test an idea that goes around in two versions: that believing is for people who don’t think, or that thinking too much is a danger to faith.

The message, what listeners take away for their own lives: believing is not giving up thinking: the faith worth having doubts and seeks the truth. Whether you believe or not, your chair does not stay empty: look at what you seat in it, and let it not be an idol. And give your attention to the person in front of you: it is the rarest and most generous thing you have.

It is Javier’s reading, and he says so. He sets out his thesis in advance with the label “what I think” (“yes, you can. I am not asking you to believe or to stop believing”) and marks as his own, with “what I think” or “this is how I read it”, what is his own:

  • that the three, because they do not speak on behalf of any Church, “also speak to those who do not believe”;
  • of Unamuno, that “desire, on its own, is not enough for him. That is why he fights”;
  • of Weil, that if attention is generosity, “truly looking at someone is giving them a gift”; and that he does not judge what happened to her: “I am reading you what she wrote”;
  • that to see what is sacred in the other “you don’t need to believe”, that those who believe give it a foundation, and that here believers and non-believers can go together, “towards the one who suffers, with the same question”;
  • of Zambrano, that poetic reason “is not giving up thinking, it is thinking in another way”, and that “we want something that fills everything, and we want it now”;
  • the present-day part: what occupies the chair “often” (followers, money, a perfect body, someone who promises to have everything figured out), which “are not bad in themselves”, and that for those who believe it is a false god and for those who don’t, “something that does not deserve the chair, and occupies it”;
  • that waiting is not giving up thinking, that “the idol demands everything, and right now”, and that what deserves the chair, “for them”, is not grabbed: it is received;
  • and the answer at the end: “the faith worth having is the one that thinks, doubts and seeks the truth”. He gives the message as “an idea”, not as his answer.

None of these sentences is put in the mouth of Unamuno, Weil or Zambrano. What is theirs goes as a quotation, with their work on screen. And the other way round: the “whether you believe or not” is not Javier’s, but Weil’s, who writes that the effort of attention bears fruit “even outside any explicit religious belief”. The only thing Javier puts in his own words is “other people exist”, and he says it for what it is, Weil’s idea: she writes that loving one’s neighbour is “knowing that the afflicted person exists”.

Faith, with respect, and asking for nothing. The lecture asks no one to believe or to stop believing, and says so out loud: “I am asking you to listen to those who believe with the same attention as to Nietzsche or Camus”. It is the series’ answer from faith, with its best argument, and Javier gives no verdict on God. He points out that “there are answers from faith in many traditions” and that today we hear three from the Christian tradition, the same faith Nietzsche was talking about. The objection that believing is consoling yourself he raises himself (“and it is a fair one”) and answers with the texts. What he says as his own holds for those who believe and for those who do not. No mockery and no proselytising: in lecture 8, it is the listener who takes a stand.

Three lives, in one sentence each. Unlike Heidegger, or Sartre and Beauvoir, in other lectures of the series, none of the three calls for a separate “why bringing them in is hard” sentence: what there is is of another kind (in Unamuno, brief political support, corrected at his own cost; in Weil, written ideas; in Zambrano, a youthful signature she herself distanced herself from), and giving them a sentence about “the bad side” in the same format would create a false symmetry, as with Camus (verificacion-vidas.md, “Para decidir”, in Spanish). Unamuno’s 1936 goes in as a fact, within his sentence: at first he supported the military rebels, and in October he stood up to them in public; as he himself told it, he said that winning is not convincing (vencer no es convencer). Telling only that would repeat the myth of the spotless hero; and it was some twelve weeks, not twelve years. What is debated about the three are ideas, and that is in “Going further”.

What the lecture does not cover. From The Tragic Sense of Life (Del sentimiento trágico de la vida) it takes what it says about faith, doubt and reason, and leaves out the rest of the book. From Weil, four writings from 1942 and 1943; left out are L'Enracinement (The Need for Roots), what she wrote about the factory, and her notebooks. From Zambrano, the Introduction to El hombre y lo divino (“Man and the Divine”), the opening of one of its chapters and the first page of Claros del bosque (“Forest Clearings”).

The journey, in nine chapters:

  • Is believing for people who don’t think? In 1882 Nietzsche let out his cry, and years later (The Gay Science, § 343, 1887) he explained that faith in the Christian God was no longer believable. Almost a century and a half later, three out of four people in the world say they have a religion (Pew, with 2020 data). In Spain, in autumn 2026, four in ten adults describe themselves as agnostic, indifferent or atheist, and more than half among those aged 18 to 24 (CIS, study 3584): “in your class, most likely, there is a bit of everything: those who believe, those who don’t and those who aren’t sure”. The idea put to the test, in its two versions. The map of the series, lecture 6 in one sentence and Camus’s quotation: “I shall never start from the principle that Christian truth is illusory, but only from this fact, that I have not been able to enter into it”. The question, the thesis and the three on the edge: two books by Unamuno on the Index (the Catholic Church’s list of forbidden books), Weil at the threshold of the Church, Zambrano thinking about the divine in her own way.
  • My religion is to struggle. Unamuno, with his life in one sentence: professor of Greek and rector of the University of Salamanca, exiled to Fuerteventura by the dictatorship of Primo de Rivera (1923–1930), which he criticised, and his 1936. In 1907, from Chile, he is asked about his religion, and he answers in a newspaper: “My religion is to seek truth in life and life in truth […]; my religion is to struggle unceasingly and untiringly with the mystery; my religion is to struggle with God from the break of dawn until nightfall” (Mi religión es buscar la verdad en la vida y la vida en la verdad […]; mi religión es luchar incesante e incansablemente con el misterio; mi religión es luchar con Dios desde el romper del alba hasta el caer de la noche), and “I want to fight my fight without caring about victory” (quiero pelear mi pelea sin cuidarme de la victoria). Seventeen years later, in exile in Paris, he writes The Agony of Christianity (La agonía del cristianismo): “Agony, then, is struggle” (La agonía es, pues, lucha), in the sense of the Greek agón. Faith is not dying out: it is fighting. And it lives on doubt: “The way of living […] of faith is to doubt” (El modo de vivir […] de la fe, es dudar); dudar, to doubt, has the root of duo, two, and he joins it to duellum, struggle. About himself: “That [contradiction] of my heart, which says yes, and my head, which says no!” (¡La de mi corazón, que dice sí, y mi cabeza, que dice no!).
  • Wanting there to be a God. For Unamuno, faith does not begin with a proof: “To believe in God is, in the first instance, […] to want there to be a God, to be unable to live without Him” (Creer en Dios es, en primera instancia, […] querer que haya Dios, no poder vivir sin Él), and it is also living as if God existed. The objection: isn’t that consoling yourself? Unamuno does not trust a calm faith: those who believe “without uncertainty, without doubt, […] believe only in the idea God” (sin incertidumbre, sin duda, […] no creen sino en la idea Dios). “Reason and faith are two enemies that cannot sustain themselves without each other. […] But join together in struggle” (Razón y fe son dos enemigos que no pueden sostenerse el uno sin el otro. […] Pero asociarse en lucha). Kant, in lecture 2, gave each its own ground; Unamuno sits them down at the same table, to fight.
  • Attention. Simone Weil, with her life in one sentence: from a Jewish family that did not practise, a philosophy teacher in provincial secondary schools (the first time, in Le Puy, in 1931, at twenty-two), a year as a factory worker, in 1936 in Spain with the anarchist militias, and close to the Christian faith, but at the threshold of the Church. In 1942 she writes for some students that having no gift at all for geometry does not prevent a problem from teaching you to pay attention, because “never, in any case, is an effort of true attention lost”. She writes it for believers, and for her “prayer is made of attention”, but she opens it out: “even outside any explicit religious belief”. Today attention is what is most fought over, “one video after another, one notification after another”; and Weil, in a letter: “Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity”.
  • Truth first. In her reasoning, the problem of God had no solution. Reciting with all her attention an English poem from almost four centuries ago, Love: “Christ himself came down and took me”. She had not foreseen “a real contact, person to person”, and she warns: “Neither the senses nor the imagination had any part in it”. She resisted with her intelligence, “out of pure concern for the truth”: “Christ likes us to prefer the truth to him, because before being Christ he is the truth. If we turn away from him to go towards the truth, we shall not go far before falling into his arms”. For that same truth she stayed at the threshold, “at the intersection of Christianity and of everything that is not it”, without separating herself from those who do not believe. Javier, to camera: “She believed with her eyes open”.
  • The sacred in every human being. Unamuno and Weil have already answered: you can believe while thinking. The image from lecture 1 comes back, the building of our ideas about dignity and its foundation, faith: if the foundation cracks, what happens to the building? One reason from those who believe, Weil’s, in London, in 1943: at the bottom of the heart of every human being there is something that “invincibly expects good and not evil to be done to it”, and that is the sacred. “You have it, and so does the person you can’t stand”. The Grail and its question: “The fullness of love of one’s neighbour is simply being able to ask him: ‘What is your torment?’”. It is knowing that the afflicted person exists, “as a human being, exactly like us”: other people exist.
  • Unable to live without gods. María Zambrano, with her life in one sentence: the daughter of two schoolteachers, she grew up in Segovia, where her father was a close friend of the poet Antonio Machado; a student of Ortega y Gasset; she defended the Republic, lived forty-five years in exile and, once back, in 1988, was the first woman to win the Cervantes Prize, the highest award for literature in Spanish. Her poetic reason (1937): “more than ever, the exercise of reason and of poetic reason is needed” (es preciso más que nunca, el ejercicio de la razón y de la razón poética), a reason that sometimes sees things whole, all at once, and that she also called “reason of love” (razón de amor). In El hombre y lo divino (1955): “It is only a very short time since man began telling his history, examining his present and planning his future without counting on the gods” (Hace muy poco tiempo que el hombre cuenta su historia, examina su presente y proyecta su futuro sin contar con los dioses), and even so, “the tragedy of the human: being unable to live without gods” (la tragedia de lo humano: no poder vivir sin dioses), which are “a reality different from and higher than the human” (una realidad distinta y superior a lo humano). There are things that, “even when denied, leave our relationship with them intact” (aun negadas, dejan intacta nuestra relación con ellas), and the relationship “changes sign and grows more intense” (cambia de signo y se intensifica): it is the chair that won’t be emptied. If the place is emptied, it is taken by history turned into a god, “this strange divinity, human and divine at once” (esta divinidad extraña, humana y divina a la vez), and the divine ends up as an “insatiable idol” (ídolo insaciable). Today, as a way of behaving: what “always demands more and never fills”.
  • Not to be sought. Claros del bosque (1977): “One must not look for it. One must not look” (No hay que buscarlo. No hay que buscar). And Weil, in the text for students: “The most precious goods must not be sought, but waited for”; whoever sets out to seek them finds “false goods”. Waiting is not giving up thinking, just as it was not in the geometry problem: “The idol demands everything, and right now. Attention waits”.
  • What is left? The three questions: what is true, “for those who believe, there is someone behind it, and seeking the truth, even while doubting, leads towards him”; what is right, “whatever respects what is sacred in every human being”; what we live for, “giving our attention”. In the chair, “for those who believe, God, sought with doubts and with eyes open”. What each of them leaves, and a lesson for everyone: “if the chair is not occupied by what deserves it, it is occupied by an idol”. In lecture 8, with Lyotard and Vattimo, “the stance is yours”. The series’ question, on its own, the message and the farewell: “Unamuno fought, Weil paid attention and Zambrano waited in the clearing. None of them gave up thinking”.

For readers outside Spain: the Bachillerato is the last two years of secondary school (ages 16–18); Philosophy is compulsory in the first year for every track, and History of Philosophy in the second. On this sheet, “1st year” and “2nd year” (1.º and 2.º in Spanish) are those two years. Royal Decree 243/2022 sets the minimum curriculum of the Bachillerato for the whole of Spain; the Philosophy Lectures are one of the channel’s series and sit outside it. The video is in Spanish, with English subtitles. Unamuno and Zambrano wrote in Spanish: on this sheet their words are given in our own English translation, with the Spanish original in italics next to them where a quotation first appears or where its wording matters. Zambrano’s books have not been translated into English, so their titles are given in Spanish. Weil’s quotations are given in our own English translation, made from her French, with the French where the sheet discusses a word; Nietzsche’s, Kant’s and Camus’s, as in the earlier lectures of the series. The CIS (Centro de Investigaciones Sociológicas) is Spain’s public sociological research centre. A comentario de texto, or text commentary, is the classic Spanish classroom exercise of explaining a philosophical passage in writing.

Where it falls in the curriculum

The Lectures sit outside the curriculum: they neither follow the syllabus nor replace it. Unamuno and Weil are not named in the decree; Zambrano is (Spain’s Royal Decree 243/2022, annex II; the decree’s wording, in our translation of the official Spanish text):

Subject and block What the decree says What the lecture adds
Philosophy, 1st year, block B.1 “Other ways of knowing: the problem of metaphysical knowledge; religious beliefs; poetic reason; common knowledge”. Religious beliefs thought through from within, by three thinkers of the Christian tradition who did not speak on behalf of any Church. And Zambrano’s poetic reason, which does not replace the other kind: “more than ever, the exercise of reason and of poetic reason is needed”
Philosophy, 1st year, block B.2 “The philosophical problem of the existence of God. Theism, atheism and agnosticism”. Not a proof of God, but how one believes without one: for Unamuno, believing begins with “wanting there to be a God”; for Weil, in her reasoning the problem of God had no solution. And today’s figures, in the world (Pew) and in Spain (CIS)
History of Philosophy, 2nd year, block C “Vital reason and poetic reason: José Ortega y Gasset and María Zambrano”. Zambrano, Ortega’s student: poetic reason (1937), the “reason of love”, and El hombre y lo divino (1955), with “the tragedy of the human: being unable to live without gods” and the “insatiable idol”
History of Philosophy, 2nd year, block B “The problem of the relationship between faith and reason” (in the Middle Ages). Two answers from the 20th century: Unamuno, “Reason and faith are two enemies that cannot sustain themselves without each other. […] But join together in struggle”, as against Kant, who gave each its own ground; and Weil, who resisted God “out of pure concern for the truth”

  • Nine chapters, each with something that moves:
  • the classroom seen from the back row, and “believing” and “thinking” separated by a line that turns yellow;
  • the letter that travels from Chile to Spain; the strip of sky that goes from the pink of dawn to the blue of night; the wrestlers on the amphora, for the agón; the word dudar, “to doubt”, which splits in two; and the boy with his two speech bubbles, “yes” by his chest and “no” by his head;
  • the woman who reaches out her hand towards the chair and takes a step; the arm-wrestling table between “reason” and “faith”, where Kant’s two boxes end up;
  • the notebook with an unsolved geometry problem, the clock whose hand goes all the way round and the points of light that come on in the margin; the phone from which notifications keep pouring out;
  • the two signposts, “Christ” and “the truth”, whose paths end in the same place, and the stone doorway of the threshold, seen from the square, with her at the entrance;
  • the building with its crack and its foundation, which lights up; the person with open palms, and another, arms crossed, who ends up opening them too; the Grail king and the young man who asks; a girl and a boy who go together towards someone sitting alone on a bench;
  • the flower that comes apart piece by piece and the one that lights up whole; the timeline full of temples, except for its last stretch; the line between the figure and the chair, which changes colour and grows thicker; the figure made of newspapers and dates that sits in the chair, onto which coins and dates fall without filling it; and what sits there today: a phone full of followers, banknotes, a mirror, a figure with a microphone;
  • the forest clearing, in broad daylight; and the two hands, the closed one (“the idol: everything, and right now”) and the open one (“attention: waits”);
  • and the chair with its three cards, which this time receives an answer in gold: “God, for those who believe”.
  • The visual thread is the chair that won’t be emptied: the line that joins it to the figure is not erased when the chair is denied, but changes colour and becomes more alive. And, as throughout the series, the chair and the three cards with its questions. Javier appears on camera to greet, halfway through (after Weil) and to say goodbye.
  • Nine pauses, without warning and without a countdown:
  • Have you ever talked seriously with someone who believes the opposite of what you believe?
  • Where does your heart say yes and your head say no?
  • Is there something you want to be true? Have you dared to doubt it?
  • What have you given your whole attention to today?
  • Would you go on seeking the truth even if it took you far from what you want to believe?
  • Who would you ask today, and really mean it, what is wrong?
  • What is occupying your chair today? Does it deserve it?
  • The series’ question: if God is dead, what is left?
  • And you: what are you going to seat in your chair?
  • Each quotation, with its work on screen. Javier reads them; the authors are given no voice.
  • Unamuno and Zambrano wrote in Spanish, and their sentences go in as they are: Unamuno, from an undated Renacimiento copy of Del sentimiento trágico de la vida, which is the first edition (1913) or an identical printing, from the Losada edition of La agonía del cristianismo (1938) and from the text of «Mi religión» (“My Religion”) on Wikisource, checked against the Austral edition; Zambrano, from the Alianza edition of El hombre y lo divino (2020), from the Seix Barral edition of Claros del bosque (1977) and, for the first “poetic reason”, from her Obras completas, quoted at second hand. Zambrano’s work is under copyright until the end of 2061: her sentences are kept short, as quotations for teaching.
  • Weil’s are our own literal translation into Spanish from the French, with traducción nuestra, “our translation”, on screen: Attente de Dieu (Waiting for God), from the first edition (La Colombe, 1950), checked against the Fayard edition (1966) and against a second 1950 copy; the letter to Joë Bousquet, from the Cahiers du Sud (1950); «La Personne et le Sacré» (“The Person and the Sacred”, published in English as “Human Personality”), from Écrits de Londres (Gallimard, 1957). The published Spanish translations have their own rights, and are not used.

What Javier says in his own words goes in chalk, not as a quotation (thus, “believing is also living as if God existed”, for a passage of Unamuno’s that was hard to follow by ear). Where a quotation is cut, the screen marks it with “[…]”.

  • The images. The portraits are works of art or free photos, with their origin on screen: Unamuno, the oil painting by Juan de Echevarría (around 1930, Museo Reina Sofía); Weil, the photo on her Free French pass (London, March 1943), small and framed, because there is no other free one of good size; Zambrano, a photo from around 1930, by an unknown photographer. The places, in free photos: the University of Salamanca and its Paraninfo (its great ceremonial hall), Le Puy-en-Velay and an amphora from the National Archaeological Museum in Madrid. The rest are illustrations, and in them Weil is always seen from behind and without features: her face is only seen in the 1943 photo. The illustrations of the three carry no crosses, stained glass or candles; temples appear only on Zambrano’s timeline, small and from many traditions (Egypt, Mesopotamia, Greece, a Romanesque church, a Gothic cathedral and a pagoda). And the clearing, with no rays of light.
  • Vocabulary.
  • Agonía, in Unamuno, is the Greek word: the agón was the contest, the struggle. The screen writes «agonía: angustia» (“agony: anguish”) and crosses it out.
  • Dudar, “to doubt”, comes from the root of “two”. That duellum belongs to the same family is Unamuno’s etymology, and the voice says so as his (“in his own way”): today’s philology does not support it (verificacion-guion.md, LEVE 6, in Spanish).
  • La idea Dios, “the idea God”, goes without de, “of”, as in the original, and mas is “but” (pero).
  • Malheureux is desdichado in Spanish, “the afflicted” on this sheet: in Weil, someone who truly suffers, crushed by affliction (malheur), not someone who is unlucky.
  • Au seuil de l'Église is en el umbral de la Iglesia, “at the threshold of the Church”: the word is hers. A la puerta, “at the door”, is a paraphrase.
  • Le Christ aime qu'on lui préfère la vérité is, word for word, Cristo ama que se le prefiera la verdad, “Christ loves it when the truth is preferred to him”; the lecture says a Cristo le gusta que uno prefiera la verdad antes que a él, “Christ likes us to prefer the truth to him”, which is easier to follow by ear.
  • Dioses, “gods”, in Zambrano, is “a reality different from and higher than the human”. The voice warns: “She is not talking about any particular religion”. And la historia divina, “the divine history”, is not Sacred History: it is the history we make ourselves, turned into a god.
  • A claro, a clearing, is “an open gap in the middle of the forest”.
  • The “yes, you can” is a reading. None of the three answers the title’s question, which is ours, in those words. What they write is that reason and faith have to join together “in struggle” (Unamuno), that “one can never resist God too much if one does so out of pure concern for the truth” (Weil) and that “the exercise of reason and of poetic reason” is needed (Zambrano). The voice puts it like this: “The three of them, each in their own way, answer yes. And this is how I read them: the faith worth having is the one that thinks, doubts and seeks the truth”.

Using it in class, if you wish

The lecture sets no task. If teachers want to use it, here are four ways, from least to most time. In a classroom there is a bit of everything, as Javier says at the start: it is best that no one has to say what they believe.

  • Reading aloud. The two passages from Weil’s “Reflections on the Right Use of School Studies” on the student worksheet, the one about the geometry problem and the one about the Grail, fit into five minutes. Then a question: why does Weil say that having no gift at all for geometry is “almost a favourable circumstance”?
  • With 1st- or 2nd-year students, ten minutes: “what is occupying the chair?”. Each student writes down, without signing it, something that demands a lot of their attention today. A few are read out and put through the lecture’s two questions: does it deserve it? Does it demand everything, and right now? It is worth saying that today’s list is Javier’s, not Zambrano’s or Weil’s, and comparing it with what they write: the “insatiable idol” and the “false goods”.
  • With 1st-year students, fifteen minutes of dialogue. Three questions, with no single answer:
  • Unamuno says that believing begins with “wanting there to be a God”, and yet he distrusts those who believe “without uncertainty, without doubt”. Where is the difference between that and consoling yourself?
  • Weil writes that “Christ likes us to prefer the truth to him”. What does that mean for those who believe? And for those who don’t?
  • Zambrano distinguishes the things that “disappear when we deny them” (desaparecen cuando las negamos) from those that, “even when denied” (aun negadas), are still there. Can you think of one of the second kind that has nothing to do with religion?
  • With 2nd-year students, a text commentary. The passage on the “two categories” in El hombre y lo divino, at the opening of one of its chapters (Alianza, p. 163), in the copy in the school library: the work is under copyright, and the student worksheet only includes a few lines. It serves for block C (Zambrano) and, together with page 113 of Del sentimiento trágico de la vida, which is out of copyright and complete online (in English, Anthony Kerrigan’s The Tragic Sense of Life), for block B (faith and reason). The lecture serves as an introduction and does not do the commentary for them: it only quotes two sentences of the passage and does not go into the rest of the chapter.

Misreadings the lecture takes apart

What is often said What the texts and the sources say
Believing is for people who don’t think; or thinking a lot ends up destroying faith The three bring the two together. Unamuno: “Reason and faith are two enemies that cannot sustain themselves without each other. […] They have to lean on each other and join together. But join together in struggle” (Razón y fe son dos enemigos que no pueden sostenerse el uno sin el otro. […] Tienen que apoyarse uno en otro y asociarse. Pero asociarse en lucha; STV, p. 113). Weil: “one can never resist God too much if one does so out of pure concern for the truth” (AD, p. 77). Zambrano: “more than ever, the exercise of reason and of poetic reason is needed” (es preciso más que nunca, el ejercicio de la razón y de la razón poética; 1937)
Believing is consoling yourself Unamuno does not hide that believing begins with wanting, but he distrusts a calm faith: “Those who, without passion of spirit, without anguish, without uncertainty, without doubt, without despair in their consolation, believe they believe in God, believe only in the idea God, but not in God himself” (Los que sin pasión de ánimo, sin congoja, sin incertidumbre, sin duda, sin la desesperación en el consuelo, creen creer en Dios, no creen sino en la idea Dios, mas no en Dios mismo; STV, p. 192). Weil held back with “not my love, but my intelligence” (AD, p. 77)
What happened to Weil was imagination She writes the opposite: “neither the senses nor the imagination had any part in it” (AD, p. 76). The lecture does not judge it: it reads what she wrote
Zambrano’s “gods” are the religions She defines them herself: “a reality different from and higher than the human” (una realidad distinta y superior a lo humano)
“You will win, but you will not convince” (Venceréis, pero no convenceréis), said in those words in the Paraninfo on 12 October 1936 It is the version of Luis Portillo (Horizon, London, December 1941), who was not at the ceremony. What Unamuno said cannot be reconstructed word for word (Delgado, 2018). What is his is in his notes (“to win and to convince”, vencer y convencer) and in his letters: “I told the whole truth, that to win is not to convince, nor to conquer to convert” (dije toda la verdad, que vencer no es convencer ni conquistar es convertir; to Quintín de Torre, 1/12/1936)
In Unamuno, “agony” means suffering with no way out “Agony, then, is struggle” (La agonía es, pues, lucha; AC, p. 20): the Greek agón, the fight
La razón y la fe son dos enemigos…, as it is often quoted in Spanish Without the articles: Razón y fe son dos enemigos…, “Reason and faith are two enemies…” (STV, p. 113). In English the difference does not show
Unamuno struggles with God like Jacob, at night In Genesis, Jacob wrestles at night “until the breaking of the day”. Unamuno turns it round: he struggles “from the break of dawn until nightfall” (desde el romper del alba hasta el caer de la noche), “as they say Jacob struggled with Him” (como dicen que con Él luchó Jacob)
The Church banned all of Unamuno’s work It put two books on the Index, Del sentimiento trágico de la vida and La agonía del cristianismo, in January 1957 (the session was on the 23rd and the decree on the 30th), and warned that others contained “many things against faith and morals” (Acta Apostolicae Sedis 49, 1957, pp. 77-78)
Unamuno was rector from 1900 to 1936, and Primo de Rivera removed him He was rector from 1900 to 1914 and from 1931 to 1936. In 1924 he was vice-rector and dean, and the exile order gives no reason
La agonía del cristianismo first came out in Spanish It came out first in French, in 1925, translated by Jean Cassou; in Spanish, in 1931. He wrote it in Paris at the end of 1924
“Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity” is from Waiting for God It is from a letter to Joë Bousquet of 13 April 1942, published in the Cahiers du Sud in 1950
“Christ came down and took me”; Herbert’s poem was given to her by a young Englishman It is «le Christ lui-même», Christ himself (AD, p. 76). Weil does not name Herbert: the young man introduced her to the English metaphysical poets, and she found the poem “later, reading them”. That it is “Love (III)” is said by the notes and the critics
Weil “stayed at the door of the Church”; or “she was never baptised” Her word is seuil, threshold (letters II and IV). That she was not baptised, or the opposite, cannot be asserted: what is certain is that she did not ask for baptism and wrote that she was staying at the threshold
La Pesanteur et la Grâce (Gravity and Grace) is a book by Weil It is a selection from her notebooks made by Gustave Thibon and published in 1947
La fatalidad del hombre que no puede vivir sin dioses, “the fate of man, who cannot live without gods”; and for Zambrano, God’s empty place is taken by the State The first sentence is from a Siruela back cover: hers is “the tragedy of the human: being unable to live without gods” (la tragedia de lo humano: no poder vivir sin dioses). And the State is not in El hombre y lo divino: the empty place is taken by divinised history, the “insatiable idol”, the future, an “unknown god” (dios desconocido), and nothingness
“Poetic reason” first appears in the review of Machado’s La guerra Earlier, in the epilogue to Madre España, in Santiago de Chile, in January 1937. Both are from 1937
Zambrano and Weil met in Valencia, at the 1937 writers’ congress Weil was in Spain only in August and September 1936. What there is is Zambrano’s memory, in a 1974 letter, of a meeting in 1936 (Rius, 2007)
Zambrano got her doctorate; the war caught her in Chile She began a thesis on Spinoza and did not finish it. On 18 July 1936 she was in Madrid, and she left for Chile in October
24.2% of the world is atheist; in Spain there are already more atheists than practising Catholics That 24.2% are people with no religious affiliation, and many of them believe in something (Pew). The atheists depend on the survey: in the October 2026 barometer (CIS 3582) they come out ahead, 17.3 against 15.6; in study 3584, with nine times as many interviews, the other way round, 16.8 against 18.3

Going further: what the lecture doesn’t say aloud

  • Unamuno in 1936, in order. On 25 July, the military mayor kept Unamuno on as a councillor in the new City Council (“Western civilisation must be saved, Christian civilisation, so seriously threatened”, according to the minutes: Hay que salvar la civilización occidental, la civilización cristiana tan seriamente amenazada). On 7 August he gave five thousand pesetas to an “official subscription in favour of the Salamanca forces” (suscripción oficial a favor de las fuerzas salmantinas; not “to the Falange”, as some headlines have it). In August and September he spoke to the press, above all the foreign press. On 22 August, the Republic removed him from office for having joined “publicly the faction in arms” (de modo público a la facción en armas); on 1 September, the National Defence Junta in Burgos reinstated him as rector for life. On seeing the repression, which reached friends of his, he gradually drew away in private. On 12 October, in the Paraninfo, he had not intended to speak, and did so to reply to the attacks on Basques and Catalans; General Millán Astray interrupted him, shouting against intellectuals. On the 13th the City Council declared him in “moral incompatibility”, on the 14th the university senate withdrew its confidence in him and on the 22nd Franco removed him by decree (the BOE, the rebels’ official gazette, Burgos, 28/10/1936). From then on, with a policeman at the door: “I am writing to you from a prison in disguise, for such is this house of mine today” (Le escribo desde una cárcel disfrazada, que tal es hoy esta mi casa). And in another letter: “How naive and how rash I was in joining Franco’s movement” (Qué cándido y qué ligero anduve al adherirme al movimiento de Franco). It was some twelve weeks, without joining anything, and he broke with them in public, at his own cost: it is not comparable to Heidegger’s case (verificacion-vidas.md, point 5, in Spanish). Why he supported the coup at first are his ideas, and they are debated as ideas.
  • Where the “you will win” comes from. From Luis Portillo’s article “Unamuno’s Last Lecture”, in Horizon (London, December 1941), which Hugh Thomas brought into his history of the war (1961). Severiano Delgado, librarian at the University of Salamanca, maintains that Portillo was not at the ceremony and that his text is “a literary re-creation”. The phrase was already circulating in the Republican press in November 1936. A witness, the doctor José Pérez-López Villamil, remembered: “bear in mind that it is one thing to win and another to convince... one thing to conquer and another to convert!” (¡tened presente que una cosa es vencer y otra convencer... una cosa es conquistar y otra convertir!).
  • The 1897 crisis. In March 1897, aged 32, Unamuno went through a crisis of anguish. One night he burst into tears, and his wife, holding him, said to him: “My child!” (¡Hijo mío!); he told the story many times afterwards in his letters. He then tried to recover the faith of his childhood, and did not entirely succeed. That much of his work springs from this tension between wanting to believe and being unable to is interpretation, common among his biographers.
  • Faith “founded on uncertainty”. In Del sentimiento trágico de la vida (p. 122), Unamuno comments on the cry of the father in the Gospel, “Lord, I believe; help thou mine unbelief” (Mark 9:24): “that contradiction is what gives all its deepest human value to that cry […]. His faith is a faith founded on uncertainty” (esa contradicción es lo que da todo su más hondo valor humano a ese grito […]. Su fe es una fe a base de incertidumbre). And the passage that the voice gives in Javier’s words is this one: “To believe in God is to long for there to be one, and it is, moreover, to behave as if there were; it is to live by that longing and to make of it our inmost spring of action” (Creer en Dios es anhelar que le haya y es además conducirse como si le hubiera; es vivir de ese anhelo y hacer de él nuestro íntimo resorte de acción; pp. 183-184).
  • Weil’s “whether you believe or not”, and the implicit. The sentence on page 116 says “explicit”, and the word matters: for Weil, those who seek the truth without believing have an implicit relationship with God, the same idea as “Christ likes us to prefer the truth to him”. Just before, in the text, faith is “the indispensable condition”; and at the end (p. 123) Weil writes that, for a generous adolescent, studies would have their effectiveness “even outside any religious belief”. Javier’s “this holds for you too, whether you believe or not” is an honest consequence, not a secularisation of Weil (verificacion-guion.md, point 1, in Spanish).
  • Weil’s three contacts with Catholicism, as she tells them in letter IV (pp. 74-76): a fishing village in Portugal, where the women went round the boats in procession, with candles, and she felt that Christianity is “the religion of slaves” (it was in 1935, according to the biographies); Assisi, in 1937, where “something stronger than I was compelled me, for the first time in my life, to go down on my knees”; and Solesmes, in Holy Week 1938, following all the services with intense headaches. The poem is “Love (III)”, by George Herbert, from The Temple (1633), which begins “Love bade me welcome: yet my soul drew back”.
  • Why she stayed at the threshold. She herself gives different reasons in each letter. In January 1942, that God’s will was not for her to enter the Church “at present”, and that no thought pained her more than that of separating herself “from the immense and afflicted mass of unbelievers” (in Weil, the “afflicted”, malheureux, are those who suffer, and the sentence is one of solidarity). Later, that the intellectual obstacles had gone, but she was afraid of “the Church as a social thing”. In May, the intersection and the anathema sit, the condemnation of anyone who does not accept a dogma. In 1938 she had written to Bernanos: “I am not a Catholic, although […] nothing Catholic, nothing Christian has ever seemed alien to me”.
  • “Person”, in Weil. «La Personne et le Sacré» begins like this: “There is something sacred in every man. But it is not his person. Nor is it the human person. It is he, this man, quite simply” (Écrits de Londres, p. 11). “Person” is a technical term here, and by ear a teenager hears a contradiction: that is why the lecture does not use that sentence. The text was first published in La Table ronde (December 1950), under another title.
  • Idolatry, in her notebooks. The lecture gives Weil’s idol through her “false goods”. In her notebooks there is more: “Idolatry comes from the fact that, thirsting for absolute good, one does not possess supernatural attention and does not have the patience to let it grow”, and “Only, we humble ourselves before false gods” (La Pesanteur et la Grâce, pp. 69-70). That book is a selection by Thibon, with his punctuation.
  • What Weil wrote about Judaism. She wrote very harsh pages against Judaism and the Old Testament, almost all in notebooks that others published, and commented, without rejecting it, on a report by a Resistance group about the Jews of France, which is her hardest text to defend. The controversy is serious: she is accused, among others, by Levinas («Simone Weil contre la Bible», 1952), Giniewski (1978), Nevin (1991) and Kaplan (2007); she is defended, or her case qualified, by Chenavier (Simone Weil y la cuestión judía, Trotta), who concludes that she was not antisemitic in the strict sense, but that her view of Judaism is mistaken, Yourgrau (2011), Sylvie Weil (2009) and Kirsch (2025). At the same time, in a letter to Xavier Vallat, Vichy’s General Commissioner for Jewish Affairs (18/10/1941), she called the statute on the Jews “unjust and absurd”, and she worked for Free France against Nazism. As a rule of the channel, no sentence of hers on that subject is quoted as one more voice.
  • Pacifism. Weil was a pacifist until 1939 and changed her mind in the spring of that year. In her London notebooks she called it “my criminal error before 1939 about the pacifist circles and their action” (La Connaissance surnaturelle, 1950). In 1936, in Spain, she spent about two months with an international group of the Durruti column, on the Aragon front, and returned to France after burning herself with boiling oil.
  • Zambrano and the Frente Español (1932). With other young disciples of Ortega, she signed the manifesto of the Frente Español (“Spanish Front”), published on 7 March 1932 in the newspaper La Luz, which wanted a “national” party, above left and right. Who drafted it is not clear. In 1989 she said that she refused to let José Antonio Primo de Rivera (who in 1933 founded the fascist Falange) join and that, on seeing different tendencies, they all left it. In 1936 she was denounced as a fascist for having signed it, and in 1937 she was writing against fascism (Los intelectuales en el drama de España). Historians read it in two ways: a “national party” (Soto Carrasco; Payne) or “the fascist-leaning Frente Español” (Luján and Baqués, 2023).
  • Zambrano, Weil and Unamuno. Zambrano wrote no essay on Weil, but read her all her life. In 1974 she wrote to a friend: “I have been on the verge of asking you whether you have read Simone Weil and whether you love her. I love her” (He estado al borde de preguntarte si has leído a Simone Weil y si la quieres. Yo la amo). And she remembered having met her in 1936, in the middle of the war: half an hour sitting on a divan, “shy” (tímidas), saying “hardly anything” (apenas nada) to each other. Where, is not clear (Rius, 2007). She read Unamuno as a girl in her father’s library, and in Cuba, around 1940, she devoted a whole book to him, unpublished until 2003 (Unamuno, Debate).
  • Poetic reason, in full. In the epilogue to Madre España (January 1937), the sentence continues: “the poetic reason that finds in instantaneous discovery what the intelligence picks apart step by step into its elements” (la razón poética que encuentra en instantáneo descubrimiento lo que la inteligencia desgrana paso a paso en sus elementos). And in the review of Machado’s La guerra (December 1937): “this reason of love that restores the rich substance of the world” (esta razón de amor reintegradora de la rica sustancia del mundo).
  • “I have never left.” Zambrano returned to Madrid on 20 November 1984, after 45 years of exile, and to the journalists who asked her what she felt she said: “What do I feel on returning to Spain? I have never left” (¿Qué siento al volver a España? Yo nunca me he ido; El País, 21/11/1984). In 1981 she had won the first Prince of Asturias Award for Communication and Humanities. The Cervantes was received on her behalf by her cousin Rafael Tomero, on 24 April 1989, and her speech was read by the actress Berta Riaza.
  • The figures, with their limits. CIS 3584 carried out 45,074 interviews from 7 to 30 October 2026. 39.8% of adults say they have no religion: 35.6% of women and 44.2% of men. Among those aged 18 to 24, 53.7% (the breakdown by sex within that age group is not published). The 2025 Barometer on Religion and Beliefs gives 61% for “young people”, without saying which ages it counts. Worldwide, Pew warns that the “religiously unaffiliated” may hold religious or spiritual beliefs, and China weighs heavily in that figure. And the sociologist Peter Berger, who had argued that the modern world was becoming secular, wrote in 1999 that the world today is “as furiously religious as it ever was”, with two exceptions: Europe and a minority with Western-type higher education.
  • Kant, the one from lecture 2. “I therefore had to deny knowledge in order to make room for faith” (Critique of Pure Reason, preface to the 2nd edition, 1787, B XXX). It is a moral faith, not the doctrine of a church. The contrast with Unamuno, who sits the two of them “at the same table, to fight”, is Javier’s.
