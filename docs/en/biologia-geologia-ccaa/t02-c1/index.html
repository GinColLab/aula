From continental drift to plate tectonics: a conjecture that took half a century

Watch the class on YouTube (in Spanish, with English subtitles)Download the sheet (PDF)Student worksheet (PDF)Discuss it with other teachers in the Ágora (in Spanish)Versión en español

In a nutshell

The class opens the topic with a question almost nobody asks: if Wegener was right in 1912 that the continents move, why did it take half a century for this to be accepted? It begins with a figure he presented in 1929 as "the first exact astronomical proof": Greenland would be moving away from Europe by 36 metres every year. The figure is left hanging and picked up again at the end.

The answer has two halves. What Wegener was missing: his evidence was about the past and some of it admitted another explanation (sunken land bridges), his engine was impossible (Jeffreys, 1929) and his measurement was a mistake. What came later: the sea floor. In 1952 Marie Tharp finds a valley in the middle of the Atlantic mountain range; the earthquakes fall inside it; Heezen explains it with an Earth that is swelling, and Hess, in 1960–1962, with a sea floor that is born at the mid-ocean ridges, sinks in the trenches and carries the continent with it, in the same piece. From this comes what a plate is and, above all, a prediction that can be measured, which students will test for themselves in class 2.

The ending goes back to Greenland: today GPS gives less than 2 cm a year, and Wegener's mistake fitted inside the delay of a person pressing a stopwatch. And it takes apart the cliché that "Wegener was right and the others were closed-minded": those who rejected him had arguments; what was missing was data from the sea floor.

Objective. By the end, students will know what evidence Wegener gave, why he was not believed, what Marie Tharp saw on the floor of the Atlantic, what Harry Hess proposed to explain it and what prediction had to be tested, and why that journey teaches that science is collective and corrects itself.

What this sheet adds:

  • the nuances the voice does not mention: how the fit on the board was calculated, the environment of Mesosaurus, where Jeffreys's figures come from, what the six profiles and the plate cross-section are, what class 2 deals with and how the Greenland measurement was disproved;
  • the mistakes to expect;
  • the solutions to everything;
  • an activity of about 15 minutes that no textbook offers: the Greenland measurement, with a calculator. Students redo Wegener's calculation, discover that one second on the clock is 200 metres of ground and work out how many years you would have to wait to see the real movement with that method;
  • a cut-out map (mapa-para-recortar.pdf, in the materials), the one the voice announces at (4:05);
  • open resources to go further.

Notes for teachers

Conjecture, hypothesis and theory (2:38). The class uses three words with a precise meaning: a conjecture may be true but cannot yet be tested; a hypothesis takes a risk, it predicts something measurable; a theory has passed those tests. This is a teaching usage, not a universal rule: in the history of science "hypothesis" and "theory" are used more loosely. What matters is the criterion: can it be tested?

The fit (3:32). Wegener did not fit the coastlines, but the 200 m depth line, the edge of the shelf (Wegener, 1929, p. 185). The board calculates the best possible fit for each line, rotating South America on the sphere until its line lies as close as possible to Africa's, as Bullard, Everett and Smith did by computer in 1965 (program encaje.py, with ETOPO 2022). The result, along the stretch facing the South Atlantic:

  • along the coast: 729,000 km² of gaps and overlaps, with a mean separation of 131 km;
  • along the 200 m edge: 346,000 km², with 61 km.

Hence "less than half". Bullard et al. found the best fit somewhat deeper, at the 500-fathom line (about 900 m), with errors of 30 to 90 km: the voice says so ("somewhat deeper"). Patagonia and the Falkland Islands (Malvinas) are left out, as in the classic fits, because that margin was stretched when the ocean opened. Jeffreys (1929, p. 322), who looked only at the coastlines, saw a misfit of about 15°.

The cut-out map (4:05). It is in the materials: four A4 pages, South America and Africa at 1:25 million, with the coastline and the 200 m line. One copy is cut out for each line. Each continent is in its own equal-area projection centred near its Atlantic coast, so the edge that is joined has almost its real shape; even so, turning paper on a table is not turning on a sphere, and the paper fit is approximate. The calculated one is on the board.

Mesosaurus (4:18). The voice says "of inland waters, which did not go out into the open sea". The classic version makes it a freshwater animal, and today this is debated: the sites in Uruguay and Brazil point to a salty inland sea, perhaps a very salty one (Piñeiro et al., 2012). For the argument it makes no difference: it could not cross an ocean. Its finds are in southern Africa and eastern South America (Brazil, Uruguay and Paraguay): the board shows the real ones, 17 sites from the Paleobiology Database (CC0). The four-fossil map found in many textbooks is not Wegener's: the Antarctic Lystrosaurus turned up in 1969–1970.

Fixists and mobilists (4:55). These are today's names, used to organise the debate. Isostasy was Wegener's best argument against the bridges: a bridge of continental crust, which is lighter, cannot sink into a denser sea floor. Du Toit, his defender, acknowledged that the bridges also explained the fossils, "though less elegantly" (in Wegener, 1929, p. V). That is why the class insists: a fact that can be explained in two ways does not decide between them.

The ice and the pole (5:39) and (6:42). About 300 million years ago, at the end of the Carboniferous and the beginning of the Permian, there were ice sheets on all the southern continents and in India (Wegener, 1929, p. 135; Rosa and Isbell, 2021). The board marks the traces at classic localities, checked one by one, in South America, Africa, Madagascar, India, Arabia (Oman), Australia and Antarctica. On the globe, the south pole of that time is labelled "according to the model, approximate": it is given by the plate model, which has a palaeomagnetic reference frame.

The torn newspaper (6:21). The comparison is Wegener's (1929, p. 79), and he considered the evidence "hard to knock down". The Sierra de la Ventana (in the south of Buenos Aires province) and the Cape Fold Belt (South Africa) are part of the same ancient mountain range, with the Falkland Islands (Malvinas) in between (Stanca et al.).

Pangaea (6:42). The animation uses the plate model of Merdith et al. (2021, CC BY 4.0), with the fossil finds travelling with their plate. It formed about 300 million years ago and began to break up about 200 million years ago (USGS). It was not the first supercontinent: there were others before it, such as Rodinia and Columbia (Mitchell et al., 2021).

Wegener's engine and the icebreaker (7:13). Wegener proposed two forces above all: the "flight from the poles", which comes from the Earth's rotation, and tidal friction; in 1929 he also mentioned convection (p. 184). The comparison with the ship breaking the ice is Nil's, not Wegener's: he compared the continents to floating icebergs (p. 38). What the sources do say is that, in his model, the continent had to force its way through the ocean floor: "sail through the sima" (Dietz, 1961, p. 855) or "plow through oceanic crust" (Hess, 1962, p. 599).

Jeffreys (7:52) and (8:23). A British geophysicist, the same man who deduced in 1926 that the Earth's core is liquid (topic 1, class 4). The dilemma is in The Earth (2nd ed., 1929, p. 322): if the ocean floor is harder, the continent cannot get through; if it is softer, the continent advances "without being fractured" and raises no mountain ranges. The figure: moving America westwards in some thirty million years by tidal friction would need a force 10¹⁰ times greater, which would stop the Earth's rotation in about a year (p. 322). With his own numbers it comes out at 3 × 10⁹; 10¹⁰ is his rounding. The statement that drift was "out of the question" is on p. 305 and comes from a different argument, the strength of the crust. That is why the voice says that what he had proved was less: that no known force was enough.

New York, 1926 (8:54). The symposium of the American Association of Petroleum Geologists, on 15 November 1926; the papers were published in 1928. The remark about "forgetting what had been learned in seventy years" was reported by R. T. Chamberlin, quoting a colleague (Du Toit, 1937, p. 31); other authors attribute it to Chamberlin himself. That is why the screen says "quoting a colleague".

Holmes (9:21). He presented his idea in a talk in January 1928 to the Geological Society of Glasgow, published in 1931: convection currents in the substratum that could pull the continents apart and create a new ocean between them (p. 579), and he himself calls it a working hypothesis (p. 600). The diagram on the board has been drawn afresh, not copied.

The echo sounder (10:21). A pulse of sound goes down, bounces and comes back; with the speed of sound in water, about 1,500 m/s, the round-trip time gives the depth. The sea floor the ship crosses is a real profile (GMRT), and the seconds on the stopwatch are calculated with that speed.

Marie Tharp (10:39) to (11:30). She worked in Lamont Hall, at Columbia University's Lamont observatory, in Palisades, on the outskirts of New York. In 1952 she completed six profiles of the North Atlantic from other people's soundings: for about fifteen years she was not allowed on board the laboratory's ships (Tharp, AIP oral history). The mountains did not match, but the valley did, "especially in the three northern ones". Two nuances the voice respects: Tharp did not discover the mountain range, which had been known since the 19th century, but the valley that runs along it; and cosas de chicas, in the Spanish voice, translates the "girl talk" with which, according to her, Heezen dismissed her interpretation (Tharp, 1999).

The six profiles on the board (11:07). They are not Tharp's: they are today's, from GMRT (the Lamont synthesis of soundings, CC BY 4.0), and that is why the board labels them as such. They are at 21.5°, 26.1°, 31.0°, 43.0°, 44.9° and 46.5° N, chosen from fifteen latitudes tested from 21.5 to 52° N: all of them have a notch, of 600 to 2,000 m, and these have one of more than 1,200 m. The Azores (37–40° N) are left out, where the ridge widens into a plateau. Each profile is aligned on the axis marked by the earthquakes (the median of their longitude at that latitude), and the valley is the deepest point less than 30 km from that axis (program fondo.py).

The earthquakes (11:53). Foster plotted the epicentres of the 1950s by hand. Those on the board are today's, like his: the 2,549 of magnitude 5 or more in the USGS catalogue between 1973 and 2025 in the North Atlantic, located by the USGS's own network (public domain). Heezen did not accept it on seeing the maps overlaid: it took him a few more months, until mid-1953, and he was also convinced by the valley continuing as far as the East African Rift (Tharp, 1999).

The 1957 map (12:19). It was Heezen and Tharp's first published map, not the first map of the sea floor: there were bathymetric charts before it. It was drawn as a landscape without water because the contour lines of the sea floor were a military secret (Tharp, 1999; Library of Congress, 2025). It is in the public domain in the US (its registration was not renewed), and the board shows a crop of it.

The swelling Earth (12:34). It was a serious hypothesis: Heezen proposed it cautiously around 1959–1960, and Hess cites it (1962, p. 610). Today it does not hold up; why is told in class 2, which is where criterion 2.2 calls for it (checking the truthfulness of information).

Hess and Dietz (12:52) to (14:02). Hess commanded the Cape Johnson during the war and left the echo sounder measuring on his crossings. He wrote the idea down in 1960, in a report that circulated as a preprint, and published it in 1962 ("History of Ocean Basins"). He calculated 1 cm a year on each side from the separation of South America and Africa (p. 608). The geopoetry quotation begins "Like Umbgrove, …" (p. 599): that is why it carries square brackets. Dietz published in Nature in June 1961 and coined the name; in 1968 Hess's priority was recognised on the strength of that preprint (Nature 220, 535). For Hess, the continent was carried by mantle convection; what really moves the plates is the subject of class 3.

The plate cross-section (14:28). It is a real cross-section at 23° S, from the trench off Chile (about 7,400 m) to the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, with the ETOPO 2022 relief exaggerated vertically. The axis of the ridge comes from the 60 nearby earthquakes. The thicknesses are schematic: about 100 km on average; less near the ridge, where the plate is born, and more under the oldest parts of the continents. The oceanic plate thickens as it moves away from the ridge (Lithgow-Bertelloni and Richards, 1998) and under the old shields the lithosphere exceeds 200 km (Artemieva and Mooney, 2001). The voice says "about a hundred kilometres on average": it is the order of magnitude given by Isacks, Oliver and Sykes (1968).

The prediction (15:35) to (16:16). Hess already pointed to it: at the crest there should be only recent sediments (1962, p. 609). The way of reading the record, the magnetic stripes, was proposed by Vine and Matthews in 1963 (and by Morley, whose paper was rejected); it was confirmed by Pitman and Heirtzler, and by Vine, in 1966, and in 1968 Isacks, Oliver and Sykes were already speaking of the "new global tectonics". The board colours the ages as a diagram; students handle the real data in class 2.

Greenland (16:27) to (17:47). The figures:

  • Wegener (1929, p. 29): 0.9 seconds of time in five years, about 36 m a year, between the 1922 measurement (Jensen) and the 1927 one (Sabel-Jørgensen), at Qôrnoq, at 64.5° N. He himself writes that the 1927 one was made with an impersonal micrometer, which removes personal error, and that assuming Jensen's had been 0.9 s was "very improbable".
  • At that latitude, one second on the clock is about 200 m of ground (see the activity).
  • Salomon-Calvi (1931, pp. 6–8) saw it at once: only the 1927 measurement removed personal error, and a personal error of 0.9 s "is within the bounds of possibility".
  • In 1936 the measurement was repeated on the same pillar and came out practically the same as in 1927 (Nørlund, 1937; Polar Record, 1939). A Nature report (1937) speaks of two epochs "seven years apart": the screen follows Nørlund.
  • Today: 17.5 mm a year at Kangerlussuaq (west) and 15.4 in the north-east, with GPS; 18.1 and 15.9 with the MORVEL model. Hence "less than two centimetres" and "about two thousand times".

What nobody tells, and it fits with the reflection at (17:47): Wegener gave a mean error nine times smaller than the signal. But that was the error of repeating the measurement. The one that mattered was each observer's delay, which does not go down however many times you repeat. It is the difference between random error and systematic error, and it applies to any laboratory measurement.

Closed-minded? (18:07). The scales do not hand out blame: in favour of movement there was evidence from many sciences; against it, an impossible engine, fossils with another explanation and a wrong measurement. The sentence worth keeping is the voice's: those who rejected him had arguments, and what was missing was data from the sea floor.

Typical mistakes and how they are handled

The first four are documented in studies with large samples: King (2010), 51 textbooks; Libarkin et al. (2005), 265 questionnaires and 105 interviews; Marques and Thompson (1997), 270 students aged 16 and 17; Mills et al. (2017), 95 students. The others are anticipated by the topic sheet.

  1. "Plates float on magma; the mantle is liquid." It is the most repeated mistake, even in textbooks (6.6% of those King found). (14:28): "Plates don't float on magma", with the magma crossed out on the board.
  2. "A plate is the crust." (15:13): crust and rigid mantle.
  3. "Plates are underground", or stacked in layers. 40% of Mills's students believed it. (15:13): the top of your plate is the ground you walk on.
  4. "Plate boundaries are the coastlines." 21% of Marques and Thompson's students. (15:13): in the Atlantic, the boundary is in the middle of the ocean.
  5. "Pangaea was the first supercontinent", or it existed when the Earth formed (Libarkin). (6:42): there were others before it.
  6. "Wegener was right and the others were closed-minded." (18:07). It helps to ask what they would have said about his engine.
  7. "Jeffreys proved that drift was impossible." (8:23): he proved that no known force was enough.
  8. "The continent rides on top of the plate, like a ship." (14:28): it is part of it.
  9. "A theory is an unproven idea." That is the everyday usage. (2:38): in science, a theory is what has passed the tests.
  10. "Marie Tharp discovered the mid-ocean ridge." (11:07): the mountain range was already known; she saw the valley.

Questions and solutions

In the video

  • In the last class we sliced the Earth into layers in two ways. One, according to whether they are rigid or softer. The top one is rigid. Do you remember its name? (0:18). The lithosphere: the crust and the top part of the mantle, about 100 km.
  • Fossils of a reptile that could not cross an ocean turn up on both sides of the Atlantic. Can you think of an explanation? (4:42); the pause, with a countdown. Two possible explanations: the continents were together, or there was a land bridge. What will come up: "by swimming", "a current carried it". Criterion: getting it right does not matter; what matters is seeing that the fact alone does not decide between the two.
  • The ocean floor is rock too. Can a continent force its way through it? (7:37); the pause, with a countdown. No, if you look at it the way Jeffreys did: if the ocean floor is harder, the continent stops; if it is softer, the continent advances without breaking and raises no mountain ranges. What will come up: "yes, like a ship".
  • If Hess is right, how old will the sea floor be as you move away from the ridge? (15:35); the pause, with a countdown. Older the further away, and the same on both sides. What will come up: "all equally old" or "older in the middle".

Practice (the solutions open class 2)

  1. Explain which of Wegener's arguments was only an impression and which was evidence, and why. The resemblance of the coastlines by eye is an impression: something that seems so, that may be chance and that is not measured (Jeffreys saw a misfit of 15°). Evidence is what can be checked and measured independently: the fit of the submerged edge, with its error in kilometres; the rocks and mountain ranges that continue on the other side; the fossils and the traces of the ice in their places. Criterion: distinguishing "it seems" from "it can be checked", and saying that evidence can also admit another explanation (the fossils). Another choice is accepted if it is well reasoned.
  2. Heezen and Hess had the same valley in front of them. Say how each of them interpreted it. And if Hess is right, say where the ocean floor has to disappear. Heezen: the Earth was swelling and the ocean floor was splitting open like the skin of a balloon. Hess: new ocean floor is born at the ridge and moves apart to both sides, without the Earth growing. That is why it has to disappear somewhere else: in the trenches, like the one off Chile, where it sinks and returns to the mantle. What will come up: that the ocean floor "builds up" or "piles up at the coasts".
  3. Calculate how far Greenland would move away in a hundred years with Wegener's figure, and how far with today's, 1.8 cm a year. 36 m × 100 = 3,600 m = 3.6 km. 1.8 cm × 100 = 180 cm = 1.8 m. Two thousand times less. Criterion: the units: the typical mistake is mixing metres and centimetres.

The open question (20:33). In 1926, in favour of Wegener there were many clues, from different sciences, pointing to the same thing. Against him, nobody knew of a force capable of moving a continent. With what was known then, would you have believed him? It has no textbook answer, and Nil does not give his. Hints for steering it without closing it: is believing an idea the same as taking it as a working hypothesis, as Holmes did? What weighs more, many clues that agree or a missing mechanism? What would you have needed to be convinced? Criterion 1.3: defending a position with arguments from both sides and listening to the other side's.

A classroom activity

The Greenland measurement, with a calculator. About 15 minutes, in pairs, with the student sheet and before watching the part "Greenland, measured". No materials are needed, except a calculator with cosine.

The idea: in 1929 longitude (where a place is from east to west) was measured by timing the passage of the stars. Students calculate how much ground corresponds to one second on the clock, redo Wegener's figure and discover what precision would be needed to see the real movement.

The data: radius of the Earth, R = 6,371 km; Qôrnoq, at 64.5° N; longitude is measured in time, and 24 hours is a full turn (see the note); difference between the two measurements, 0.9 s in five years; today's speed, 1.8 cm a year.

The solutions:

Step Calculation Result
1. Radius of Qôrnoq's parallel r = R · cos 64.5° = 6,371 · 0.4305 2,743 km
2. Full circle of that parallel 2 · π · r 17,233 km
3. Ground that passes in one second 17,233,000 m / 86,400 s about 200 m
4. Wegener's figure 0.9 s · 200 m/s = 180 m, in 5 years 36 m a year
5. Years to see 0.9 s for real 180 m / 0.018 m a year about 10,000 years
6. Years to see a tenth of a second 20 m / 0.018 m a year about 1,100 years
7. Precision to see 5 years of real movement 5 · 1.8 cm = 9 cm; 0.09 m / 200 m/s 0.45 thousandths of a second

The conclusion that has to come out: with a stopwatch and the stars, the real movement of Greenland was invisible within a human lifetime. Wegener's figure did not measure the continent: it measured the difference between two observers. Today GPS antennas can detect a fraction of a millimetre a year.

A note for those who want to be more precise. Wegener's "nine tenths of a second" are a difference in longitude expressed in time, the usual form in astronomy: 24 hours are 360°, so one second is exactly 15 seconds of arc. At 64.5° N, 15″ of arc is 199.5 m of ground, the same as comes out in step 3. If someone objects that the Earth turns relative to the stars in 23 h 56 min, that is the answer: here the second does not measure a length of time, but an angle. The table rounds to 200 m.

Assessment. For criterion 3.4: each pair explains in two lines why the 1929 measurement "proves nothing", even though Greenland does move, and what would have been needed for it to prove something (the same observer and the same method, or a method without human delay, and many more years). If the idea comes up that repeating a measurement does not remove an error that repeats itself the same way every time, that is systematic error: it is worth naming it.

And the cut-out map, at home or in class if there is time left: two copies of the PDF, one cut out along the coastline and the other along the blue line. Each pair puts the pieces together as best they fit and measures with a ruler the widest gap in each fit; with the scale, 1 mm on the paper is 25 km. Along the submerged edge it should come out better, as on the board.

What the class leaves out, and why

The class has one question, why it took half a century, and whatever does not answer it stays out, even if it is good.

  • The magnetic stripes and the age of the sea floor. They are the evidence, and they have a class of their own, class 2, with real data that students handle.
  • Why the Earth does not grow. Also in class 2.
  • What moves the plates. Class 3: today we know that the main pull comes from the plate that sinks, not that convection drags the plates along.
  • The types of boundary. Classes 4 to 6.
  • The classic four-fossil map, for the reason given above: it is not Wegener's.
  • A portrait of Marie Tharp. None of her photos has a verified licence; the class shows her map, which is free.
  • Predicting where the continents will be. Stretching today's speeds over millions of years is an extrapolation the class cannot justify.
