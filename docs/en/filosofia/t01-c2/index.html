What is philosophy? Philosophers at the table

Download the sheet (PDF)Student worksheet (PDF)Versión en español

In a nutshell

This is the first idea lab of the course: in Philosophy the lab is dialogue, without a lab coat. Seven philosophers from different times sit at an imaginary table and each answers, in their own words, the question in the title: what is philosophy?

  • Socrates: examining your own life.
  • Aristotle: seeking the knowledge of first causes, for the sake of knowing.
  • Thomas Aquinas: what human reason can reach on its own.
  • Descartes: the tree of all knowledge, whose fruit is living well.
  • Kant: four questions that lead to one, what is the human being?
  • Marx: not stopping at interpreting the world, but helping to change it.
  • María Zambrano: a search with a method, which is not everything without poetry.

The class draws out what they share (a search, with reasons, for what lies deepest: the four features from class 1) and where they disagree (what the search is for, and how far it goes). It takes apart the mistake of thinking that, if they don't agree, it's pure opinion. It opens a window outside Europe (Confucius, the Buddha and al-Kindi) and ends with the most important part: each answer at the table becomes a personal question for the student. At the end, the eighth chair is theirs.

Aim. By the end, students will be able to say what philosophy is for seven great philosophers, in their own words, what they have in common, where they disagree, and which convinces them most and why.

Typical mistakes and how they are handled

  1. «If not even philosophers agree on what philosophy is, it's pure opinion.» This is the class's mistake (12:58). They share what's essential (critical, radical, rational and of the whole) and each gives reasons that can be discussed. And the question of what a field of knowledge is (what physics is, what philosophy is) isn't answered with that field's methods: it is philosophical. Arguing about it is part of philosophy's work; competence 5 of the decree says so: disagreement is «an intrinsic property of philosophical thought».
  2. «Socrates said: I only know that I know nothing.» As seen in class 1, the phrase isn't in Plato. What his Socrates says is «what I don't know, I don't think I know either» (Apology 21d), and elsewhere he says he does know some things.
  3. «Thomas knew Aristotle through the Arabs.» He read him in Latin, almost always translated directly from Greek; from Averroes, whom he called «the Commentator», he took the commentaries.
  4. «Marx said: enough thinking, it's time to act.» The key to thesis XI is «only»: he criticises stopping at interpreting, not thinking.
  5. «Philosophy is learning what the philosophers said.» With seven names on the board it's worth saying: the class ends by turning each answer into a question for the student. Kant put it this way: you cannot learn philosophy, only learn to philosophise (Critique of Pure Reason, A837–838/B865–866).

Questions and solutions

In the video

  • What were the four features of philosophy? (0:26) Critical (it doesn't accept something just because lots of people say it), radical (it goes to the root), rational (it gives reasons) and of the whole (it looks at everything together).
  • Prediction: if the whole of philosophy were a tree, what would be in the roots and what fruit would it bear? (7:10) Any reasoned answer is fine. Descartes: roots, metaphysics (first principles); trunk, physics; branches, medicine, mechanics and morals, and the fruit is picked at the ends of the branches, above all in morals.
  • Stop and think: why do Kant's first three questions lead to the fourth? (9:41) Because what I can know, what I ought to do and what I may hope depend on what kind of being I am.
  • The seven questions (15:02) have no solution: they are for thinking about. In class they can be used for a philosophical journal or to choose one and write a page.

From the student worksheet

  • What did Socrates compare himself to, and why? To a gadfly on a large, noble but slow horse that needs waking up (Apology 30e): his role was to wake the city up with questions.
  • What does Thomas distinguish at the start of the Summa Theologiae? Two kinds of knowledge: theology, which starts from what God reveals, and the philosophical disciplines, which are investigated by human reason.
  • What is in each part of Descartes' tree? Roots: metaphysics; trunk: physics; branches: medicine, mechanics and morals; the fruit, on the branches.
  • Which word is the key to Marx's sentence? «Only»: he doesn't say interpreting is useless, but that it isn't enough.
  • How do philosophy and poetry differ, for Zambrano? Philosophy searches, with a method; poetry finds, «a finding by grace». They are two halves.
  • What do Confucius and Socrates have in common? Both place wisdom in recognising what one doesn't know. They couldn't have met: according to the traditional dates, Confucius died about ten years before Socrates was born.

Practice (the solutions open class 3)

  1. Choose two at the table who clash: what would one say to the other? Any pair with an argument on each side. Example: Aristotle to Marx, «knowledge is worth it for its own sake, even if it changes nothing»; Marx to Aristotle, «if you only interpret the world, the world stays the same».
  2. Your eighth chair, in five lines. Look for a clear answer, a reason, an objection from another author at the table and a reply to that objection.
  3. Ask someone at home what they think philosophy is. Which of the seven is it like? Which feature is missing? Open answer: what counts is justifying the likeness with what each author said and naming the missing feature.

Keep thinking: seven answers in more than twenty centuries: what would the 21st century's be? It has no textbook answer; it links to class 7 of the topic (is philosophy any use in the age of AI?).

A classroom activity: structured controversy

About 16 minutes, in groups of four. Based on Johnson and Johnson's structured controversy: defend a position, switch to the opposite one and reach a synthesis.

  1. Groups of four, two pairs (1 minute). Choose a tension from the class: is philosophy for living better or for knowing? (Socrates and Aristotle), interpreting or changing? (Aristotle and Marx), is reason enough? (Thomas and Zambrano).
  2. Prepare (3 minutes). With their author's quotation, each pair writes its best argument, with an example of its own.
  3. Present (2 + 2 minutes). Each pair presents; before replying, the other pair repeats in its own words the reason it has heard.
  4. Switch sides (3 minutes). Each pair now defends the opposite position, trying to do it better.
  5. Synthesis (4 minutes). The four write a sentence they all sign: what each side gets right. If they can't, they write exactly where they disagree.
  6. Close (1 minute). Each student writes on their worksheet: «For me, philosophy is… because…».

For teachers: as in the video, the teacher moderates and doesn't give a verdict on what is controversial (article 9 of the channel's Constitution). In the tension with Marx, make sure nobody turns it into an argument about today's parties: the question is what philosophy is, in each author's words.

Other answers, to go further

Checked at their source, to widen the table in class:

  • Plato: thinking is a dialogue of the soul with itself (Theaetetus 189e–190a).
  • Epicurus: nobody is too young or too old to philosophise, because it is about the health of the soul (Letter to Menoeceus 122).
  • Hannah Arendt: the need of reason is inspired by the quest for meaning, not for truth (The Life of the Mind, 1978, vol. 1).
  • Wittgenstein: philosophy is not a doctrine but an activity that clarifies thoughts (Tractatus, 4.112).
  • Ortega y Gasset, Zambrano's teacher: ¿Qué es filosofía? (course of 1929, published in 1957).
